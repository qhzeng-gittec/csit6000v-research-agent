Congratulations on your pregnancy! Based on the article in `doc.txt` (a Mayo Clinic Staff piece titled "Pregnancy nutrition: Foods to avoid during pregnancy"), here is a detailed explanation of the items your doctor's office likely listed and the reasons each is not acceptable during pregnancy.

**1. Seafood high in mercury**
Some fish and shellfish contain levels of mercury that could be harmful. According to the article, too much mercury could damage your baby's growing nervous system. The bigger and older the fish, the more mercury it's likely to contain. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) says not to eat the following while pregnant: bigeye tuna, king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, swordfish, shark, and tilefish. The article notes that seafood can still be a great source of protein, and the omega-3 fatty acids in many fish can help your baby's brain and eye development, so low-mercury options like salmon, shrimp, sardines, and light canned tuna are safe in moderation (8 to 12 ounces per week).

**2. Raw, undercooked, or tainted seafood**
To avoid harmful bacteria or viruses in seafood, you should not eat raw fish and shellfish. Examples of raw or undercooked foods to avoid include sushi, sashimi, ceviche, and raw oysters, scallops, or clams. You should also avoid uncooked seafood that's refrigerated, such as seafood labeled nova style, lox, kippered, smoked, or jerky (though smoked seafood is OK if it's in a casserole or other cooked dish, and canned and shelf-stable versions are safe). You should also watch for local fish advisories if you eat fish from local waters, especially when water pollution is a concern. Seafood should be cooked well: fish to an inside temperature of 145 F (63 C), until it flakes with a fork and looks milky white throughout; shrimp, lobster, and scallops until milky white; and clams, mussels, and oysters until their shells open (throw away any that don't open).

**3. Undercooked meat, poultry, or eggs**
During pregnancy, you're at a higher risk of food poisoning from bacteria, called foodborne illness. How your body reacts to food poisoning when pregnant might be worse than if you weren't pregnant, and although it's rare, food poisoning may affect the baby too. To prevent this: fully cook all meats and poultry (use a meat thermometer); cook hot dogs and lunch meats until steaming hot or don't eat them at all, since they can be sources of a rare but serious foodborne illness known as a listeria infection; don't eat pates and meat spreads stored in a refrigerator (canned and shelf-stable versions are OK); cook eggs until the yolks and whites are firm, since raw eggs can have harmful bacteria; and don't eat foods that may be made with raw or partly cooked eggs, such as homemade eggnog, raw batter and dough, tiramisu, freshly made or homemade hollandaise sauce, homemade Caesar salad dressing, and homemade ice cream. Also avoid ready-made meat salads or seafood salads, including ham salad, tuna salad, and chicken salad.

**4. Unpasteurized foods**
Don't eat or drink anything that contains milk that hasn't gone through pasteurization, because products with unpasteurized milk could cause foodborne illness. Avoid soft cheeses such as brie, feta, and blue cheese unless the label says they're pasteurized or made with pasteurized milk. Also don't drink juice or cider that isn't pasteurized.

**5. Unwashed fruits and vegetables and raw sprouts**
To get rid of harmful bacteria, wash all raw fruits and vegetables well. Don't eat any raw sprouts, including alfalfa, clover, radish, and mung bean, because they might have harmful bacteria. Be sure to fully cook sprouts.

**6. Too much caffeine**
Caffeine can cross to the baby, but the effects on the baby aren't clear. To be safe, your health care professional might tell you not to have caffeine at all, or to limit it to less than 200 milligrams (mg) a day. For reference, an 8-ounce cup of brewed coffee has about 95 mg of caffeine, an 8-ounce cup of brewed tea about 47 mg, and a 12-ounce cola with caffeine about 33 mg.

**7. Herbal tea**
Not much is known about the effects of certain herbs on fetuses. As a result, don't drink herbal tea unless your health care professional says it's OK — and that includes the types of herbal tea specifically made for pregnancy.

**8. Alcohol**
No amount of alcohol has been proved safe during pregnancy, so to be safe, don't drink any alcohol at all. The article explains the risks: drinking alcohol during pregnancy leads to a higher risk of miscarriage and stillbirth, and may result in fetal alcohol syndrome, which can cause the face to form oddly and cause lower intelligence. If you're worried about alcohol you drank before you knew you were pregnant, or if you think you need help to stop drinking, the article advises talking to your health care professional.

In summary, the common thread behind all these restrictions is protecting you and your baby from harmful bacteria, viruses, mercury, and other substances that can cause foodborne illness or developmental harm, since your body's response to these threats can be more severe during pregnancy.
