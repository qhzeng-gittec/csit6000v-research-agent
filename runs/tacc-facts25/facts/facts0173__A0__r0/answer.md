Periorbital hyperchromia, also known as periorbital hyperpigmentation, peri-palpebral hyperpigmentation, dark eyelids, dark eye circles, dark circles, or simply under-eye circles, is described in the document as a mere color difference between the palpebral (eyelid) skin and the remaining facial skin. Despite being only a color difference, it makes people look tired or older, which negatively affects their quality of life.

**Prevalence and affected groups.** The condition has a higher prevalence in individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes, and it affects age groups and genders equally. Nevertheless, there are more complaints from women, especially senior women. Few studies exist on its etiology, but dark eye circles with a vascular component are known to present a dominant autosomal family inheritance pattern.

**Causes.** Periorbital hyperpigmentation seems to have multifactorial causes involving intrinsic factors (determined by the individual's genetics) and extrinsic factors such as sun exposure, smoking, alcoholism, and sleep deprivation. A distinctive feature of its etiopathogeny is the presence of melanic pigment and hemosiderotic pigment in the affected sites.

Melanic hyperpigmentation is more frequent in brunet adults, as a consequence of excessive and cumulative sun exposure, which increases melanin production, reduces skin thickness, and increases the dilatation of blood vessels. Intense vascularization is mainly found in people belonging to certain ethnic groups such as Arabs, Turks, Hindus, inhabitants of the Iberian Peninsula, and their respective descendants. In these ethnicities, the condition tends to appear earlier, often during childhood. In those individuals there is no change in skin color; the eyelid appears darkened because the dilated vessels are visible due to the transparency of the skin. The problem is often aggravated when the lower eyelid's vessels are more dilated (for example from fatigue, insomnia, oral breathing, or crying), causing dermal blood extravasation. The local liberation of ferric ions entails the formation of free radicals that stimulate melanocytes, generating melanic pigmentation.

Other causes noted as responsible for dark eye circles include post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation secondary to atopic and contact dermatitis, sleep deprivation, oral breathing, alcoholism, smoking, use of certain medications (contraceptives, chemotherapy, antipsychotics, and some types of eye drops), palpebral sagging due to aging, and disorders that develop with hydric retention and palpebral edema (thyroid disorders, nephropathies, cardiopathies, and pneumopathies) — all of which worsen the unattractive appearance of dark eye circles.

**Anatomical background.** The document explains that palpebral skin is the thinnest in the human body (less than 1 mm), with a very thin epidermis (0.4 mm) compared to the palmoplantar region (about 1.6 mm). The palpebral dermis is composed of loose conjunctive tissue and is extremely thin; it is absent in the pre-tarsal skin and in the medial and lateral ligaments of the eyelid. The thinness of the skin, combined with the lack of fatty tissue, gives the region its characteristic translucency, so that accumulation of melanin and/or vessel dilatation can easily be seen as bilateral homogeneous hyperpigmentation. The color of the palpebral skin results from a combination of genetic-racial factors (such as the amount of melanin pigment) and individual, regional, or even gender-related factors, such as the thickness of the various components and the blood volume in their vessels.

**Treatments.** Various treatments have been proposed for periorbital hyperchromia, although there are few studies on their long-term efficacy. The main types of treatment are: topical application of depigmenting products, chemical peelings, dermabrasion, cryosurgery, fillings with hyaluronic acid, intense pulsed light, and CO2, argon, ruby, and excimer lasers.

**In summary, the individuals most affected by periorbital hyperchromia are those with darker skin, hair, and eyes, and it is especially prevalent among people of certain ethnic groups (Arabs, Turks, Hindus, and inhabitants of the Iberian Peninsula and their descendants), in whom it often appears earlier, even during childhood.**
