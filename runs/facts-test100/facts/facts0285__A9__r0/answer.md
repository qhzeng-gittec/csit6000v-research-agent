**Potential benefits / reassuring findings**
- Taking prescribed dextroamphetamine-amphetamine as directed is not expected to make it harder to get pregnant.
- It is not expected to increase the chance of miscarriage.
- Every pregnancy starts with a 3–5% background risk of birth defects. Most studies suggest that taking dextroamphetamine or amphetamine during the first trimester does not increase the chance of birth defects, and a large study of people taking stimulants for ADHD during pregnancy reported no increased risk of birth defects.
- A Danish study suggested no increase in neurodevelopmental disorders, like ADHD, in children of people who continued their ADHD medication during pregnancy versus those who stopped before becoming pregnant (though this was limited by looking at all ADHD medications together).
- Your healthcare providers can talk with you about the benefits of treating your condition and the risks of untreated illness during pregnancy.

**Potential detriments / risks**
- Although data are limited, taking dextroamphetamine-amphetamine during pregnancy has sometimes been associated with a higher chance of pregnancy-related problems, such as poor growth (babies born small and/or with a small head size), low birth weight (less than 5 pounds, 8 ounces [2500 grams]), or preterm delivery (before week 37).
- You may experience side effects such as weight loss from decreased appetite, changes in heart rate, and changes in blood pressure. The document suggests talking with your healthcare provider about monitoring these side effects to help improve outcomes for you and your baby.
- It is not known if taking the medication throughout pregnancy could cause withdrawal symptoms in a newborn after birth; this has not been well studied.
- If you were to stop, sudden stopping can cause withdrawal in some people, and it is not known if or how withdrawal may affect a pregnancy. If you stop, providers may discuss slowly reducing your dose over time.

**Breastfeeding (if relevant after birth)**
- Amphetamine and dextroamphetamine pass into breast milk, and the effect on infant behavior and brain development has not been well studied. No adverse effects were reported in 4 infants (ages 3–10 months) whose mothers took dextroamphetamine for ADHD.
- Some evidence suggests large doses could lower milk supply in people newly breastfeeding.
- The product label recommends that people who are breastfeeding not use this medication, but the benefit may outweigh possible risks.

The document emphasizes that you should talk with your healthcare providers before making any changes to how you take your medication.
