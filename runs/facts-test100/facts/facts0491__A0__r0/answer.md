**What it is and when it came about**
On November 13, 2023, the Supreme Court announced that it was adopting a Code of Conduct for the first time. The Court said the Code's purpose is to "set out succinctly and gather in one place the ethics rules and principles that guide the conduct of the Members of the Court." The Court emphasized that most of these rules are not new — the Code is largely just a written-down (codified) version of principles the Justices have long followed.

**What's in it**
- The Code consists of **five ethical canons** (broad rules) plus **accompanying commentary** (explanations).
- The canons are nearly the same as those in the Code of Conduct for U.S. Judges, which applies only to lower federal judges.
- Many provisions deal with **conflicts of interest** and the **appearance of impropriety**.
- Some provisions specifically address financial activities. For example, Canon 4(H) states the Justices' commitment to comply with financial disclosure laws, and the commentary says the Justices comply with the EIGA (the financial disclosure law), the STOCK Act (which requires reporting stock trades over $1,000 within 45 days), and current Judicial Conference regulations on financial disclosure.
- The commentary also says the Justices comply with Judicial Conference regulations on gifts, foreign gifts and decorations, and outside earned income, honoraria, and employment — consistent with their long-standing voluntary compliance.

**What it does NOT do**
- It **does not impose any new financial disclosure requirements** on the Justices.
- It has **no enforcement mechanism** — there is no process for addressing alleged violations, so compliance may be left to the Justices' own discretion.

**Related context from the document**
- There's some uncertainty about whether the Judicial Conference (which manages the lower federal courts) even has authority over the Supreme Court — the Chief Justice said in 2011 that its committees have no mandate to set rules for the Supreme Court. Still, at least one Associate Justice said he would follow the 2023 guidance, and the Justices have stated they comply with current Judicial Conference regulations.
- The Justices, through the Code, acknowledged they file "the same financial disclosure reports as other federal judges."
- The document also notes that proposed legislation (like the Supreme Court Ethics, Recusal, and Transparency Act) could overlap with the Code, and that questions remain about whether Congress has the authority to regulate Supreme Court ethics.
