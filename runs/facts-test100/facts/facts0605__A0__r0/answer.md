**Paper:** "Revisiting False-Positive and Imitated Dissociative Identity Disorder" by Pietkiewicz, Bańbura-Nowak, Tomalski, and Boon (Frontiers in Psychology, 2021).

**Background and aim**
- ICD-10 and DSM-5 offer no clear guidelines for distinguishing "genuine" DID from imitated or false-positive cases, and clinicians lack adequate training in diagnosing dissociative disorders.
- Complex dissociative disorders are both underdiagnosed (patients avoid reporting symptoms; clinicians prefer more familiar diagnoses) and at risk of overdiagnosis, because media and internet information lead some people to self-diagnose and report DID symptoms "by the book."
- The study explores the meaning that patients with false-positive or imitated DID attributed to their diagnosis.

**Method**
- 85 people with elevated SDQ-20 dissociation scores underwent in-depth clinical assessment (semi-structured interview, the TADS-I interview, and a psychiatric interview) in Poland (2018–2019).
- Six women (aged 22–42) whose earlier DID diagnosis was disconfirmed were selected; their video-recorded interviews (3h15m–7h20m each) were transcribed and analyzed with interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA).
- None of the six met DID criteria: no evidence of autonomous dissociative parts, no amnesia for daily events, no PTSD intrusions, no auditory hallucinations, and no significant somatoform dissociation despite high SDQ-20 scores.

**Five main themes found**
1. **Endorsement and identification with the diagnosis** – all hoped to confirm DID; several had been steered toward it by clinicians or therapists, and all had researched DID in books and online.
2. **The notion of dissociative parts justifies identity confusion and conflicting ego-states** – participants used "parts" as a metaphor to explain emotional conflicts and to free themselves from guilt/shame, while maintaining a first-person narrative; the "inner characters" personified unintegrated ego-states rather than autonomous parts.
3. **Gaining knowledge about DID affects the clinical presentation** – reading and watching DID content led them to adopt clinical jargon and report symptoms in ways that changed their presentation.
4. **Fragmented personality becomes an important discussion topic with others** – the "multiple personalities" attracted attention from family/friends, fueled video blogs, diaries, and plans (e.g., writing a book), i.e., secondary gains.
5. **Ruling out DID leads to disappointment or anger** – four of six were openly disappointed or angry when the diagnosis was disconfirmed; only one (Victoria) felt relieved.

**Discussion / differential-diagnosis implications**
- Genuine DID patients are avoidant, ashamed, and rarely report symptoms spontaneously, whereas imitators eagerly present symptoms, sometimes announcing and performing "switches," and keep a first-person perspective.
- The paper lists 13 "red flags" for false-positive/imitated DID (Table 4), e.g., expecting confirmation of a self-diagnosis, DID suggested by others without assessment, use of clinical jargon, little avoidance/shame, no evidence of traumatic intrusions or amnesia for neutral/pleasant activities, announcing a switch during assessment, apparent gains from the diagnosis, and upset when DID is ruled out.
- Self-report scores (SDQ-20) should be interpreted cautiously; clinicians must ask for specific examples and attend to countertransference.

**Conclusions / recommendations**
- Clinicians need more systematic training in assessing dissociative disorders to understand the quality of symptoms and how dissociative vs. non-dissociative patients report them, to prevent both under- and over-diagnosis.
- Patients whose DID diagnosis is not confirmed should not be encouraged to learn more about DID symptomatology, as this can alter their presentation and lead to iatrogenic misdiagnosis; results should be communicated with empathy, and treatment should target their actual problems.
- Limitations: small idiographic IPA sample, and the TADS-I and the Polish psychoform-dissociation instrument used were not yet validated.
