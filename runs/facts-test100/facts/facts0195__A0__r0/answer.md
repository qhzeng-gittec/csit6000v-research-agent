## Ellana's Background in Medicine

- **Credentials and experience:** Ellana Stinson is a board-certified emergency medicine physician who has practiced emergency medicine for 15 years.
- **Steward Health System (2013–2020):** She worked on staff as an emergency medicine physician at Carney Hospital, Good Samaritan, and the former Quincy Hospital, all part of the Steward Health System. At Carney, she served a very diverse population in the Dorchester neighborhood of Boston, which she described as essentially how she had always imagined her career trajectory — serving the most vulnerable populations.
- **Nationwide work:** Over the past 11 years, she has worked intermittently throughout the country at various facilities run and operated by HCA, Envision, Tenant, Team Health, and CHS.
- **Early passion:** When she entered medicine, she deeply loved what she did — caring for patients in their most vulnerable moments, working in "beautifully orchestrated chaos," and bearing witness to life-changing moments, which filled her with pride at the end of each shift.

## Her Concerns About Changes in Emergency Medicine

**Dwindling resources and closures:** She felt she had to decide whether to stay or go when she could no longer care for patients the way she would want a loved one treated. She watched resources being pulled from each Steward facility, specialty services disappearing, and Quincy Hospital reduced to "bare bones" before its closure. The absence of blood products, respiratory therapy, and certain specialty services made her feel she could no longer provide safe or quality care.

**Private equity's impact:** She notes that private equity firms have led buyouts, mergers, and acquisitions of over 30% of U.S. hospitals, and that the deprivation at PE sites was sometimes worse. Specific problems she cites include:
- Increased wait times, critical staff shortages, dangerous boarding levels, and patient-to-nurse ratios in the ED reaching as high as 14 to 1.
- The pandemic exacerbating these problems, with increased cost of care, infection rates, mortality, and even death.
- Medicine at PE-led places no longer being about patient safety and quality, but about corporate decision-making with profit motives at the expense of patients.
- Staff being forced to see patients in the waiting room to make wait times appear reduced and to improve door-to-doc times.
- Calling codes for sepsis and strokes as "innovative ways to make profits."
- Increasingly daunting, nearly unattainable and unsafe metrics required of physicians and staff.
- Emergency physicians being removed from in-network status, causing higher "surprise billing" for patients who have no choice of where to go — sometimes with the next closest facility an hour away.

**Harm to vulnerable communities:** Most of the facilities she worked in served vulnerable populations — mostly people of color, low-income, or with limited access to other facilities or primary care — forcing them to seek care only at PE-backed sites. She notes that most buyouts target hospitals already struggling or with higher Medicaid and Medicare populations, and that the financialization of medicine will continue to exacerbate health disparities despite efforts to close the gaps.

**Impact on physicians:** These practices felt unsafe to her and her colleagues (often emergency physicians of color), raising questions of medical-legal risk and the emotional toll on those who entered the profession to do no harm. Being on staff at a PE-backed facility also did not qualify physicians with student debt for loan forgiveness. She observes that a profession once competitive is now among the least competitive to enter, with 554 residency spots unfilled in 2023, and that PE-backed hospitals have opened residency programs not adequately equipped to properly train emergency medicine trainees.

She concludes that while they train to deal with hard stuff, they are "running out of options" on how to continue providing safe and adequate care at PE-run facilities.
