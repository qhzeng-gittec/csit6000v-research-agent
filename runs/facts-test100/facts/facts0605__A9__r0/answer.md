Based on `doc.txt`, the key points of the paper ("Revisiting False-Positive and Imitated Dissociative Identity Disorder," Pietkiewicz et al., 2021) are:

**Problem and aim**
- ICD-10 and DSM-5 do not provide clear guidelines for diagnosing DID, making it difficult to distinguish "genuine" DID from imitated or false-positive cases.
- The study explores the meaning that patients with false-positive or imitated DID attributed to their diagnosis.

**Method**
- 85 people with elevated SDQ-20 dissociative symptom scores underwent clinical assessment (Trauma and Dissociation Symptoms Interview, TADS-I, plus a psychiatric interview).
- Video recordings of six women (aged 22–42) whose earlier DID diagnosis was disconfirmed were transcribed and analyzed with interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA).
- None of the six met DID criteria; none showed evidence of autonomous dissociative parts, amnesia for daily events, PTSD symptoms, or auditory verbal hallucinations. All had sought information about DID in literature and on the Internet, and several had been told by clinicians they might have DID.

**Five main themes identified**
1. Endorsement and identification with the diagnosis.
2. The notion of dissociative parts justifies identity confusion and conflicting ego-states.
3. Gaining knowledge about DID affects the clinical presentation (e.g., participants began reporting "intrusions of parts" and using clinical terms like "flashback" for experiences that were not clinical symptoms).
4. Fragmented personality becomes an important discussion topic with others (family, friends, therapists; some made videoblogs or journals about their "parts").
5. Ruling out DID leads to disappointment or anger (four participants were openly disappointed; only Victoria was relieved).

**Discussion / differential diagnosis**
- Genuine DID is described as a "disorder of hiddenness": patients are reluctant, ashamed, and avoidant about reporting dissociative symptoms. In contrast, people with imitated DID report symptoms "by the book," eagerly and without shame, sometimes exaggeratedly.
- The paper provides guidelines for differential diagnosis, including a table of 13 "red flags" for false-positive or imitated DID (e.g., expecting confirmation of a self-diagnosis; DID previously suggested by others; keen familiarity with DID symptoms; use of clinical jargon; little avoidance; justifying loss of control by reference to an alternative personality; no evidence of traumatic intrusions; no amnesia for neutral or pleasant everyday activities; announcing and performing a "switch" during assessment; apparent gains from the diagnosis; upset when DID is not confirmed).
- It notes that high self-report scores (e.g., SDQ-20) can be exaggerated and should be interpreted with caution; clinicians should ask for specific examples and attend to their own countertransference.

**Recommendations and limitations**
- Clinicians should receive more systematic training in assessing dissociative disorders to understand subtle differences in the quality of symptoms and how dissociative vs. non-dissociative patients report them.
- Patients whose diagnosis is not confirmed should not be encouraged to learn about DID symptomatology, as this can affect their presentation and lead to iatrogenic misdiagnosis; results should be communicated with empathy.
- Limitations: small idiographic sample; the Polish psychoform instrument and the TADS-I are not yet validated; there are no gold standards for diagnosing dissociative disorders in Poland.
