**Amazon's acquisition of Whole Foods Market (2017, ~$13.2 billion)**
- **Integration of offline and online retail:** Amazon gained brick-and-mortar grocery store locations it could integrate with its online services — e.g., Prime members became eligible for discounts and free pickup/delivery of Whole Foods groceries in selected zip codes, and Amazon Hub Lockers are often located in Whole Foods Markets.
- **Increased competitive pressure in grocery retail:** The acquisition may have increased competition in the grocery retail market. Other retailers responded: Walmart launched an online delivery service in 2017, Kroger did so in 2018, and Walmart launched the Walmart+ membership delivery service in 2020. Consumers may have benefited, particularly from free delivery on orders over $35.
- **Pressure on third-party services:** The changes may have increased pressure on services like Instacart, which stopped delivering groceries from Whole Foods in 2019.
- **Growing dominance in e-commerce:** The acquisition raised concern about Amazon's dominance — eMarketer estimated Amazon had a 38.7% share of e-commerce sales in 2020 (vs. Walmart's 5.3%), and a House Subcommittee staff report suggested a 50%+ share when restricted to products sold on Amazon, with over 60% of U.S. online product searches beginning on Amazon.
- **Stronger bargaining power and vertical integration:** Through the acquisition, Amazon gained access to additional consumer data, strengthening its bargaining power with suppliers. Combined with vertical integration (its AmazonBasics private label and its own delivery system, with ~$60 billion invested since 2014 and the fourth-largest U.S. package-delivery share in 2019), Amazon may further strengthen its e-commerce position; consumers could benefit from faster delivery even if it becomes harder for other companies to compete.

**Facebook's acquisition of Instagram (2012, $1 billion)**
- **Antitrust scrutiny:** Although the FTC closed its investigation without action in 2012, in December 2020 the FTC sued Facebook, alleging it maintained its monopoly by buying up companies that present competitive threats (specifically naming Instagram and WhatsApp). A coalition of 46 state attorneys general filed a parallel lawsuit on similar grounds. An internal email from Mark Zuckerberg describing Instagram as "a large and viable competitor" has been used to support the claim that Facebook acquired it to eliminate a potential competitor.
- **Rapid growth and profitability of Instagram:** Instagram grew from about 100 million monthly active users in February 2013 to 500 million in June 2016 and 1 billion in June 2018. It used Facebook's resources — advertising services and data infrastructure — which were key to its profitability, including among paid "influencer" users.
- **Possible counterfactual:** It is unclear how successful Instagram would have been without the merger; the document notes it might have struggled due to resource constraints, as happened with Friendster, which rejected a Google buyout and later closed down.
- **Market-definition complications:** Evaluating the merger's effect is complicated by how to define the market in fast-evolving digital markets; at the time, Instagram was described as a photo-sharing app competing with apps like Photobucket and Flickr rather than with Facebook, and social networking services are not necessarily substitutes for one another.
