**Problem and aim**
- "ICD-10 and DSM-5 do not provide clear diagnosing guidelines for DID, making it difficult to distinguish 'genuine' DID from imitated or false-positive cases." The study "explores meaning which patients with false-positive or imitated DID attributed to their diagnosis."
- A false-positive diagnosis "is unfavorable for the patient, because using treatment developed for DID with patients without autonomous dissociative parts may be inefficient or even reinforce their pathology."

**Method**
- "85 people who reported elevated levels of dissociative symptoms in SDQ20 participated in clinical assessment using the Trauma and Dissociation Symptoms Interview, followed by a psychiatric interview."
- "The recordings of six women, whose earlier DID diagnosis was disconfirmed, were transcribed and subjected to interpretative phenomenological analysis." The six participants (aged 22–42) "did not meet DID criteria on the TADS-I interview but believed themselves that they qualified for that diagnosis," and "None of them reported symptoms indicating the existence of autonomous dissociative parts."

**Main findings — five themes**
1. "Endorsement and identification with the diagnosis" — all six "hoped to confirm they had DID" and had read books, browsed the Internet, and watched YouTube videos about dissociation.
2. "The notion of dissociative parts justifies identity confusion and conflicting ego-states."
3. "Gaining knowledge about DID affects the clinical presentation."
4. "Fragmented personality becomes an important discussion topic with others."
5. "Ruling out DID leads to disappointment or anger" — e.g., participants "doubted if their descriptions were accurate enough, or they challenged the interviewer's understanding of the symptoms."

**Differential-diagnosis observations**
- Genuine DID is described as a "'disorder of hiddenness' because patients often find their dissociative symptoms bizarre and confusing and do not disclose them readily due to their shame," whereas people with personality disorders "enthusiastically report DID symptoms by the book."
- The paper discusses how to distinguish cases by "intrusions, voices, switches, amnesia, use of language, depersonalisation." For example, two participants "tried to present 'alternate personalities' and they actually announced this would happen," and "None of them reported auditory hallucinations."
- High self-report scores can mislead: three participants had very high SDQ-20 scores, but "further interviews revealed that they aggravated their symptoms and, in fact, had low levels of somatoform dissociation."
- Table 4 lists "Red flags for identifying false-positive or imitated DID," including that the patient "Directly or indirectly expects to confirm self-diagnosed DID," "Uses clinical jargon: parts, alters, dissociating, switch, depersonalisation, etc.," "Reveals little avoidance: eagerly talks about painful experiences and dissociation," and "Gets upset or disappointed when DID is not confirmed."

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- "To avoid misdiagnoses, clinicians should receive more systematic training in the assessment of dissociative disorders, enabling them to better understand subtle differences in the quality of symptoms and how dissociative and non-dissociative patients report them."
- "patients whose diagnosis has not been confirmed by a thorough diagnostic assessment should not be encouraged to develop knowledge about DID symptomatology, because this may affect their clinical presentation," which "may lead to a wrong diagnosis and treatment, which can become iatrogenic."
- "communicating the results should be done with understanding, empathy and care."

**Limitations**
- The study is idiographic and "by nature limited to a small number of participants"; "information about the level of psychoform symptoms has not been given, because the validation of the Polish instrument used for that purpose is not complete," and "TADS-I ... has not been validated, either." The authors recommend "further qualitative and quantitative research to formulate and validate more specific diagnostic criteria for DID and guidelines for the differential diagnosis."
