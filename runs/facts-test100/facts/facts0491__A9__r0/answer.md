**What it is and when it came out**
- On November 13, 2023, the Supreme Court announced that it was, for the first time, adopting a Code of Conduct. Its purpose is to "set out succinctly and gather in one place the ethics rules and principles that guide the conduct of the Members of the Court."
- The Court said most of the rules in it "are not new" — the Code is largely just writing down principles the Justices have long followed.

**What's in it**
- It is made up of five ethical canons (basic rules) plus commentary explaining them.
- The canons are nearly the same as the canons in the Code of Conduct for U.S. Judges, which applies only to lower federal judges.
- Many provisions deal with conflicts of interest and the appearance of impropriety.
- Some provisions specifically cover financial activities and compliance with the Ethics in Government Act (EIGA). For example, Canon 4(H) states the Justices' commitment to comply with financial disclosure laws, and the commentary says the Justices comply with the EIGA, the STOCK Act, and current Judicial Conference financial disclosure regulations.
- The commentary also says the Justices comply with Judicial Conference regulations on gifts, foreign gifts and decorations, and outside earned income, honoraria, and employment — which the document notes is consistent with the Justices' long-standing voluntary compliance.

**What it does not do**
- Although it affirms compliance with existing financial disclosure laws, it does not appear to impose any new financial disclosure requirements on the Justices.
- It has no enforcement mechanisms — there is no process for dealing with alleged violations of the Code. The document notes that, without enforcement, compliance with federal ethics laws may be left to the Justices' own discretion.

**Related context in the document**
- The Justices, through the Code, acknowledged that they file "the same financial disclosure reports as other federal judges."
- There is some uncertainty about whether Judicial Conference regulations apply to the Supreme Court at all, since the Judicial Conference manages the lower federal courts.
- Proposed legislation (such as the Supreme Court Ethics, Recusal, and Transparency Act) and congressional attempts to enforce the Code raise open questions about whether Congress has the authority to regulate Supreme Court ethics — an issue the document describes as untested.
