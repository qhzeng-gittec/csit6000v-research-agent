**Power source**
- **HEVs** are powered by an internal combustion engine (or other propulsion source) running on conventional or alternative fuel, plus an electric motor that uses energy stored in a battery.
- **PHEVs** are powered by conventional fuels and by electrical energy stored in a battery; they also have an internal combustion engine and an electric motor.
- **EVs** use only a battery to store the electrical energy that powers the motor; they use no other fuel.

**How the battery is charged**
- **HEVs** do not require a plug; they charge using regenerative braking (the electric motor acting as a generator, capturing energy normally lost during braking) and the internal combustion engine.
- **PHEVs** can be charged several ways: by an outside electric power source (plugging in, using grid electricity), by the internal combustion engine, or through regenerative braking.
- **EVs** are charged only by plugging the vehicle into an electric power source.

**Battery size and all-electric capability**
- **HEVs** have a smaller engine (enabled by the electric motor's extra power), giving better fuel economy without sacrificing performance; the battery provides extra power during acceleration and auxiliary power when idling.
- **PHEVs** have larger battery packs than HEVs, making it possible to drive using only electric power — an all-electric range of about 10 to 40 miles in current models. If a PHEV is never plugged in, its fuel economy is about the same as a similarly sized HEV.
- **EVs** run entirely on the battery, but currently available EVs have a shorter range per charge than most conventional vehicles have per tank of gas; manufacturers typically target a minimum range of 100 miles, which the Federal Highway Administration says is sufficient for more than 90% of all U.S. household vehicle trips.

**Emissions**
- **HEVs** combine high fuel economy and low emissions with the power and range of conventional vehicles.
- **PHEVs** can reduce emissions, depending on the electricity source, and reduce petroleum consumption when charged from the grid.
- **EVs** are considered zero-emission vehicles by the U.S. EPA because their motors produce no exhaust or emissions, even though electricity production may contribute to air pollution.
