**VA (Department of Veterans Affairs):** VA received Mr. Taylor's claim for service-connected disability benefits in 2007. A VA medical examiner diagnosed Mr. Taylor with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) resulting from his time at Edgewood, which was reaggravated during his time serving in Vietnam. VA ultimately granted Mr. Taylor a total disability rating with an effective date of February 28, 2007, the day VA received his claim, relying on 38 U.S.C. § 5110 (implemented under 38 C.F.R. § 3.400) to establish this effective date. After the BVA remanded the case in February 2014, VA procured a secrecy oath that most participants in the Edgewood program signed, and VA and the BVA again concluded that Mr. Taylor was entitled to VA benefits starting February 28, 2007. The document further describes the VA adjudicatory system as "the exclusive forum for securing his legal entitlement to the benefits at issue," and notes that the final effective date determination (which could be September 7, 1971) is pending VA assessment of this issue on remand.

**BVA (Board of Veterans' Appeals):** The BVA received Mr. Taylor's appeal of the VA determination, in which he sought an effective date of September 7, 1971, the day he was discharged from service. In July 2010, the BVA held that 38 U.S.C. § 5110 prevented the effective date from being earlier than the day he filed his claim, stating that "there was nothing stopping [Mr. Taylor] from filing the claim . . . earlier and [VA] asking for a delay in the adjudication of the case following the obtaining of important evidence." In February 2014, the BVA remanded the case to VA to ascertain the secrecy oath's language and reassess the case. In April 2017, the BVA made its determination and provided three reasons: (1) Mr. Taylor could have filed a disability claim earlier than 2007 premised on his experiences serving in Vietnam, which were partially responsible for his PTSD; (2) Mr. Taylor could have divulged his participation in the Edgewood program because he had done so on a previous occasion in pursuit of care, though the health care provider at that time did not believe him; and (3) Section 5110 delineates when VA benefits can begin and does not provide for equitable tolling.

**CAVC (U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims):** The CAVC received Mr. Taylor's appeal of the BVA's decision. In June 2013, in a single-judge decision, the CAVC held that the BVA "failed to provide an adequate statement of reasons or bases for its . . . decision," specifically sought the language of the original secrecy oath, and vacated the BVA's decision and remanded the case. In April 2019, in a 2-1 decision, a panel of CAVC judges affirmed the BVA's determination that Mr. Taylor was only entitled to VA disability benefits beginning February 28, 2007. The CAVC detailed three components of its analysis: (1) Mr. Taylor was not entitled to relief under the Constitution's Fifth Amendment Due Process Clause because he did not have a property interest in his VA benefits prior to February 28, 2007, relying on Federal Circuit precedent that individuals only gain a constitutionally cognizable property interest in VA benefits upon filing claims; (2) the court determined that it lacked authority to provide Mr. Taylor with equitable relief by ordering VA to establish a process through which he and similarly situated veterans could establish effective dates preceding their claim-filing dates; and (3) the CAVC held that 38 U.S.C. § 5110 required the effective date to be February 28, 2007, the date he filed his claim.

**Federal Circuit (U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit):** The Federal Circuit received Mr. Taylor's appeal of the CAVC's decision. A three-judge panel issued its opinion in June 2021, ruling in favor of Mr. Taylor, holding that: (1) the Veterans Court may exercise equitable powers within the scope of its statutory jurisdiction to ensure that all veterans entitled to benefits receive them; (2) the effective date requirement under 38 U.S.C. § 5110(a)(1) is not jurisdictional and can be subject to equitable considerations and relief; (3) where the Government has affirmatively and intentionally prevented veterans from seeking medical care and VA benefits under threat of criminal prosecution, the CAVC is within its authority to hold the Government equitably estopped from asserting that those veterans are not entitled to an earlier effective date; and (4) the government is equitably estopped from asserting 38 U.S.C. § 5110(a)(1) against Mr. Taylor because the government's actions caused him to file his benefits claim when he did. The panel reversed the CAVC's decision and remanded the case. In July 2021, the Federal Circuit voted to hear the case en banc, and the full court heard oral argument on February 10, 2022. The Federal Circuit stayed all proceedings in Taylor until the Supreme Court decided Arellano, and after the Arellano decision, requested supplemental briefing by the parties. In June 2023, the full Federal Circuit decided in favor of Mr. Taylor. After finding equitable estoppel unavailable under Section 5110(a)(1) in light of Supreme Court precedent, the court held that the government violated Mr. Taylor's constitutional right to due process under the Fifth Amendment, concluding that Mr. Taylor had "a constitutional right of access to adjudicatory forums" to vindicate his legal entitlements, and that the government unconstitutionally denied him "access to the adjudication system of VA, the exclusive forum for securing his legal entitlement to the benefits at issue," through the secrecy oath and threat of criminal prosecution. The court ruled that the relevant statutory limits on effective dates were "unconstitutional as applied" in Mr. Taylor's case, determined that he was entitled to "the effective date he would have had if no unconstitutional denial of access had occurred," which could be September 7, 1971, pending VA assessment on remand, and reversed the CAVC's decision and remanded the case. The government has not sought Supreme Court review as of this writing, though it still has time to do so—petitions must be filed within 90 days of judgment.

**U.S. Supreme Court:** The Supreme Court granted review of Arellano v. McDonough, a case concerning whether 38 U.S.C. § 5110(b)(1)—an exception to subsection (a)(1)'s requirements mandating that the effective date of an award of disability compensation be the day following the date of the veteran's discharge or release if application is received within one year from such date—is subject to equitable tolling. In January 2023, the Supreme Court held, in relevant part, that subsection (b)(1) is not subject to equitable tolling because "the structure of §5110 reinforces Congress's choice to set effective dates solely as prescribed in the text," and because subsection (a)(1) provides "an instruction to attend to specifically enacted language to the exclusion of general, unenacted carveouts."
