**Positive correlations found in studies:**
- In one study, social and environmental exposure administered to puppies was **positively correlated with measures of sociability** and **negatively correlated with measures of fear and aggression**. Specifically, dogs that attended puppy preschool were found to be **less fearful, less aggressive, and more social**.
- Another study showed that **attendance at puppy classes reduced the risk of aggression toward unfamiliar people**. (Other factors that also predicted less aggressive behavior included being a desexed female, having a female owner, attending ring-craft classes, and using positive reinforcement training methods.)
- A further study showed a similar result: **attendance at puppy socialization classes was associated with improved positive social behaviors and fewer behavioral problems as adults**. (Other factors predicting these outcomes were being a male dog, a higher level of environmental and social interactions overall, and remaining in the litter longer.)

**Important caveats noted in the document:**
- The evidence is **less clear on the benefit of puppy classes specifically**, even though puppy socialization practices in general are considered crucial for normal social relationships and psychological health in adult dogs.
- Most studies on puppy classes are **epidemiological in nature** — they correlate adult dog behavior with demographic, owner, and environmental factors. While useful for identifying which factors have stronger predictive value, there is debate about whether they can truly determine **causal relationships**, which limits their utility in evaluating whether a single factor like puppy class attendance has a clear effect.
- The studies **sometimes show contradictory results** (e.g., male dogs showed fewer behavioral problems in one study, while desexed females showed less aggression in another), suggesting that samples or unaccounted-for variables may have affected the results.
- Puppy socialization classes do not always appear to singularly affect adult behavior, possibly because **classes are not always well run** — in most countries there are no accreditation procedures, and classes may be run by people lacking the necessary skills or experience. If puppies find the experience **too stressful**, they may not receive a noticeable benefit, since while mild stress is a component of socialization, too much stress prevents the benefits from being conferred.

In summary, the document reports that puppy socialization class attendance is generally associated with better adult outcomes (less fear, less aggression, more sociability, fewer behavioral problems), but the evidence is correlational rather than definitively causal, and results are inconsistent, partly due to study design limitations and variable class quality.
