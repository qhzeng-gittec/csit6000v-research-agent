**What the article says about selling puts and assignment**

The article explains that "a put option6 gives the owner the right to sell the underlying security." (doc.txt) On the seller's side, it states: "when you sell an option, you may be assigned—at any time regardless of the ITM amount—if the option owner chooses to exercise. The option seller has no control over assignment and no certainty as to when it could happen" (doc.txt). It adds that "Once the assignment notice is delivered, it's too late to close the position and the option seller must fulfill the terms of the options contract" (doc.txt).

Specifically for your position, the article states: "A short put assignment results in buying the underlying stock at the strike price" (doc.txt). Its table confirms that if the underlying stock price is lower than the strike price, "A short put is... ITM and typically assigned," while if the stock is higher than the strike, it is "OTM and typically abandoned" (doc.txt). So the article's own framework says a short put that ends in the money is typically assigned, and the seller has no control over that outcome.

**What happens if you are assigned**

The article says that if an option is exercised or assigned and you are "uncovered" (you don't have an existing long or short position in the underlying security), "a long or short position in the underlying stock will replace the options. A long call or short put will result in a long position in XYZ" (doc.txt). In other words, an assigned short put turns into a long stock position. The article warns: "For long stock positions, you need to have enough cash to cover the purchase or else you'll be issued a margin call, which you must meet by adding funds to your account," and the broker "at its discretion, has the right to liquidate positions in your account to meet a margin call" (doc.txt).

It also notes that "If an option is ITM by as little as $0.01 at expiration, it will automatically be exercised for the buyer and assigned to a seller," though a buyer can submit a "do not exercise (DNE) request," in which case "it's possible that a short ITM position might not be assigned" (doc.txt). The article further describes "pin risk," where a stock settles exactly on a strike price and "you may or may not be assigned—and you may not be able to trade out of any unwanted positions until the next business day," concluding that "The best way to avoid it is to close the position before expiration" (doc.txt).

**What the article does not address**

The article does not mention credit spreads, put spreads, or any strategy combining a short put with a long put. It does not use the term "defined risk" or describe any structure that caps losses. It does not state whether holding a long put against a short put would prevent assignment, limit losses, or change the assignment mechanics described above. Therefore, based solely on this document, I cannot confirm that a put credit spread "would truly have defined risk and prevented you from being assigned shares." The document's statements about short puts—assignment at the strike price, no seller control over assignment, and the resulting long stock position—apply to the short put leg as described, and the document is silent on how a spread would alter any of that.
