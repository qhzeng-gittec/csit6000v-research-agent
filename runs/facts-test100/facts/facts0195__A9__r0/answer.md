

**Ellana's background in medicine (per doc.txt):**

- Her name is Ellana Stinson; she is a board-certified emergency medicine physician who has practiced emergency medicine for 15 years.
- From 2013 to 2020, she worked on staff as an emergency medicine physician at Carney Hospital, Good Samaritan, and the former Quincy Hospital, all part of the Steward Health System.
- Over the past 11 years, she has also worked intermittently throughout the country at various facilities run and operated by HCA, Envision, Tenant, Team Health, and CHS.
- When she entered medicine, she "deeply loved what I did," finding pride in caring for patients in their most vulnerable moments and in "beautifully orchestrated chaos."

**Her concerns about the changes she is witnessing in Emergency Medicine:**

- **Dwindling resources at Steward facilities:** She realized resources were being pulled from each facility; most no longer had certain specialty services, and Quincy Hospital was "taken down to bare bones" before its closure. The absence of blood products, respiratory therapy at times, and certain specialty services made her feel she could no longer provide safe or quality care.
- **Private equity's reach:** She notes buyouts, mergers, and acquisitions led by private equity firms of over 30% of hospitals in the US, and that at other PE sites the level of deprivation was "seemingly worse in some areas."
- **Staffing and safety problems:** Increased wait times, critical shortage of staff, dangerous boarding levels, and critically dangerous patient-to-nurse ratios in the emergency department, "upwards to 14 to 1 ratios at times."
- **Pandemic effects:** The pandemic exacerbated the situation, resulting in increased cost of care, infection rates, mortality, and even death.
- **Profit over patient safety:** At PE-led places, medicine is "no longer about patient safety and quality, but about making medical decisions and judgment due corporate decision making with profit motives at the expense of patients." She cites forcing staff to see patients in the waiting room to make wait times appear reduced and improve door-to-doc times, and calling codes for sepsis and strokes "in order to find innovative ways to make profits."
- **Unsafe metrics:** Increasingly daunting metrics required of physicians and other staff were "nearly unattainable and unsafe in many instances, but very much expected."
- **Surprise billing:** Many sites removed emergency physicians from in-network, resulting in higher bills for patients; these practices harm patients and increase financial burdens, especially when the next closest facility can be upwards to an hour away.
- **Impact on vulnerable communities:** Most facilities she worked in served vulnerable populations—mostly people of color, low income, or with limited access to other facilities or primary care—forcing them to seek care only at PE-backed sites. Most buyouts target hospitals already struggling or with higher Medicaid and Medicare populations, and she warns the financialization of medicine will continue to exacerbate health disparities.
- **Toll on physicians:** These practices often felt unsafe, sparking questions of medical legal risk and the emotional toll on those who entered the profession to do no harm. Being on staff at a PE-backed facility also would not qualify physicians with student debt for loan forgiveness.
- **Decline of the profession:** A profession once competitive is now "one of the least competitive fields to enter"; in 2023, 554 residency spots went unfilled, and more PE-backed hospitals opened residency programs not adequately equipped to provide proper training for an emergency medicine trainee.
- She concludes that while they "train to deal with hard stuff," they are "running out of options on how to continue providing safe and adequate care at these PE run facilities."
