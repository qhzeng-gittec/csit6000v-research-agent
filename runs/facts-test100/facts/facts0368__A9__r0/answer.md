**Consoles vs. PCs in raw graphical performance**
- The document doesn't give raw hardware specs (TFLOPS, etc.); it compares the two in practical terms. The PS5 and Xbox Series X can technically output an 8K signal and 120 FPS, but the author calls that "marketing bullshit": the most graphically demanding games are "lucky to upscale to 4K at a framerate of 60 FPS," and many titles include a 30 FPS "graphics" or "visuals" mode.
- On PC, 60 FPS is considered the bare minimum for an optimal experience, with 120 FPS or greater preferable. Native resolution beyond 4K is possible (e.g., 7,680 x 2,160 on the Samsung Neo G9), which the doc says is only achievable on a PC.
- Console versions of games tend to run a lower quality preset than PC versions—generally similar to the "Medium" or "High" preset in the PC release.
- **Ray-tracing:** the PS5 and Xbox Series X|S can handle it, but game support is "underwhelming"—even big next-gen exclusives like Starfield lack it. Cross-platform titles that do support it, like Cyberpunk 2077, "usually stick to a level of quality that's a notch or two below the maximum available on PC."
- Caveats for PC: Nvidia's low video memory on cards under 16GB (the amount the PS5 and Xbox Series X support) is catching up, some PC ports have optimization problems, and the best PC experience "can prove extremely expensive."
- Bottom line per the document: "PC gaming beats console gaming on both performance (as measured by framerate) and visual quality," and that gap "will only increase in the coming years as the current console generation ages relative to new PC graphics cards."

**Game availability**
- Consoles: most games are now cross-platform, and the PS5 and Xbox Series X|S are backwards-compatible with many previous-gen titles, boosting each console's library "into the thousands."
- PC: the library is "too many to count," with an estimate of over 100,000 titles—over 12,000 games were released to Steam in 2022 alone. A PC can also emulate the libraries of most older titles and, in some cases, even modern games (the doc cites The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom, noting that emulating without buying is piracy).
- The document's verdict: "A PC is the way to go if you want access to the largest game library on the face of the planet." Winner: PC.

**Price of a solid PC that keeps up with consoles**
- A good mid-range, off-the-shelf desktop or gaming laptop with performance comparable to an Xbox Series X or PlayStation 5: **$1,500 to $2,000**, depending on whether you accept a basic desktop from Dell or HP or want a boutique machine from Origin PC or Digital Storm.
- DIY: you can build a "console-slaying" desktop for **under $1,500** with an AMD Ryzen 5600X and a budget GPU like a Radeon RX 7800 XT.
- For context, the doc warns that a PC bought for the price of an Xbox Series X or PS5 "will end up with an outdated graphics card that can't handle games designed for modern game consoles," and PCs at entry-level console prices usually lack a graphics card entirely. It also notes that if you need a solid PC for other demanding tasks, budgeting around $1,000 for that base makes the PC-vs-console price difference less extreme.

One note: the document doesn't quantify "raw" performance in hardware terms (it measures the comparison via framerate, resolution, and visual quality), so if you were looking for spec-level comparisons, that's not covered in this file.
