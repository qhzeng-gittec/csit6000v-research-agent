According to `doc.txt`, lupus complicates pregnancy and the baby's health for several reasons:

**The disease itself**
- Lupus is a chronic condition in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues (skin, joints, muscles, nerves, kidneys, heart, or lungs), causing ongoing inflammation and pain.
- Flares of lupus symptoms may occur during pregnancy or immediately after delivery (though this is less likely if the condition was stable before conception).
- It can be difficult to distinguish between a lupus flare and normal pregnancy symptoms (fatigue, fluid build-up in joints, skin changes, hair loss, shortness of breath, joint pain), so close monitoring is needed.

**Complications that can occur during pregnancy**
- High blood pressure (hypertension)
- Pre-eclampsia (high blood pressure with excessive protein in the urine), which occurs in one in five women with lupus and, if left untreated, can endanger the life of both the woman and her baby
- The baby being born with low birth weight
- Premature labour
- Increased risk of blood clots in the legs or lungs
- Increased risk of miscarriage
- Increased risk of emergency caesarean section
- Increased risk of excessive bleeding after delivery

**Antibodies that affect the placenta and the baby**
- About one third of women with lupus have antibodies that may cause blood clots and interfere with the proper functioning of the placenta, most likely in the second trimester. The placenta then cannot supply the baby with sufficient nourishment, the baby's growth is slowed, and this may require early delivery via caesarean section.
- Around one third of women with lupus have antibodies that may cause lupus-like symptoms in the baby once it is born (neonatal lupus), which may include skin rash, unusual blood count and, rarely, heartbeat irregularities.

**Medication issues**
- Some lupus medications, like methotrexate, shouldn't be taken during pregnancy, and some medication taken for lupus can cross the placenta and pose a threat to the baby. Some medications may need to be stopped months before trying to get pregnant, so medication changes must be planned with doctors before conception.

The document notes that most women with lupus are still able to have a healthy baby, and that planning the pregnancy carefully—being in good health before conception, waiting at least six months after a flare, and working closely with a rheumatologist and specialist obstetrician—helps reduce these risks.
