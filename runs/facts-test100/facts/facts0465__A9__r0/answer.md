

- The report gives an overview of Big Tech's current financial offerings, how the existing regulatory framework applies to them, and related policy implications; it notes that concerns and regulatory treatment could change if Big Tech expands its offerings or as policymakers continue to assess data practices and antitrust issues.
- The section considers possible future paths for Big Tech financial services without assuming any particular strategy is more likely or that the companies are pursuing one.
- **Investment advisory services** (advice on securities, including asset, portfolio, and wealth managers): a November 2021 survey found more than 90% of wealth managers believe Big Tech companies will enter the market, with over 50% expecting significant disruption; fintechs have already entered investment management using machine learning for robo-advising and retail stock trading.
- If Big Tech pursued this approach, it would have to register as investment advisors with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and may also need to register with the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority if offering brokerage services.
- **Insurance**: a major management consulting firm says outside tech-powered giants are shaping the insurance market; **Amazon** and **Google** have partnered with insurance companies to offer various insurance products, and pushing further without a partner would invite state-level regulation, since insurance has been chartered and regulated solely by the states for the past 150 years, with no federal regulators akin to the SEC or the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency.
- **Banking**: Big Tech's entry into banking generates great interest and speculation; some have pursued nonbank lending, and the "Plex" project, which **Google** abandoned in late 2021, was the most direct step to date by the industry.
- U.S. law generally prohibits commercial (nonfinancial) enterprises from operating banks, a separation dating back to the Glass-Steagall Act and the Bank Holding Company Act, aimed at preventing one company from both offering a product and the deposit-funded loan to purchase it.
- An exception is the industrial loan company (ILC), a financial institution chartered in six states that functions much like a typical bank but can be owned by nonfinancial, commercial firms; ILCs meeting certain criteria are exempt from the definition of bank under the Bank Holding Company Act and thus not subject to Federal Reserve oversight, but are considered state banks under the FDIC Act and eligible for deposit insurance.
- This patchwork framework lets a company with other commercial interests offer banking services, obtain deposit insurance, and operate nationally without consolidated supervision by the Federal Reserve System.
- **Relevant history**: in the mid-2000s, **Walmart** and **Home Depot** made unsuccessful attempts to establish or acquire ILCs and dropped them amid public opposition; the FDIC then imposed a moratorium on new ILC insurance applications from July 2006 to January 2008, and Section 603 of the Dodd-Frank Act imposed another from July 2010 to July 2013.
- The FDIC did not grant an ILC insurance application again until March 2020, when it approved **Nelnet** and **Square** for deposit insurance; **Square** offers services similar to the Big Techs in the report, including a mobile wallet with payment and peer-to-peer transfer functions.
- An outstanding ILC charter application by Japanese e-commerce company **Rakuten** is also relevant; it filed its most recent application on January 15, 2021, after withdrawing two previous ones, and is sometimes called the "Amazon of Japan" with a business model similar to U.S. Big Tech companies, so an FDIC decision would carry regulatory arbitrage considerations.
- Theoretically, a Big Tech with an ILC charter could benefit from access to deposits and deposit insurance without the increased regulatory burden and costs of consolidated financial oversight by the Federal Reserve System.
