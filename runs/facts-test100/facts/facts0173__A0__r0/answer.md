Periorbital hyperpigmentation, also referred to as peri-palpebral hyperpigmentation, dark eyelids, dark eye circles, dark circles, or under-eye circles, is a color difference between the palpebral skin and the remaining facial skin. Although it is merely a color variation, it makes people look tired or older, which negatively affects their quality of life.

The condition has a higher prevalence in individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes, and it affects all age groups and genders equally. However, there are more complaints from women, especially senior women. Dark eye circles with a vascular component are known to present a dominant autosomal family inheritance pattern.

Periorbital hyperpigmentation appears to have multifactorial causes involving both intrinsic factors, determined by an individual's genetics, and extrinsic factors such as sun exposure, smoking, alcoholism, and sleep deprivation. A distinctive feature in its etiopathogeny is the presence of melanic pigment and hemosiderotic pigment in the affected sites.

Melanic hyperpigmentation is more frequent in brunet adults as a consequence of excessive and cumulative sun exposure, which increases melanin production, reduces skin thickness, and increases the dilatation of blood vessels. Intense vascularization is mainly found in people belonging to certain ethnic groups, including Arabs, Turks, Hindus, inhabitants of the Iberian Peninsula, and their respective descendants. In these ethnicities, the condition tends to manifest earlier, often during childhood. In such individuals, there is no change in the color of the skin; rather, the eyelid appears darkened because dilated vessels are visible due to the transparency of the skin. The problem is often aggravated when the lower eyelid's vessels become more dilated, for example from fatigue, insomnia, oral breathing, or crying, causing dermal blood extravasation. The liberation of ferric ions takes place locally, entailing the formation of free radicals that stimulate melanocytes, which in turn generates melanic pigmentation.

Other causes noted as responsible for the appearance of dark eye circles include post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation secondary to atopic and contact dermatitis, sleep deprivation, oral breathing, alcoholism, smoking, use of certain medications (contraceptives, chemotherapy, antipsychotics, and some types of eye drops), the presence of palpebral sagging due to aging, and disorders that develop with hydric retention and palpebral edema, such as thyroid disorders, nephropathies, cardiopathies, and pneumopathies. All of these factors worsen the unattractive appearance of dark eye circles.

The palpebral skin is the thinnest in the human body, measuring less than 1 mm. Its epidermis is very thin at 0.4 mm compared to the palmoplantar region, which is approximately 1.6 mm. The palpebral dermis is composed of loose conjunctive tissue and is extremely thin in that region. The thinness of the skin, combined with the lack of fatty tissue, gives that region its characteristic translucency. As a result, the accumulation of melanin and/or vessel dilatation in that region can be easily seen through transparency, appearing as bilateral homogeneous hyperpigmentation.

The color of the skin in the palpebral region results from the combination of several factors, some of genetic-racial origin, such as the amount of melanin pigment, and others of individual, regional, or even gender origins, such as the thickness of the various components and the blood volume in their vessels.

Various treatments have been proposed for periorbital hyperchromia, however there are few studies on their long-term efficacy. The main types of treatment include topical application of depigmenting products, chemical peelings, dermabrasion, cryosurgery, fillings with hyaluronic acid, intense pulsed light, and CO2, argon, ruby, and excimer lasers.

**Individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes are most affected by periorbital hyperchromia.**
