**Background context:** The document traces the idea back to J.C.R. Licklider's 1962 "Galactic Network" concept at MIT/DARPA. Licklider, as the first head of DARPA's computer research program, convinced his successors—including MIT researcher Lawrence G. Roberts—of the importance of this networking concept.

**Leonard Kleinrock's contributions:**
- At MIT, he published the first paper on packet switching theory in July 1961 and the first book on the subject in 1964.
- He convinced Roberts of the theoretical feasibility of communications using packets rather than circuits, which the document calls "a major step along the path towards computer networking."
- His team at UCLA prepared the network measurement system for the ARPANET.
- Because of his early development of packet switching theory and his focus on analysis, design, and measurement, his Network Measurement Center at UCLA was selected to be the first node on the ARPANET.
- In September 1969, BBN installed the first IMP (Interface Message Processor) at UCLA and connected the first host computer.
- One month later, the first host-to-host message on the ARPANET was sent from Kleinrock's laboratory to SRI.

**Lawrence G. Roberts' contributions:**
- In 1965, working with Thomas Merrill, he connected the TX-2 computer in Massachusetts to the Q-32 in California over a low-speed dial-up telephone line, creating the first (however small) wide-area computer network ever built. The experiment showed that time-shared computers could work well together, but that the circuit-switched telephone system was totally inadequate for the job—confirming Kleinrock's conviction that packet switching was needed.
- In late 1966 he went to DARPA to develop the computer network concept and published his plan for the "ARPANET" in 1967.
- At the conference where he presented the paper, he learned of parallel work at NPL in the UK (Donald Davies and Roger Scantlebury) and at RAND. The word "packet" was adopted from the NPL work, and the proposed ARPANET line speed was upgraded from 2.4 kbps to 50 kbps.
- In August 1968, after Roberts and the DARPA-funded community refined the overall structure and specifications for the ARPANET, DARPA released an RFQ for the packet switches (IMPs), which was won by Frank Heart's group at BBN.
- While BBN built the IMPs, Roberts, working with Howard Frank and his team at Network Analysis Corporation, designed and optimized the network topology and economics.
- In July 1972, Roberts expanded the utility of the new email application by writing the first email utility program to list, selectively read, file, forward, and respond to messages.

**Outcome:** By the end of 1969, four host computers (UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara, and University of Utah) were connected into the initial ARPANET, and, as the document puts it, "the budding Internet was off the ground."
