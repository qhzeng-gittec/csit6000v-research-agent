Based on `doc.txt`, here is a summary of the contributions of Lawrence G. Roberts and Leonard Kleinrock to the origin of the internet:

**Leonard Kleinrock**
- At MIT, he published the first paper on packet switching theory in July 1961 and the first book on the subject in 1964.
- He convinced Roberts of the theoretical feasibility of communications using packets rather than circuits, which the document calls "a major step along the path towards computer networking."
- His conviction of the need for packet switching was confirmed by Roberts's 1965 experiment, which showed that the circuit-switched telephone system was totally inadequate for connecting time-shared computers.
- His team at UCLA prepared the network measurement system for the ARPANET.
- Because of his early development of packet switching theory and his focus on analysis, design, and measurement, his Network Measurement Center at UCLA was selected to be the first node on the ARPANET. BBN installed the first IMP there in September 1969, and the first host computer was connected.
- One month later, when SRI was connected, the first host-to-host message was sent from Kleinrock's laboratory to SRI.

**Lawrence G. Roberts**
- As an MIT researcher, he was among those convinced by J.C.R. Licklider of the importance of Licklider's networking concept.
- In 1965, working with Thomas Merrill, he connected the TX-2 computer in Massachusetts to the Q-32 in California with a low-speed dial-up telephone line, creating the first (however small) wide-area computer network ever built. The experiment showed that time-shared computers could work well together, but that the circuit-switched telephone system was totally inadequate for the job.
- In late 1966 he went to DARPA to develop the computer network concept and quickly put together his plan for the "ARPANET," publishing it in 1967.
- At the conference where he presented the paper, he learned from Roger Scantlebury about the NPL work of Donald Davies and Scantlebury and the work of Paul Baran and others at RAND. The word "packet" was adopted from the NPL work, and the proposed line speed for the ARPANET design was upgraded from 2.4 kbps to 50 kbps.
- In August 1968, after Roberts and the DARPA-funded community had refined the overall structure and specifications for the ARPANET, DARPA released an RFQ for the packet switches called Interface Message Processors (IMPs), which was won in December 1968 by a group headed by Frank Heart at BBN.
- While the BBN team worked on the IMPs, Roberts, working with Howard Frank and his team at Network Analysis Corporation, designed and optimized the network topology and economics.
- In July 1972, Roberts expanded the utility of electronic mail by writing the first email utility program to list, selectively read, file, forward, and respond to messages.

Together, these efforts led to the initial ARPANET: by the end of 1969, four host computers (UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara, and University of Utah) were connected, and "the budding Internet was off the ground."
