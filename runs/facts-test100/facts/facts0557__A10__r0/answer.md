Based on `doc.txt`, informal financial services are divided into two broad categories: those owned by their users and those offered for profit.

**User-owned informal financial services**

The document states that "Systems which facilitate financial transactions and are owned by their users are many and varied, and range from simple reciprocal arrangements between neighbours, savings clubs and rotating savings and credit associations (ROSCAS), to forms of insurance, building societies, and systems of co-operative business finance." Examples given include:

- **ROSCAs** — "They exist in almost every country (for example, 'partners' in Jamaica and Britain, bui in Vietnam, and njangi in Cameroon)." Specific examples: rickshaw ROSCAS in Bangladesh, where "a group of drivers forms, and each driver saves a set amount from his daily takings. When the fund is large enough (this usually takes about 15 days) a rickshaw is bought and distributed by lottery to one of the members"; and "'Merry- go-rounds', as ROSCAS are called among Kikuyu women in Kenya, are sometimes used by women as a means of accumulating enough money to buy new household utensils or clothes."
- **Neighbourhood reciprocity** — "Among Moslems in Kerala State in southern India kuri kalyanam are invitations to a feast to which the guest is expected to bring a cash gift," and "In Vietnam one kind of hui (a generic name for various financial devices) involves a similar pooling of resources for one person on one occasion to be reciprocated later by others, at different times."
- **An accumulating savings club in Mexico** — "In towns and villages in Mexico neighbours place frequent but irregular savings with trusted shopkeepers. Just before Christmas, the cash is returned to the saver."
- **Building societies** — "In a lower-middle-class area of Dhaka, 165 employees in the Public Works Department belong to their own building society' which was started over 16 years ago. Each saves 200 taka ($5) a month out of his wages."
- **Insurance funds / funeral funds** — "Originally burial societies, iddir have extended to provide a wide range of insurance services in urban Ethiopia," usually paying "out for funeral expenses or financial assistance to families of the deceased, and sometimes to cover other costs, such as medical expenses and losses due to fire or theft."

**Informal financial services for profit**

"These fall into two groups: deposit takers (often also called money-guards) and lenders." Examples:

- **Deposit takers** — "alajos, or peripatetic deposit takers" in Nigeria, e.g. Idowu Alakpere, who "uses a bicycle to go door-to-door round the outer suburb of Lagos" where "500 customers who each save about 10 or 15 naira with him (about 50 to 75 cents US) at each daily visit."
- **Money-lenders** — "Forms of lending also operate in a variety of ways, such as money-lenders; pawnbrokers, who take collateral in the form of physical assets; and forms of trade credit and hire purchase." In Cochin, India, "'Tamilians' provide a money-lending service to poor slum dwellers on a daily basis."
- **Pawnbrokers** — "Residents of the slums of Vijayawada use their local pawnbroker when they need money quickly. He is reliably available at his gold- smithing shop and he charges 3 per cent a month for loans pledged against gold, 5 per cent for silver and 9 per cent for brass."
- **Advance crop sales** — "A practice common in many countries is known as ijon in some areas of Indonesia. Farmers often need cash to get them through the 'bungry' season... They are forced to make an advance sale of the crop, usually to a grain buyer or his agent."
- **Trade credit** — people "obtain the fertiliser on credit from a nearby shop" and return the money from sales at the close of the market; and "The dadon credit system used to finance prawn cultivation in Bangladesh is an example of a trading system in which credit is passed on through a chain of intermediaries between the prawn farmer and exporters to Europe."

The document also notes that "Informal services available include savings facilities, provision of credit for consumption, and funding for predictable but expensive events such as marriages and funerals."
