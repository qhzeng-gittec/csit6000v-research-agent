**General danger**
- Bacterial meningitis is described as "a rare but potentially fatal disease" that can cause stroke, hearing loss, and permanent brain damage.
- Both meningitis and encephalitis "can progress quickly and have the potential to cause severe, irreversible neurological damage," so suspected cases should receive immediate, aggressive medical treatment.

**By type**
- **Pneumococcal meningitis** is the most common and "most serious form of bacterial meningitis" (about 6,000 U.S. cases per year). Survivors "often suffer neurological damage ranging from deafness to severe brain damage."
- **Meningococcal meningitis** is highly contagious (about 2,600 U.S. cases per year). "Between 10-15 percent of cases are fatal, with another 10-15 percent causing brain damage and other serious side effects."
- **Haemophilus influenzae meningitis** was once the most common form, but the vaccine has greatly reduced cases.
- **Fungal meningitis** is treatable but "often recurs in nearly half of affected persons."
- **Amoebic meningitis** is rare but "can be rapidly fatal."
- The document also notes (in a somewhat garbled sentence) that "meningitis is rarely life threatening and no specific treatment is needed," which appears to refer to milder (likely viral) forms.

**Prognosis**
- Outcome depends on the infectious agent, severity, and how quickly treatment is given.
- In most cases, people with very mild meningitis can make a full recovery, though it may be slow; those with only headache, fever, and stiff neck may recover in 2–4 weeks.
- In more serious cases, meningitis can cause hearing and/or speech loss, blindness, permanent brain and nerve damage, behavioral changes, cognitive disabilities, lack of muscle control, seizures, and memory loss, and affected individuals may need long-term therapy, medication, and supportive care.
- On the positive side, appropriate antibiotic treatment for most types of bacterial meningitis "can greatly reduce the risk of dying from the disease," and effective vaccines exist for Haemophilus influenzae, pneumococcal, and meningococcal meningitis.

In short, the document portrays meningitis as ranging from mild and fully recoverable to rapidly fatal, with bacterial forms being the most dangerous and capable of causing death or permanent neurological damage.
