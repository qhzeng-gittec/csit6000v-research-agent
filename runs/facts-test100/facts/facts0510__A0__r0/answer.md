## Key differences

**Traditional publishing**
- You get support from a full team of experts — editors, designers, and salespeople — which often leads to higher sales and a more polished product.
- It's notoriously slow, and has historically excluded writers from marginalized groups.
- Most major publishers don't accept submissions directly; you need an agent, who sells your work to publishers in return for around 15% of profits. Querying agents is considered one of the hardest parts — writers may receive hundreds of rejections over multiple books.
- Multiple rounds of agent and editor revisions are built into the process.
- You're paid an advance (a lump sum the publisher expects to earn back from sales): as low as $1,000–$2,000 from small indie publishers, up to six figures or more from large publishers, depending on genre, target age group, and career stage. After the advance is "earned out," you earn royalties on every additional copy sold.
- You pay nothing up front. If an agent or editor charges "reading fees" or asks you to pay for design/production, the document says you're being scammed.

**Self-publishing** (via platforms like IngramSpark, Amazon Kindle Direct, or Indie Author Project / Biblioboard)
- A quicker way to share your book with readers, with freedom from industry pressure to write something "sellable."
- Books can suffer from a lack of professional editing and design; you decide how much to revise (hire a freelance editor, use friends/family, or skip revisions).
- Once published, you *are* the team: pricing, formatting, selling, marketing, and distribution — even for e-only publishing you must format your manuscript (or pay someone to) and submit to platforms, and you must compete against professional marketing teams from billion-dollar publishers.
- Income depends entirely on how many books you sell; there are no advances. You may pay up front for editing, design, or printing, though free e-publishing options exist and print-on-demand (IngramSpark) avoids paying to print and warehouse unsold copies.

## How to decide which route

The document says there's no one right answer — it depends on your personal goals. It suggests weighing:
1. **How you want to spend your time** — querying agents (traditional) vs. becoming an expert in everything from typesetting to distribution (self-publishing).
2. **Why you want a writing career** — if money isn't a goal, knowing that up front saves you stress and rejection.
3. **Your genre and target age group** — these can have a huge impact on profitability:
   - **Romance** is a rare genre that tops traditional sales *and* has seen significant income growth for self-published authors (many romance writers are now "hybrid").
   - **Memoirs** are notoriously hard to sell either way unless you're already famous.
   - **Niche topics** (e.g., regional history) may have too small an audience for big publishers, making self-publishing the only way to share them.
   - **Children's books** tend to struggle with self-publishing, since many self-published books are e-books only, fewer children have e-readers, and adults buying them want physical gifts.

## Which route has greater profit potential?

The document does **not** declare one route more profitable than the other. It explicitly says it "can be difficult to know whether it's financially better to shop around your manuscript a little longer, waiting for that big advance, or whether you should go ahead and self-publish to start selling copies now," and that the answer depends on your genre and target age group — "sometimes, both can be profitable."

What it does show: traditional publishing can offer large advances (up to six figures or more) plus royalties and often higher sales, while self-publishing lets you start earning immediately but pays only what you sell, with possible up-front costs. So the "greater profit" answer, per this document, is: it depends on your genre, audience, and whether you'd rather wait for a potential big advance or start selling now.
