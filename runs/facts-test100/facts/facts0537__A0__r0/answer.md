(Note: the document does not mention generative AI specifically; the cases concern risk-assessment algorithms, computer-generated evidence, and DNA-analysis software.) The courts' conclusions were:

**1. State v. Loomis, 371 Wis.2d 235 (2016), cert. denied (2017)**
- The defendant, convicted of offenses arising from a drive-by shooting, argued that the sentencing judge's consideration of an evidence-based risk assessment (which indicated a high risk of recidivism) violated his due process rights.
- **Conclusion:** The Supreme Court rejected the due process argument but imposed conditions on the use of risk assessments.

**2. State v. Morrill, 2019 WL 3765586 (N.M. App. 2019)**
- The defendant argued that attestations made by a computer program (whether attributable to an AI software or its developer) and the automated conclusions of the Roundup and Forensic Toolkit programs constituted inadmissible hearsay not covered by the business record exception.
- **Conclusion:** The court upheld the district court's determination that the computer-generated evidence was **not hearsay**. Because the software programs made the relevant assertions without any intervention or modification by a person using the software, the assertions are not "statements by a person" governed by the hearsay rules.

**3. State v. Pickett, 466 N.J. Super. 270 (App. Div. 2021)**
- A case of first impression in New Jersey on forensic evidentiary technology, concerning whether the defendant was entitled to the trade secrets (source code and related documentation) of the TrueAllele probabilistic genotyping DNA software to challenge its reliability at a Frye hearing.
- **Conclusion:** The court held that the defendant is entitled to access the proprietary information, which is directly relevant to testing whether the software operates as intended and to the judge's gatekeeping reliability determination. "Hiding the source code is not the answer"; the solution is producing it under a protective order, which safeguards both the company's intellectual property rights and the defendant's constitutional liberty interest.

**4. State v. Saylor, 2019 Ohio 1025 (Ct. App. 2019) (concurring opinion of Froelich, J.)**
- The trial court considered the defendant's ORAS (recidivism risk) score in sentencing a 27-year-old heroin addict to 12½ years.
- **Conclusion:** The concurrence observed that the "algorithmization" of sentencing is perhaps a good-faith attempt to remove unbridled discretion and its inherent biases, but noted that recidivism risk modeling still involves human choices about which characteristics to assess, their hierarchy, and their relative weight. Ultimately, the court found **no basis for reversal**, because the trial court's decision was not clearly and convincingly unsupported by the record, regardless of any weight given to the ORAS score.
