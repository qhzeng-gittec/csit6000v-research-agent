According to `doc.txt`, if you can't qualify for a normal (unsecured) credit card, the article offers these alternatives:

1. **Open a secured credit card** (Step 6): The article says that if your credit is heavily damaged and you have trouble qualifying for a credit card, "a secured credit card can help." Secured cards are "pretty easy to get, even if your credit is damaged," because they rely on a cash security deposit to minimize risk to the issuer. If you pay off your balance in full, you get the deposit back when you close the account, and some issuers will even automatically upgrade you to an unsecured account and return your deposit. The FAQ section reinforces this: "If you can't qualify for an unsecured card without an annual fee, consider a secured credit card instead." It also suggests that banks where you already have a good reputation and your local credit union are often the best places to find a secured card, and to look for one that lets you graduate easily to an unsecured card (and avoid annual fees, if possible).

2. **Become an authorized user on someone else's card** (Step 7): You can be added as an authorized user on another person's card (such as a trusted family member). You receive your own credit card with your name on it, but the account remains the primary holder's responsibility, and the card company typically reports the account to the credit bureaus for both people. As long as the account is in good standing, this can help raise your credit score — though the article cautions that if the cardholder or authorized user runs up a high balance, both users could see credit score damage, so only tie your credit to individuals you trust.
