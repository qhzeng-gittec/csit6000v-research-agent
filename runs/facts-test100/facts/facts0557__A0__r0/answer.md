Based on `doc.txt`, informal financial services fall into two broad categories — user-owned services and services offered for profit — with the following examples:

**User-owned informal financial services**
- **Simple reciprocal arrangements between neighbours** — e.g., *kuri kalyanam* among Moslems in Kerala, southern India (cash gifts at feasts, later returned doubled), and a form of *hui* in Vietnam involving pooling of resources for one person, reciprocated later.
- **Savings clubs** — e.g., an accumulating savings club in Mexico, where neighbours place frequent but irregular savings with trusted shopkeepers and receive the cash back just before Christmas.
- **Rotating savings and credit associations (ROSCAs)** — e.g., "partners" in Jamaica and Britain, *bui* in Vietnam, *njangi* in Cameroon, "merry-go-rounds" among Kikuyu women in Kenya, and rickshaw ROSCAS in Dhaka, Bangladesh, where drivers pool daily savings to buy a rickshaw by lottery.
- **Insurance funds / funeral funds** — e.g., *iddir* in urban Ethiopia, which pay out for funeral expenses and sometimes medical costs or losses from fire or theft.
- **Building societies** — e.g., a building society in a lower-middle-class area of Dhaka, Bangladesh, where employees save monthly and lend to members to buy land and building materials.
- **Systems of co-operative business finance.**

**Informal financial services provided for profit**
- **Deposit takers (money-guards)** — e.g., the mobile *alajo* in Nigeria, a peripatetic deposit taker who cycles door-to-door, collects small daily savings, and charges a negative interest rate on withdrawals.
- **Money-lenders** — e.g., the "Tamilians" in Cochin, India, who lend to poor slum dwellers on a daily basis with set terms (a 300% APR).
- **Pawnbrokers** — e.g., in Vijayawada, western India, who lend against gold, silver, and even brass (kitchen pots and pans) at 3–9% per month.
- **Trade credit and hire purchase** — e.g., market traders selling fertiliser on credit from a nearby shop, and the *dadon* credit system financing prawn cultivation in Bangladesh, where credit passes through a chain of intermediaries.
- **Advance crop sales** — e.g., *ijon* in Indonesia, where farmers make an advance sale of their crop to a grain buyer to get cash through the hungry season.

The document also notes that some informal systems evolve into formal ones — e.g., UK friendly societies becoming building societies, *njangi* ROSCAS in Cameroon evolving into small banks, and chit funds in India as a formalised version of a ROSCA.
