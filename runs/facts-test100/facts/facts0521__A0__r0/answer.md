**The Internet**
- Began in 1969 as ARPANET, a U.S. Department of Defense project to build a nationwide computer network that could withstand major disasters, thanks to its decentralized structure.
- Most people define it as a collection of computer networks, where a network is a group of two or more computers connected together with cables that allow them to share information.
- All computers "on the Internet" use the same protocols to send information back and forth, so they can communicate regardless of their hardware or software.
- It was originally designed for research and scholarly communication (used by government, military, and educational institutions in the 1960s–70s), but later expanded to informal communication, entertainment, and commerce.
- Today it remains decentralized, made up of independently owned and managed networks of all sizes, with large high-speed "backbone providers" and Internet Service Providers (ISPs) that sell connections to consumers.

**The World Wide Web**
- Invented in 1989 by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN in Geneva, who wanted to make the information he used for research on the Internet more organized and accessible.
- It is based on hypertext — a method of linking documents using embedded hyperlinks (which can be underlined/colored text or graphics).
- Web documents are written in HTML, stored on computers called servers, and retrieved by a Web browser (a program that knows how to read and display hypertext documents and communicate with servers).
- The communication between browser and server uses the Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP). Documents are called Web pages, organized into Web sites, and each page has its own address, a URL.

**The key difference**
The Internet is the physical/logical infrastructure — a collection of networks and the shared protocols that let computers exchange information. The World Wide Web is a specific application built on that infrastructure: a hypertext system of HTML documents, servers, browsers, and HTTP that lets users retrieve and display linked documents. The document lists the World Wide Web as one of the "Uses for the Internet" (alongside e-mail, Telnet, FTP, Usenet, and IRC), and notes that the Web's ease of use and graphical "point-and-click" interface was a major reason for the Internet's growth explosion. In short, the Web is a service that runs over the Internet, not the Internet itself.
