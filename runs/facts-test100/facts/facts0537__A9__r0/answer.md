**State v. Loomis, 371 Wis.2d 235, 881 N.W.2d 749 (2016), cert. denied (2017)**
- The defendant, convicted of offenses arising from a drive-by shooting, argued on appeal that the sentencing judge's consideration of an evidence-based risk assessment (which indicated a high risk of recidivism) violated his right to due process.
- The Supreme Court rejected that argument, but imposed conditions on the use of risk assessments.

**State v. Morrill, No. A-1-CA-36490, 2019 WL 3765586 (N.M. App. July 24, 2019)**
- The defendant argued that attestations made by a computer program (whether attributable to an artificial intelligence software or its developer) constituted inadmissible hearsay not admissible under the business record exception.
- The court concluded the district court correctly determined that the computer-generated evidence produced by Roundup and Forensic Toolkit was **not hearsay**. Because the software programs made the relevant assertions without any intervention or modification by a person using the software, the assertions are not statements by a person governed by the hearsay rules.

**State v. Pickett, 466 N.J. Super. 270 (App. Div. 2021)**
- In a case of first impression, the court addressed whether the defendant was entitled to the trade secrets of a private company (including the source code and related documentation of TrueAllele, a probabilistic genotyping DNA software) solely to challenge at a Frye hearing the reliability of the science underlying the software and expert testimony.
- The court held that the defense expert's access to the proprietary information is directly relevant to the general-acceptance question and would allow independent testing of whether the software operates as intended; without it, the defendant is "relegated to blindly accepting the company's assertions as to its reliability," and the judge cannot reach an informed reliability determination.
- The court concluded that "hiding the source code is not the answer" and that the solution is producing it under a protective order, which safeguards both the company's intellectual property rights and the defendant's constitutional liberty interest.

**State v. Saylor, 2019 Ohio 1025 (Ct. App. 2019) (concurring opinion of Froelich, J.)**
- The concurrence noted that the "algorithmization" of sentencing (via the ORAS risk score) is perhaps a good-faith attempt to remove unbridled discretion and its inherent biases, but that "recidivism risk modeling still involves human choices about what characteristics and factors should be assessed, what hierarchy governs their application, and what relative weight should be ascribed to each."
- The court found no basis for reversal: the trial court's decision was not clearly and convincingly unsupported by the record, regardless of any weight potentially given to the ORAS score.
