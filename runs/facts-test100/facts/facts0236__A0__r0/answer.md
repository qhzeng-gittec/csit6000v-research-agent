**The underlying immune malfunction**
Lupus is a chronic condition caused by a malfunctioning immune system. Instead of only attacking foreign invaders like bacteria and viruses, the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues — skin, joints, muscles, nerves, kidneys, heart or lungs — causing ongoing inflammation and pain. Because of this, the overall health of the mother before and during pregnancy directly affects the outcome: "the healthier you are before you get pregnant, the greater the chance that you will have a healthy pregnancy and a healthy baby."

**Risk of flares**
Lupus symptoms can flare during pregnancy or immediately after delivery (though this is less likely if the condition was stable before conception). Flares are also hard to manage because normal pregnancy symptoms — fatigue, fluid build-up in the joints, skin changes, hair loss, shortness of breath, and joint pain — can mimic lupus symptoms, making it difficult to distinguish a flare from a normal pregnancy.

**Medication problems**
Some lupus medications (such as methotrexate, mycophenolate, or cyclophosphamide) are harmful to the baby; some can cross the placenta and pose a threat to it. Women often need to stop certain medications months before conceiving, and doctors may need to make important medication changes to ensure a safe pregnancy.

**Antibodies that harm the placenta and baby**
- About one third of women with lupus have antibodies that may cause blood clots and interfere with the proper functioning of the placenta, most often in the second trimester. The placenta then can't supply the baby with sufficient nourishment, growth slows, and early delivery via caesarean section may be required.
- These antibodies (antiphospholipid antibodies) also increase the risk of miscarriage, though blood thiners can help prevent this in many women.
- Around one third of women with lupus have antibodies that may cause lupus-like symptoms in the baby after birth (neonatal lupus) — such as skin rash, unusual blood count, and rarely heartbeat irregularities.

**Increased rates of pregnancy complications**
Women with lupus face higher risks of:
- high blood pressure (hypertension)
- pre-eclampsia (high blood pressure plus protein in the urine), which occurs in one in five women with lupus and, if left untreated, can endanger the life of both the woman and the baby
- premature labour and preterm birth
- the baby being born with low birth weight
- blood clots in the legs or lungs
- miscarriage
- emergency caesarean section
- excessive bleeding after delivery

**Who is at higher risk**
The document notes that some women should delay pregnancy altogether — those with active lupus, those taking certain medications (methotrexate, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide), those with kidney disease, and those with a history of thrombosis or miscarriage.

In short, the combination of an overactive immune system, potentially harmful medications, antibodies that can damage the placenta or the newborn, and a higher likelihood of complications like pre-eclampsia and flares is what makes pregnancy more complicated for women with lupus — which is why the document stresses careful planning, stable disease for at least six months before conception, and close monitoring by a rheumatologist and specialist obstetrician throughout the pregnancy.
