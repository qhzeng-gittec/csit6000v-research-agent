- **Overview**: The report examines Big Tech's current financial offerings, how existing regulations apply to them, and related policy implications. - **Investment Advisory Services**: Investment advisory services (asset, portfolio, and wealth management) are a potential area for Big Tech expansion. A November 2021 survey found over 90% of wealth managers believe Big Tech companies will enter the market, with more than 50% expecting significant disruption. Fintechs have already entered investment management using machine learning (e.g., robo-advising, retail stock trading). Entry would require registration as investment advisors with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and possibly with the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority for brokerage services.

- **Insurance**: A major management consulting firm noted that "outside tech-powered giants" are shaping the insurance market. **Amazon** and **Google** have partnered with insurance companies to offer various insurance products. Moving further without partners would trigger state-level regulation, since insurance has been chartered and regulated solely by the states for 150 years, with no federal regulators akin to the SEC or the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency.

- **Banking**: Big Tech's potential entry into banking generates significant interest and speculation. Some have pursued nonbank lending. **Google**'s "Plex" project, abandoned in late 2021, was the most direct step to date toward becoming a bank. Expanding into bank-like activities (e.g., accepting deposits) would likely require an industrial loan company (ILC) charter.

- **Legislative Framework**: U.S. law generally prohibits nonfinancial firms from operating banks, a separation dating back to the Glass-Steagall Act and the Bank Holding Company Act, aimed at preventing one company from both selling a product and offering the deposit-funded loan to buy it. ILCs are an exception: charted in six states, they function like banks but can be owned by commercial firms. ILCs meeting certain criteria are exempt from the bank definition under the Bank Holding Company Act (avoiding Federal Reserve oversight) yet are considered state banks under the FDIC Act, making them eligible for deposit insurance. This patchwork allows a commercial firm to offer banking services, obtain deposit insurance, and operate nationally without consolidated Federal Reserve supervision.

- **Relevant History**: Nonfinancial companies have historically pursued the ILC route. In the mid-2000s, **Walmart** and **Home Depot** made unsuccessful attempts to establish or acquire ILCs, dropping them amid public opposition. The FDIC then imposed a moratorium on new ILC insurance applications (July 2006–January 2008), and Section 603 of the Dodd-Frank Act imposed another (July 2010–July 2013). The FDIC did not approve an ILC insurance application again until March 2020, when it approved **Nelnet** and **Square** for deposit insurance. **Square** offers services similar to Big Tech's, including a mobile wallet with payment and peer-to-peer transfer functions.

- **Rakuten Application**: Japanese e-commerce company **Rakuten**, sometimes called the "Amazon of Japan," has an outstanding ILC charter application (most recently filed January 15, 2021, after two withdrawn applications). A decision by the FDIC would carry regulatory arbitrage considerations and likely influence other companies with similar models.

- **Bottom Line**: Theoretically, a Big Tech company with an ILC charter could access deposits and deposit insurance without the increased regulatory burden and costs of consolidated oversight by the Federal Reserve System.
