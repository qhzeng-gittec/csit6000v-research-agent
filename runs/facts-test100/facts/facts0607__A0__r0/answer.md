## What a concussion actually is

According to sports medicine physician Kristin Ernest, MD (Texas Children's Hospital), **a concussion is any force transmitted to the brain itself**. While it generally occurs with a blow to the head, any injury that causes the brain to shift rapidly within the skull — such as a whiplash injury — can cause a concussion.

**Common symptoms** (which vary from child to child) include:
- Headache
- Nausea
- Dizziness
- Irritability or crying
- Trouble concentrating
- Sleep disturbance (sleeping more or less than usual)

**Diagnosis:** A concussion will *not* show up on imaging tests such as MRI. It is diagnosed through medical evaluation of symptoms and often neurocognitive testing (a computer-based 20–30 minute test measuring brain functions like visual and verbal memory and reaction time, such as ImPACT testing).

**Treatment:** Both physical and cognitive rest — rest from all athletic activities, as well as from homework and screen time. Recovery generally takes about 7–10 days, **longer for younger children** and for those with a history of migraine or ADHD.

## How it affects a growing child

The document raises several concerns specific to children:

1. **Rising rates:** Concussion rates for children under 19 who play tackle football have doubled over the last decade, with most occurring during practices.

2. **Permanent damage if untreated:** If a concussion is undiagnosed and left untreated, it can lead to permanent brain damage.

3. **Subconcussive hits matter too:** Repeated blows to the head that don't result in concussions can, over a long period, have long-term health effects — primarily **chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE)**, a progressive degenerative disease of the brain found in athletes with a history of repetitive brain trauma.

4. **Cellular-level damage can accumulate:** Dr. Bennet Omalu (who discovered CTE in former NFL player Mike Webster) noted that a child playing football can show evidence of brain damage at the cellular level *even without* documented concussions or reported symptoms. If the child continues to play over many seasons, these cellular injuries accumulate to cause irreversible brain damage (CTE).

5. **Repeat concussions are more likely:** A child who has a first concussion is **3–6 times more likely** to suffer another than an athlete who has never had one. Returning to play before the brain has fully healed puts the child at higher risk for complications if a second concussion occurs.

## What the document suggests to reduce risk

- Learn your state's youth concussion laws (limits on full-contact practice, coach education, removal from play when a head injury is suspected, written medical clearance for return).
- Make sure your child is taught "Heads Up" tackle technique — research presented at the AAP's meeting showed that limits on full-contact practice plus Heads Up tackling are working to reduce concussion risk.
- Consider a baseline neurocognitive test (e.g., ImPACT) before the season, repeated if there are signs of concussion.
- Ensure properly fitted protective equipment, worn at all times.
- Teach your child the symptoms and require him to report them; a suspected concussion should be evaluated by a licensed health professional as soon as possible.
- Follow the doctor's orders exactly — failure to follow rest orders can prolong recovery.
- Dr. Jonathan Minor (CHOC) suggests starting with non-impact football so kids develop skills before contact play, plus core and neck strengthening exercises, which some research shows can help prevent traumatic brain injury.

The document's bottom line: it's up to parents to decide whether the risks of youth tackle football outweigh the recreational benefit — and it notes that while some experts (like Omalu) say children shouldn't play, others (like Dr. Ernest) say there's no conclusive evidence children shouldn't, provided they learn proper technique and wear properly fitting equipment.
