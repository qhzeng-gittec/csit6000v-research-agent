**Propranolol**
- Properties: "Propranolol has an equal affinity for β1- and β2-receptors, lacks intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA), and has no α-adrenergic receptor activity."
- Cons: "It is the most lipidsoluble β-blocker and generally has the most central nervous system side effects." Also, "First-pass liver metabolism (90%) is very high, requiring much higher oral doses than intravenous doses for pharmacodynamic effect." For continuous infusion, it "can prevent tachycardia and hypertension but must be used cautiously because of the potential of cumulative effects." In a comparative study, "50% of patients treated with propranolol developed clinically significant bronchospasm," and its hypotension incidence (6%) was lower than esmolol's (36%).
- Pros: A continuous infusion "of 1 to 3 mg/hr can prevent tachycardia and hypertension."

**Metoprolol**
- Pros: "Metoprolol was the first clinically used cardioselective β-blocker (Table 8-2). Its affinity for β1-receptors is 30 times higher than its affinity for β2-receptors, as demonstrated by radioligand binding."
- Cons: "As with any cardioselective β-blocker, higher serum levels may result in greater incidence of β2-blocking effects." Also, "The potency of metoprolol is approximately one half that of propranolol."
- Other pharmacology noted: "Metoprolol is lipid soluble, with 50% of the drug metabolized during first-pass hepatic metabolism and with only 3% excreted renally. Protein binding is less than 10%. Metoprolol's serum half-life is 3 to 4 hours."

**Esmolol**
- Pros: Its methylester group makes it "susceptible to rapid hydrolysis by red blood cell esterases (9-minute half-life)," and its effects "were completely reversed 30 minutes after discontinuation of the infusion." It "is cardioselective, blocking primarily β1-receptors. It lacks ISA and membrane-stabilizing effects and is mildly lipid soluble." It "appears safe in patients with bronchospastic disease," and in a comparative study "esmolol and placebo did not change airway resistance whereas 50% of patients treated with propranolol developed clinically significant bronchospasm."
- Cons: "Hypotension is a common side effect of intravenous esmolol. The incidence of hypotension was higher with esmolol (36%) than with propranolol (6%) at equal therapeutic endpoints." The text explains: "The cardioselective drugs may cause more hypotension because of β1-induced myocardial depression and the failure to block β2 peripheral vasodilation." Also, "Initial therapy during anesthesia may require significant reductions in both the loading and infusion doses."

**Labetalol**
- Pros: "Labetalol provides selective α1-receptor blockade and nonselective β1- and β2-blockade," with "partial β2-agonist effects that promote vasodilation." "In contrast to other β-blockers, clinically, labetalol should be considered a peripheral vasodilator that does not cause a reflex tachycardia." "BP and systolic vascular resistance decrease after an intravenous dose. Stroke volume (SV) and CO remain unchanged, with HR decreasing slightly." "The reduction in BP is dose related, and acutely hypertensive patients usually respond within 3 to 5 minutes after a bolus dose of 100 to 250 μg/kg."
- Cons/cautions: "the more critically ill or anesthetized patients should have their BP titrated beginning with 5- to 10-mg intravenous increments," and "Reduction in BP may last as long as 6 hours after intravenous dosing."
