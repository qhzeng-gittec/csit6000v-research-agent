**Propranolol**
- Properties: equal affinity for β1- and β2-receptors (nonselective), no intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA), no α-adrenergic activity; most lipidsoluble β-blocker.
- Cons:
  - Generally has the most central nervous system side effects (due to high lipidsolubility).
  - First-pass liver metabolism is very high (90%), requiring much higher oral doses than IV doses.
  - Continuous infusion (1–3 mg/hr) can prevent tachycardia and hypertension but must be used cautiously because of potential cumulative effects.
  - In a comparative study, 50% of patients treated with propranolol developed clinically significant bronchospasm (esmolol and placebo did not change airway resistance).
- Pros:
  - Continuous infusion can prevent tachycardia and hypertension after noncardiac surgery in patients with cardiac disease.
  - Lower incidence of hypotension than esmolol (6% vs. 36%) at equal therapeutic endpoints.

**Metoprolol**
- Pros:
  - First clinically used cardioselective β-blocker; β1-receptor affinity is 30 times higher than β2 affinity.
  - Low protein binding (<10%); only 3% excreted renally; serum half-life 3–4 hours.
- Cons:
  - As with any cardioselective β-blocker, higher serum levels may result in greater incidence of β2-blocking effects.
  - 50% of the drug is metabolized during first-pass hepatic metabolism.
  - Potency is approximately one half that of propranolol.

**Esmolol**
- Pros:
  - Very short 9-minute half-life (rapid hydrolysis by red blood cell esterases); effects completely reversed 30 minutes after discontinuation of infusion.
  - Steady-state concentrations reached within 5 minutes with a loading dose (30 minutes without).
  - Cardioselective (primarily β1), lacks ISA and membrane-stabilizing effects.
  - Appears safe in patients with bronchospastic disease; in a comparative study, esmolol did not change airway resistance, whereas 50% of propranolol-treated patients developed clinically significant bronchospasm.
  - Produced significant reductions in BP, HR, and cardiac index in patients with coronary artery disease.
- Cons:
  - Hypotension is a common side effect of IV esmolol (36% incidence vs. 6% with propranolol at equal therapeutic endpoints).
  - Initial therapy during anesthesia may require significant reductions in both loading and infusion doses.

**Labetalol**
- Pros:
  - Provides selective α1-receptor blockade plus nonselective β1/β2-blockade (β-blockade potency 5- to 10-fold greater than α1-blockade); partial β2-agonist effects promote vasodilation.
  - Unlike other β-blockers, it acts as a peripheral vasodilator that does not cause reflex tachycardia; BP and systolic vascular resistance decrease while stroke volume and CO remain unchanged and HR decreases only slightly.
  - Acutely hypertensive patients usually respond within 3–5 minutes after a 100–250 μg/kg bolus; BP reduction may last up to 6 hours after IV dosing.
  - Completely absorbed after oral administration.
- Cons:
  - First-pass hepatic metabolism is significant, producing inactive metabolites.
  - More critically ill or anesthetized patients require careful BP titration with 5- to 10-mg IV increments.
