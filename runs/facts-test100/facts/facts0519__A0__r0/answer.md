## Propranolol
**Characteristics:** Equal affinity for β1- and β2-receptors (nonselective); no intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA); no α-adrenergic activity; most lipidsoluble β-blocker.

**Pros:**
- Continuous infusion (1–3 mg/hr) can prevent tachycardia and hypertension after noncardiac surgery in patients with cardiac disease.
- Lower incidence of hypotension than esmolol (6% vs. 36%) at equal therapeutic endpoints.

**Cons:**
- Most lipidsoluble, so it generally has the most central nervous system side effects.
- Very high first-pass liver metabolism (90%), requiring much higher oral doses than IV doses for effect.
- Continuous infusion must be used cautiously because of potential cumulative effects.
- In a comparative study, 50% of patients treated with propranolol developed clinically significant bronchospasm (whereas esmolol and placebo did not change airway resistance).

## Metoprolol
**Characteristics:** First clinically used cardioselective β-blocker; β1-affinity 30 times higher than β2-affinity; lipid soluble; 50% first-pass hepatic metabolism; only 3% renally excreted; protein binding <10%; serum half-life 3–4 hours.

**Pros:**
- Cardioselective (β1-preferring), which is its distinguishing clinical advantage.

**Cons:**
- As with any cardioselective β-blocker, higher serum levels may result in greater incidence of β2-blocking effects.
- Potency is approximately one half that of propranolol.

## Esmolol
**Characteristics:** Cardioselective (primarily β1); lacks ISA and membrane-stabilizing effects; mildly lipid soluble; 9-minute half-life due to rapid hydrolysis by red blood cell esterases (not plasma cholinesterase); metabolites clinically insignificant.

**Pros:**
- Very short half-life (9 minutes) and rapid onset: steady-state concentrations within 5 minutes with a loading dose (30 minutes without).
- Effects are completely reversed 30 minutes after discontinuation of the infusion.
- Produced significant reductions in BP, HR, and cardiac index in patients with coronary artery disease.
- Appears safe in patients with bronchospastic disease (did not change airway resistance, unlike propranolol).

**Cons:**
- Hypotension is a common side effect (36% incidence vs. 6% with propranolol at equal therapeutic endpoints); the document notes cardioselective drugs may cause more hypotension due to β1-induced myocardial depression and failure to block β2 peripheral vasodilation.
- Initial therapy during anesthesia may require significant reductions in both loading and infusion doses.

## Labetalol
**Characteristics:** Selective α1-receptor blockade plus nonselective β1- and β2-blockade; β-blockade potency 5- to 10-fold greater than α1-blockade; partial β2-agonist effects promoting vasodilation; moderately lipid soluble; completely absorbed orally; significant first-pass hepatic metabolism to inactive metabolites; minimal renal excretion of unchanged drug; elimination half-life ~6 hours.

**Pros:**
- Unlike other β-blockers, it acts clinically as a peripheral vasodilator that does not cause reflex tachycardia.
- BP and systolic vascular resistance decrease after IV dosing, with stroke volume and cardiac output unchanged and only a slight HR decrease.
- Rapid response in acute hypertension: acutely hypertensive patients usually respond within 3–5 minutes after a 100–250 μg/kg bolus.
- BP reduction is dose related and may last as long as 6 hours after IV dosing.

**Cons:**
- The document does not explicitly list disadvantages for labetalol. It does note that critically ill or anesthetized patients require careful titration (starting with 5–10 mg IV increments), and that first-pass hepatic metabolism is significant (producing inactive metabolites), but these are not framed as drawbacks in the text.
