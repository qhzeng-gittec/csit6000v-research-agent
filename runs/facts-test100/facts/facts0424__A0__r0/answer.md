**Argument in favor of the Bill (as stated by the British Ambassador, Sir Auckland Geddes):**
- The Bill is "a sincere attempt to place definitely and finally in the hands of the elected representatives of the Irish people the duty and responsibility of working out their own salvation and the salvation of their country." The author notes this statement was cabled to America, and the rest of the text is his examination of "how far this statement is justified."

**Arguments against the Bill (the author's own):**
1. **Control of taxation and trade controls destiny:** The author's fundamental proposition is that whoever controls a country's taxation and trade policy controls its destiny and the entire character of its civilization — the power to make a country industrial or agricultural, to balance urban and rural interests, and to direct external trade.
2. **These powers are expressly denied to Ireland:** The Bill withholds from Ireland control over customs, excise, income tax, supertax, excess profits duty, and external trade.
3. **Less power than under the Act of Union:** Under the Act of Union, Ireland had 102 members in the Imperial Parliament who could at times hold the balance of power; even though that power was not very real (both British parties united against Ireland when interests conflicted), Irish votes were "worth angling for" and had to be paid for with Land Acts or other measures.
4. **Irish representation is reduced to near-powerlessness:** The new Bill reduces Irish representation at Westminster to forty-two members, making Ireland "practically powerless" at Westminster, while everything that really affects Irish economic interests is still legislated for by the British Parliament.

In short, the only "for" argument in the document is the Ambassador's claim that the Bill hands responsibility for Ireland's salvation to its elected representatives; the author counters that the Bill actually leaves Ireland with less economic power than it had under the Act of Union.
