**Violent media and real-world crime**
- **Markey and Ferguson (2017):** Countries that consume more video games have lower levels of violent crime than countries devoid of this media.
- **Markey, Markey, and French (2015):** Months when people play violent video games the most tend to be safer than months when they play them less.
- **Beerthuizen, Weijters, and van der Laan (2017):** Even when violent video games like *Grand Theft Auto* were first released, there tends to be a decrease in violent crimes.
- **Cunningham, Engelstätter, and Ward (2016) and Ward (2011):** These findings (that violent video game play is associated with lower crime) have been replicated by psychologists, economists, and sociologists at various universities while considering numerous other variables.
- **Messner (1986):** Violent assaults, rapes, and murders all decrease when people are watching extremely violent television shows.
- **Dahl and DellaVigna (2009) and Markey, French, and Markey (2015):** Years in which the most violent films were released saw decreases in violent crime, and crime consistently decreases in the days following the release of popular violent movies.
- **Snider (2014):** Male gamers in the United States spend a total of 468 million hours each month playing video games.

**Video games and aggression**
- **Krahé and Möller (2004), Anderson and Dill (2000), Greitemeyer (2019), and Anderson et al. (2004):** Their questionnaires measure aggression with items assessing whether an individual might be "jerky," antisocial, gossipy, or even conservative, rather than examining real acts of aggression.
- **Bushman and Gibson (2011):** Individuals who played violent video games were more likely to expose others to loud, irritating noises.
- **Anderson and Dill (2000):** Participants who played violent video games reported feeling more hostile on a questionnaire.
- **Deselms and Altman (2003):** Participants who played violent video games gave longer prison sentences to hypothetical criminals.
- **Yang, Huesmann, and Bushman (2014):** Participants who played violent video games gave hot sauce to people who do not like spicy food.
- **Kühn et al. (2019):** Many other researchers have been unable to replicate these experimental effects.
- **Bartholow, Sestir, and Davis (2005) and Carnagey and Anderson (2005):** These studies (along with others) used different scoring methods for the competitive reaction time task—e.g., the product of intensity and duration, or the square root of duration multiplied by intensity—illustrating that no standardized scoring method exists.
- **Elson et al. (2014):** Given the many scoring permutations, one can make it appear as if video games increase aggression, decrease aggression, or have no effect on aggression even within the same sample.
- **Ferguson (2015b) and Hilgard, Engelhardt, and Rouder (2017):** Meta-analyses found that, on average, only 0.4 percent to 4 percent of the variance in minor forms of aggression can be explained by violent video games.
