**Violence and crime section**
- **Markey and Ferguson (2017):** Countries that consume more video games have lower levels of violent crime than those devoid of this media.
- **Markey, Markey, and French (2015):** Months when people play violent video games the most tend to be safer than months they play them less.
- **Beerthuizen, Weijters, and van der Laan (2017):** Even when violent video games, like Grand Theft Auto, were first released, there tends to be a decrease in violent crimes.
- **Cunningham, Engelstätter, and Ward (2016); Ward (2011):** The above findings have been replicated by psychologists, economists, and sociologists at various universities considering numerous other variables.
- **Messner (1986):** Violent assaults, rapes, and murders all decrease when people are watching extremely violent television shows.
- **Dahl and DellaVigna (2009); Markey, French, and Markey (2015):** Years in which the most violent films were released saw decreases in violent crime, and crime consistently decreases in the days following the release of popular violent movies.
- **Felson (1994):** Presented as the source of "routine activity theory" — the notion that for a violent crime to occur, a perpetrator must be in the same location as the victim, in a location free of those who would likely prevent the crime (a theory rather than an empirical finding).
- **Snider (2014):** Male gamers in the United States spend a total of 468 million hours each month playing video games.

**Aggression section**
- **Bushman et al. (2016):** Aggressive acts tend not to cause such extreme physical harm as violent behaviors like homicides.
- **Krahé and Möller (2004); Anderson and Dill (2000); Greitemeyer (2019); Anderson et al. (2004):** Cited as examples of questionnaires used to measure aggression with items assessing whether an individual might be "jerky," antisocial, gossipy, or conservative.
- **Bushman and Gibson (2011):** Individuals who play violent video games are more likely to expose others to loud irritating noises.
- **Anderson and Dill (2000):** Participants who played violent video games reported feeling more hostile on a questionnaire.
- **Deselms and Altman (2003):** Participants who played violent video games gave longer prison sentences to hypothetical criminals.
- **Yang, Huesmann, and Bushman (2014):** Participants who played violent video games gave hot sauce to people who do not like spicy food.
- **Kühn et al. (2019):** Many other researchers cannot replicate these effects.
- **Bartholow, Sestir, and Davis (2005):** Cited as one of the ways the competitive reaction time task (CRTT) has been scored — as the product of the intensity and duration of the noise burst.
- **Carnagey and Anderson (2005):** Cited as another CRTT scoring method — the square root of the duration score multiplied by the intensity score.
- **Elson et al. (2014):** Given the many scoring permutations, one can make it appear as if video games increase aggression, decrease aggression, or have no effect on aggression even within the same sample.
- **Ferguson (2015b); Hilgard, Engelhardt, and Rouder (2017):** On average, only 0.4 percent to 4 percent of the variance in minor forms of aggression can be explained by violent video games.
