**What it is and when it came about**
- On November 13, 2023, the Supreme Court announced that it was adopting a Code of Conduct for the first time.
- Its purpose is to "set out succinctly and gather in one place the ethics rules and principles that guide the conduct of the Members of the Court."
- The Court emphasized that most of the rules are "not new" — the Code is largely just a written codification of principles the Justices have long followed.

**What it contains**
- The Code is made up of five ethical canons (general rules) plus accompanying commentary.
- The canons are nearly the same as those in the Code of Conduct for U.S. Judges, which applies only to lower federal judges.
- Many provisions deal with conflicts of interest and the appearance of impropriety.
- Specific provisions address financial activities and compliance with the Ethics in Government Act (EIGA). For example, Canon 4(H) declares the Justices' commitment to comply with financial disclosure laws, and the commentary says the Justices comply with the EIGA, the STOCK Act, and current Judicial Conference financial disclosure regulations.
- The commentary also says the Justices comply with Judicial Conference regulations on gifts, foreign gifts and decorations, and outside earned income, honoraria, and employment — consistent with their long-standing voluntary compliance.
- The Justices acknowledged through the Code that they file "the same financial disclosure reports as other federal judges."

**What it does NOT do**
- It seemingly does not impose any *new* financial disclosure requirements on the Justices.
- It has no enforcement mechanisms — there is no process to address alleged violations, so compliance may be left to the Justices' own discretion.

**Related context in the document**
- There's some uncertainty about whether Judicial Conference regulations even apply to the Supreme Court, since the Chief Justice said in 2011 that the Judicial Conference has no mandate to set rules for the Supreme Court. Nonetheless, at least one Associate Justice said he would follow the 2023 guidance, and the Justices have stated they comply with current Judicial Conference regulations.
- The document also notes proposed legislation (like the Supreme Court Ethics, Recusal, and Transparency Act) and ongoing debate over whether Congress has the authority to impose or enforce ethics rules on the Supreme Court — a question that remains untested.
