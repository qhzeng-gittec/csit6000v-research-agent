Periorbital hyperchromia, also known as periorbital hyperpigmentation, peri-palpebral hyperpigmentation, dark eyelids, dark eye circles, or under-eye circles, is a condition in which the skin around the eyes (the palpebral skin) shows a color difference compared to the rest of the facial skin. Although it is merely a color difference, it makes people look tired or older, which negatively affects their quality of life.

The condition has a higher prevalence in individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes, and it affects age groups and genders equally. Nevertheless, more complaints come from women, especially senior women. Few studies exist on its etiology, but dark eye circles with a vascular component are known to present a dominant autosomal family inheritance pattern.

Periorbital hyperpigmentation seems to have multifactorial causes involving intrinsic factors (determined by the individual's genetics) and extrinsic factors such as sun exposure, smoking, alcoholism, and sleep deprivation. A distinctive feature in its etiopathogeny is the presence of melanic pigment and hemosiderotic pigment in the affected sites. Melanic hyperpigmentation is more frequent in brunet adults, as a consequence of excessive and cumulative sun exposure, which increases melanin production, reduces skin thickness, and increases the dilatation of blood vessels.

Intense vascularization is mainly found in people belonging to certain ethnic groups such as Arabs, Turks, Hindus, inhabitants of the Iberian Peninsula, and their respective descendants. In these ethnicities, the condition tends to appear earlier, often during childhood. In those individuals there is no change in the color of the skin; the eyelid appears darkened because the dilated vessels are visible due to the transparency of the skin. The problem is often aggravated when the lower eyelid's vessels are more dilated (for example from fatigue, insomnia, oral breathing, or crying), causing dermal blood extravasation. The local liberation of ferric ions entails the formation of free radicals that stimulate the melanocytes, generating melanic pigmentation.

Other causes noted as responsible for dark eye circles include post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation secondary to atopic and contact dermatitis, sleep deprivation, oral breathing, alcoholism, smoking, use of certain medications (contraceptives, chemotherapy, antipsychotics, and some types of eye drops), palpebral sagging due to aging, and disorders that develop with hydric retention and palpebral edema (thyroid disorders, nephropathies, cardiopathies, and pneumopathies).

The anatomy of the palpebral region helps explain the condition: palpebral skin is the thinnest in the human body (less than 1 mm), with a very thin epidermis (0.4 mm) and an extremely thin dermis of loose conjunctive tissue. The thinness of the skin, combined with the lack of fatty tissue, gives the region its characteristic translucency, so that accumulation of melanin and/or vessel dilatation can be easily seen as bilateral homogeneous hyperpigmentation. The color of the palpebral skin results from a combination of genetic-racial factors (such as the amount of melanin pigment) and individual, regional, or even gender-related factors, such as the thickness of the various components and the blood volume in their vessels.

Various treatments have been proposed for periorbital hyperchromia, though there are few studies on their long-term efficacy. The main types of treatment are: topical application of depigmenting products, chemical peelings, dermabrasion, cryosurgery, fillings with hyaluronic acid, intense pulsed light, and CO2, argon, ruby, and excimer lasers.

**Individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes are most affected by periorbital hyperchromia.**
