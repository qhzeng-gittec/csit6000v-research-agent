Periorbital hyperpigmentation, also called peri-palpebral hyperpigmentation, dark eyelids, dark eye circles, dark circles, or simply under-eye circles, is described in the document as "a mere color difference between the palpebral skin and the remaining facial skin," yet it "makes people look tired or older, which negatively affects their quality of life." It has a higher prevalence in individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes, and "affects age groups and genders equally," although "there are a higher number of complaints from women, especially senior women." Few studies exist on its etiology, but "dark eye circles with a vascular component are known to present a dominant autoso- mal family inheritance pattern."

The condition seems to have multifactorial causes involving intrinsic factors "determined by the individual's genetics" and extrinsic factors such as "sun exposure, smoking, alcoholism and sleep deprivation." A distinctive feature in its etiopathogeny is "the presence of melanic pigment and hemosiderotic pigment in the affected sites." Melanic hyperpigmentation is more frequent in brunet adults, "as a consequence of excessive and cumulative exposure to the sun, which increases the production of melanin, reduces the skin's thickness and increases the dilatation of blood vessels."

Intense vascularization is mainly found in people belonging to certain ethnic groups "such as Arabs, Turks, Hindus, inhab- itants of the Iberian Peninsula and their respective descendants." In these ethnicities, the manifestation "tends to take place earlier, often during childhood." There is no change in skin color; "the eyelid appears darkened because the dilated vessels are visible due to the transparency of the skin." The problem is often aggravated when the lower eyelid's vessels are more dilated (e.g., from fatigue, insomnia, oral breathing, crying), causing dermal blood extravasation. "The liberation of ferric ions takes place locally, entailing the for- mation of free radicals that stimulate the melanocytes, which generates melanic pigmentation."

Other causes noted include "post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation secondary to atopic and contact dermatitis, sleep deprivation, oral breathing, alcoholism, smoking, use of certain medications (contraceptives, chemotherapy, antipsychotic and some types of eye drops), the presence of palpebral sagging (due to aging) and of disorders that develop with hydric retention and palpebral edema (thyroid disorders, nephropathies, cardiopathies and pneumopathies)."

Anatomically, palpebral skin is "the thinnest in the human body (< 1 mm)," with an epidermis of only 0.4 mm. The palpebral dermis is "extremely thin," and "the thinness of the skin, combined with the lack of fatty tissue, gives that region its characteristic translucency," so "the accu- mulation of melanin and/or vessel dilatation in that region can be easily seen, through transparency, as bilateral homogeneous hyperpigmentation." The skin's color results from a combination of genetic-racial factors (such as melanin amount) and individual, regional, or gender factors, "such as the thickness of the several components and the blood volume in their vessels."

Regarding treatment, "Various treatments have been proposed for periorbital hyperchromia, however there are few studies on their long-term efficacy." The main types are "topical application of depigmenting products, chemical peelings, dermabrasion, cryosurgery, fillings with hyaluronic acid, intense pulsed light, CO2, argon, ruby and excimer lasers."

**Individuals with darker skin, hair and eyes are most affected by it.**
