## Propranolol
**Pros**
- Continuous infusion (1–3 mg/hr) can prevent tachycardia and hypertension after noncardiac surgery in patients with cardiac disease.
- Lower incidence of hypotension than esmolol (6% vs. 36%) at equal therapeutic endpoints.

**Cons**
- Nonselective: equal affinity for β1- and β2-receptors; no ISA; no α-activity.
- Most lipidsoluble β-blocker → generally the most central nervous system side effects.
- Very high first-pass liver metabolism (90%), requiring much higher oral doses than IV doses.
- Continuous infusion must be used cautiously because of potential cumulative effects.
- In a comparative study, 50% of patients treated with propranolol developed clinically significant bronchospasm (whereas esmolol and placebo did not change airway resistance).

## Metoprolol
**Pros**
- First clinically used cardioselective β-blocker; β1-affinity is 30 times higher than β2-affinity.
- Low protein binding (<10%); only 3% excreted renally; serum half-life 3–4 hours.

**Cons**
- 50% of the drug is lost to first-pass hepatic metabolism.
- As with any cardioselective β-blocker, higher serum levels may produce greater β2-blocking effects.
- Potency is only about one-half that of propranolol (max effect at 0.2 mg/kg IV).

## Esmolol
**Pros**
- Very short 9-minute half-life (rapid hydrolysis by red blood cell esterases); effects completely reversed ~30 minutes after stopping the infusion.
- Rapid onset: steady-state concentrations within 5 minutes with a loading dose (30 minutes without).
- Cardioselective (primarily β1); lacks ISA and membrane-stabilizing effects.
- Produced significant reductions in BP, HR, and cardiac index in patients with coronary artery disease.
- Appears safe in patients with bronchospastic disease (no change in airway resistance, unlike propranolol).

**Cons**
- Hypotension is a common side effect (36% incidence vs. 6% with propranolol); cardioselective drugs may cause more hypotension due to β1-induced myocardial depression and failure to block β2 peripheral vasodilation.
- Initial therapy during anesthesia may require significant reductions in both loading and infusion doses.

## Labetalol
**Pros**
- Combined selective α1-blockade plus nonselective β1/β2-blockade (β-blockade 5–10× more potent than α1-blockade), with partial β2-agonist effects promoting vasodilation.
- Acts as a peripheral vasodilator without causing reflex tachycardia; BP and systolic vascular resistance decrease while stroke volume and cardiac output remain unchanged.
- Rapid, dose-related BP reduction: acutely hypertensive patients usually respond within 3–5 minutes of a 100–250 μg/kg bolus; effect may last up to 6 hours.
- Completely absorbed after oral administration.

**Cons**
- Significant first-pass hepatic metabolism (to inactive metabolites); minimal renal excretion of unchanged drug; elimination half-life ~6 hours.
- In more critically ill or anesthetized patients, BP must be carefully titrated with small (5–10 mg) IV increments.
