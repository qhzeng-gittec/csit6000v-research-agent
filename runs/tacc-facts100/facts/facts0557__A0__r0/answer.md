**User-owned informal financial services (Section 2.2):**
- **Reciprocal arrangements between neighbours** — e.g., *kuri kalyanam* among Moslems in Kerala State, southern India (feast invitations with cash gifts that are later returned doubled), and a kind of *hui* in Vietnam involving pooling of resources.
- **Savings clubs** — e.g., an accumulating savings club in Mexico, where neighbours place irregular savings with trusted shopkeepers and receive the lump sum back before Christmas (no interest).
- **Rotating savings and credit associations (ROSCAs)** — e.g., "partners" in Jamaica and Britain, *bui* in Vietnam, *njangi* in Cameroon, "merry-go-rounds" among Kikuyu women in Kenya, and rickshaw ROSCAs in Dhaka, Bangladesh, where drivers save daily takings until a rickshaw is bought and allocated by lottery.
- **Insurance funds / popular insurance** — e.g., *iddir* funeral funds in Ethiopia, which pay out for funeral expenses and sometimes medical expenses or losses from fire or theft.
- **Building societies** — e.g., a society of 165 Public Works Department employees in Dhaka, Bangladesh, who save monthly and lend to members to buy land and building materials.
- **Systems of co-operative business finance.**

**Informal financial services for profit (Section 2.3):**
- **Deposit takers (money-guards)** — e.g., the mobile *alajo* in Nigeria, who cycles door-to-door collecting small daily savings and charges a negative interest rate on withdrawals.
- **Money-lenders** — e.g., the "Tamilians" in Cochin, India, who lend to slum dwellers on a daily basis with a fee deducted at source.
- **Pawnbrokers** — e.g., a goldsmithing-shop pawnbroker in Vijayawada, western India, who lends against gold and silver collateral.
- **Trade credit and hire purchase.**

The document also notes (Section 2.5) that informal services generally include **savings facilities, credit for consumption, and funding for predictable but expensive events such as marriages and funerals**.
