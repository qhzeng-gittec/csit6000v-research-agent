The article describes hypertension as one of the greatest contributors to cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related years of life lost globally, alongside tobacco exposure, high body mass index, and high fasting plasma glucose. It notes that hypertension affects more than 4 billion people worldwide, representing a near doubling in the absolute prevalence of hypertension since 1990. In the United States, nearly half of adults (47%) have hypertension, but only about 1 in 4 (24%) have their condition under control.

The article also highlights disparities in hypertension, stroke, and stroke mortality, which exhibit significant sociodemographic (for example, racial) and geographic (for example, rural–urban, county, zip code) variations. Intervention programs aimed at addressing these issues include behavioral modifications that strengthen chronic disease awareness, the use of self-measured blood pressure monitoring, and sodium intake reduction, all of which are evaluated within the collection.

Regarding younger populations, Goulding and colleagues used National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data collected from 2011 through 2018 to provide estimates of the prevalence of high blood pressure among US children aged 8 to 17 years. The authors documented that elevated blood pressure was most prevalent among children who were older, male, or non-Hispanic Black, with factors beyond inequalities in body weight likely contributing to disparities in elevated blood pressure.

The article further connects hypertension to other health conditions. Obesity is described as a major health challenge because it substantially increases the risk of diseases such as hypertension, myocardial infarction, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and dementia, thereby contributing to a decline in both quality of life and life expectancy. Additionally, Salahuddin and colleagues documented zip code variations in infant mortality rates associated with a high prevalence of maternal cardiometabolic high-risk conditions, which included chronic or gestational hypertension, chronic or gestational diabetes, smoking during pregnancy, and prepregnancy obesity, in 2 counties in Texas.

Finally, the article mentions that systematic reviews and meta-analyses within the collection evaluated the associations of various factors with blood pressure or stroke. One meta-analysis by Zhang and colleagues found an L-shaped dose–response relationship between circulating levels of a certain nutrient and the risk of hypertension; however, the pooled results of randomized controlled trials did not show supplementation to be effective in preventing hypertension. Overall, these 20 articles advance our understanding of effective CVD risk management and intervention programs in multiple settings — in the general population and among high-risk groups — with a health equity lens.
