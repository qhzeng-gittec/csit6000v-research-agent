**The underlying problem**
- Lupus is a chronic condition caused by a malfunctioning immune system. Instead of only attacking foreign bodies like bacteria and viruses, the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's own tissues — such as skin, joints, muscles, nerves, kidneys, heart, or lungs — causing ongoing inflammation and pain. Because of this, pregnancy needs careful planning and monitoring.

**Complications that can occur during pregnancy**
The document lists several problems that can arise:
- Flares of lupus symptoms during pregnancy or immediately after delivery (less likely if the condition was stable before conception)
- High blood pressure (hypertension)
- Pre-eclampsia — high blood pressure with excessive protein lost through urine. It occurs in one in five women with lupus and, if left untreated, can endanger the life of both the woman and her baby
- Premature labour
- Increased risk of blood clots in the legs or lungs
- Increased risk of miscarriage
- Increased risk of emergency caesarean section
- Increased risk of excessive bleeding after delivery
- The baby may be born with low birth weight

**Placenta problems and premature birth**
- About one third of women with lupus have antibodies that may cause blood clots and interfere with the proper functioning of the placenta, most likely in the second trimester. The placenta then can't supply the baby with sufficient nourishment, the baby's growth is slowed, and early delivery via caesarean section may be required. Doctors can screen for these (antiphospholipid) antibodies and prescribe a blood thinner, which can help prevent miscarriage in many women.

**Risks to the baby after birth (neonatal lupus)**
- Around one third of women with lupus have antibodies that may cause lupus-like symptoms in their baby once it's born — skin rash, unusual blood count, and rarely heartbeat irregularities. (Symptoms usually resolve by three to six months, and heartbeat irregularities can be treated.)

**Medication issues**
- Some lupus medications, like methotrexate, shouldn't be taken during pregnancy and can be harmful to the baby; some medication can cross the placenta and pose a threat to the baby. Women may need to stop certain medications months before trying to conceive, and doctors may need to change treatment.

**Diagnosis difficulties**
- It can be hard to distinguish a lupus flare from normal pregnancy symptoms, since pregnancy can cause fatigue, fluid build-up in the joints, skin changes (rashes, flushes, darkening), hair loss after childbirth, shortness of breath, and joint pain — all of which can mimic lupus.

**Who should delay pregnancy**
- Women whose lupus is active, women taking medications such as methotrexate, mycophenolate, or cyclophosphamide, women with kidney disease, and women with previous thrombosis or miscarriage are advised to delay pregnancy and discuss plans with their doctor.

The document notes that despite these complications, most women with lupus are able to have a healthy baby — the healthier and more stable the woman is before conception (ideally no active symptoms for at least six months), and the closer she works with her healthcare team (rheumatologist and specialist obstetrician), the better the chances of a healthy pregnancy and baby.
