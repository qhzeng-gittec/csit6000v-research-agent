**Video Game Play and Real-World Violence**

- **Markey and Ferguson (2017):** Countries that consume more video games have lower levels of violent crime than countries devoid of this media.
- **Markey, Markey, and French (2015):** Months when people play violent video games the most tend to be safer than months when they play them less.
- **Beerthuizen, Weijters, and van der Laan (2017):** Even when violent video games like Grand Theft Auto were first released, there tends to be a decrease in violent crimes.
- **Cunningham, Engelstätter, and Ward (2016)** and **Ward (2011):** The above findings have been replicated by psychologists, economists, and sociologists at various universities while considering numerous other variables.
- **Messner (1986):** Violent assaults, rapes, and murders all decrease when people are watching extremely violent television shows.
- **Dahl and DellaVigna (2009)** and **Markey, French, and Markey (2015):** Years in which the most violent films were released saw decreases in violent crime, and crime consistently decreases in the days following the release of popular violent movies.
- **Felson (1994):** Articulated "routine activity theory" — for a violent crime to occur, a perpetrator must be in the same location as the victim, and that location tends to be free of those who would likely prevent the crime (used in the document to explain why violent media exposure may reduce crime).
- **Snider (2014):** Male gamers in the United States spend a total of 468 million hours each month playing video games — time during which at-risk individuals remain at home instead of on the streets.

**Video Game Play and Aggression**

- **Bushman et al. (2016):** Aggressive acts, while similar to violent behaviors like homicides, tend not to cause such extreme physical harm.
- **Krahé and Möller (2004), Anderson and Dill (2000), Greitemeyer (2019), and Anderson et al. (2004):** Used questionnaire items (e.g., believing it is acceptable to say something nasty behind someone's back, feeling unsociable, spreading gossip, or holding conservative views) to measure aggression — items the document notes do not examine real acts of aggression.
- **Bushman and Gibson (2011):** Individuals who played violent video games were more likely to expose others to loud, irritating noises (and scored aggression as the sum of noise intensity and duration).
- **Anderson and Dill (2000):** Participants who played violent video games reported feeling more hostile on a questionnaire (and scored the competitive reaction time task using only the log-transformation of duration, ignoring intensity).
- **Deselms and Altman (2003):** Participants who played violent video games gave longer prison sentences to hypothetical criminals.
- **Yang, Huesmann, and Bushman (2014):** Participants who played violent video games gave hot sauce to people who do not like spicy food.
- **Kühn et al. (2019):** Could not replicate the effects found by the researchers above.
- **Bartholow, Sestir, and Davis (2005):** Scored aggression on the competitive reaction time task as the product of intensity and duration.
- **Carnagey and Anderson (2005):** Scored aggression as the square root of the duration score multiplied by the intensity score.
- **Elson et al. (2014):** Because there are at least 147 different ways researchers have scored the competitive reaction time task, one can make it appear as if video games increase, decrease, or have no effect on aggression even within the same sample.
- **Ferguson (2015b)** and **Hilgard, Engelhardt, and Rouder (2017):** Meta-analyses found that, on average, only 0.4 percent to 4 percent of the variance in minor forms of aggression can be explained by violent video games.
