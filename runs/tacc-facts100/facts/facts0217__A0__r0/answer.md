**Point 6 — The right to information**
- Every child and young person has a right to information about their health and healthcare, in a form they can understand, including information about the choice of health care services available.
- It covers the freedom to seek, receive and impart information and ideas, not only orally but through other means of the child's choice (e.g., play and art).
- The language and format must be appropriate to the child's abilities and level of understanding, and the child must be helped to understand the information and given every opportunity to participate in conversations about their health and care.
- It includes access to information in Te Reo Māori or in the child's own language for those from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds.
- Health professionals must talk directly to the child (as well as to families/whānau), be as open as possible, and give honest information about the condition and treatment outcomes, what will happen before a procedure, and help with coping strategies.
- Timely, accurate information and advance preparation for hospitalisation or surgery help the child retain a sense of control and mastery over the healthcare environment.

**Point 7 — The right to participate in decision-making**
- Every child and young person has a right to participate in decision-making and, as appropriate to their capabilities, to make decisions about their care, to the greatest extent possible in line with their capacities for understanding.
- This includes the right to be involved in decisions about the use, return or disposal of any bodily parts or substances removed, changed or added during health care.
- Children should be offered healthcare choices wherever possible and are always entitled to a second opinion; their questions and ideas should be heard, and if their views cannot be acted on, they are entitled to an explanation.
- Health professionals must be available, trained and committed to communicating with children; effective communication is critical so children can give informed consent, talk with staff, understand who the staff are, and question them.
- Participation includes verbal and nonverbal communication (play, art, other media), with attention to nonverbal cues of children who use this as their main form of communication (infants, very young children, those with disabilities).
- The right extends beyond the individual: it includes supporting children and young people as groups to be involved in consultation on the development, implementation and evaluation of services, policies and strategies affecting them.
- It also covers teaching and research: informed consent must be sought from children, young people and their families/whānau; those who agree may withdraw at any time without giving a reason; the decision not to participate must not alter access to treatment; and ethical oversight by a Human Research Ethics Committee is required for all research projects in child healthcare services.

**Key differences in summary**
- **Scope of the right:** Point 6 is about *receiving* understandable information about one's health and care; Point 7 is about *participating in and making* decisions about one's care.
- **Unique to Point 6:** freedom to seek/receive/impart information, language access (Te Reo Māori/own language), honesty about condition and outcomes, advance preparation for hospitalisation/procedures, and maintaining a sense of control.
- **Unique to Point 7:** decisions about bodily parts/substances, being offered choices, the right to a second opinion, an explanation when views cannot be acted on, informed consent, group participation in consultation on services/policies/strategies, and the rules governing teaching and research (consent, withdrawal, no effect on treatment access, Human Research Ethics Committee oversight).
- **Shared ground:** both emphasize communication appropriate to the child's abilities, including through play, art and other media, and both require health professionals to communicate effectively and directly with children.
