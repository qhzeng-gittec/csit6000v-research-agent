**Amazon's acquisition of Whole Foods Market (2017, ~$13.2 billion)**
- **Integration of offline and online retail:** Amazon gained brick-and-mortar grocery locations it integrated with its online services — Prime members became eligible for discounts and free pickup/delivery of Whole Foods groceries in selected zip codes, and Amazon Hub Lockers are often located inside Whole Foods stores.
- **Increased competitive pressure in grocery retail:** The acquisition may have increased competition. Other retailers responded: Walmart launched online delivery in 2017, Kroger in 2018, and Walmart+ (a membership delivery service with no minimum order) in 2020. Consumers may have benefited, especially from free delivery on orders over $35. The pressure also affected third-party services like Instacart, which stopped delivering groceries from Whole Foods in 2019.
- **Growing dominance in e-commerce:** The deal raised concern about Amazon's dominance — eMarketer estimated Amazon had 38.7% of e-commerce sales in 2020 (vs. 5.3% for Walmart), and over 60% of U.S. online product searches begin on Amazon.
- **Stronger bargaining power and vertical integration:** Through the acquisition, Amazon gained access to additional consumer data, strengthening its bargaining power with suppliers. Combined with vertical integration (its AmazonBasics private label and its own delivery system, with ~$60 billion invested since 2014 and the fourth-largest U.S. package-delivery share in 2019), Amazon may further strengthen its e-commerce position — potentially benefiting consumers through faster delivery, even if it becomes harder for other companies to compete.

**Facebook's acquisition of Instagram (2012, $1 billion)**
- **Antitrust scrutiny:** The FTC closed its 2012 investigation without action, but in December 2020 the FTC sued Facebook, alleging it maintained its monopoly by buying up companies that posed competitive threats (specifically naming Instagram and WhatsApp); a coalition of 46 state attorneys general filed a parallel suit. Zuckerberg's internal email calling Instagram "a large and viable competitor" was used to support the claim that Facebook acquired it to eliminate a potential competitor.
- **Instagram's rapid growth using Facebook's resources:** Instagram grew from ~100 million monthly active users (Feb 2013) to 500 million (June 2016) and 1 billion (June 2018), leveraging Facebook's advertising services and data infrastructure, which were key to its profitability (including paid "influencer" marketing).
- **Counterfactual uncertainty:** It is unclear how successful Instagram would have been without the merger; it may have struggled from resource constraints, as happened with Friendster, which turned down a $30 million Google buyout in 2003, suffered technical problems as it grew, and eventually closed.
- **Complicated market effects:** Evaluating the merger's effect is complicated by market definition in fast-evolving digital markets — at the time, Instagram was described as a photo-sharing app competing with Photobucket and Flickr rather than Facebook, and social networking services are not necessarily substitutes for one another (one report estimates users hold an average of about seven social media accounts).
