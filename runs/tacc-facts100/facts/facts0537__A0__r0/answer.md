1. **State v. Loomis, 371 Wis.2d 235 (2016), cert. denied (2017)** — The defendant, convicted of offenses arising from a drive-by shooting, argued that the sentencing judge's consideration of an evidence-based risk assessment (which indicated a high risk of recidivism) violated his due process rights. The Wisconsin Supreme Court **rejected the argument**, but **imposed conditions on the use of risk assessments**.

2. **State v. Morrill, 2019 WL 3765586 (N.M. App. 2019)** — The defendant argued that automated conclusions from the Roundup and Forensic Toolkit software were inadmissible hearsay not covered by the business record exception. The court **concluded the district court correctly determined the computer-generated evidence was not hearsay**: because the software programs made the relevant assertions "without any intervention or modification by a person using the software," the assertions are not "statements by a person" governed by the hearsay rules.

3. **State v. Pickett, 466 N.J. Super. 270 (App. Div. 2021)** — In a case of first impression on forensic evidentiary technology, the court addressed whether the defendant was entitled to the trade secrets (source code and related documentation) of TrueAllele, a probabilistic genotyping DNA software, to challenge its reliability at a Frye hearing. The court held that **hiding the source code is not the answer; the solution is producing it under a protective order**, which safeguards both the company's intellectual property rights and the defendant's constitutional liberty interest. Without access, the defendant would be "relegated to blindly accepting the company's assertions as to its reliability," and the judge could not make an informed reliability determination in his gatekeeping role.

4. **State v. Saylor, 2019 Ohio 1025 (Ct. App. 2019) (concurring opinion of Froelich, J.)** — The trial court considered the ORAS (recidivism risk) score in sentencing Saylor to 12½ years. The concurring opinion expressed concern about the "algorithmization" of sentencing, noting that "recidivism risk modeling still involves human choices about what characteristics and factors should be assessed, what hierarchy governs their application, and what relative weight should be ascribed to each." Nevertheless, the court **found no basis for reversal**, because the trial court's decision was not clearly and convincingly unsupported by the record, regardless of any weight given to the ORAS score.
