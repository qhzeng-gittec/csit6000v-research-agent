**Minerals/ions in seawater that affect performance**
- **Alkaline earth metal ions, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺** — during electrolysis they form insoluble deposits, which makes seawater hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) challenging.
- **Chloride ions (Cl⁻)** — Cl⁻ has a high affinity for the metal active sites of the catalysts and possesses strong depassivating and penetrating properties.
- The document also notes that seawater's complex composition and reduced ionic mobility diminish the pH dependence of the HER, requiring higher applied voltages to reach the desired efficiency.

**What causes the decrease in performance**
- The insoluble deposits formed by Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ during electrolysis.
- Cl⁻ adsorbing onto the metal active sites of the catalysts, which leads to severe degradation (corrosion) of the catalyst and hinders direct seawater electrolysis for hydrogen production.

**How to prevent performance degradation**
The document describes several strategies:
1. **General strategies** previously developed: selective active site engineering, three-dimensional structure design, and carbon layer protection.
2. **In situ localized alkaline environment** (Guo et al.): enhancing the kinetic processes by generating an in situ localized alkaline environment, achieving excellent seawater catalytic performance at 60°C.
3. **The protective "armor" strategy presented in this work**: introducing a V₂O₃ layer by in situ reduction into a catalyst with low loading of Pt and Ni₃N (Pt-Ni₃N@V₂O₃). The V₂O₃ layer acts as an "armor" during electrolysis, markedly reducing the adsorption of Cl⁻ and alkaline earth cations (Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺) from seawater and preventing corrosion of the active sites on the electrode. As a result, the assembled Pt-Ni₃N@V₂O₃ catalyst exhibits remarkable HER activity in alkalized natural seawater and maintains its performance for at least 500 hours at industrial-grade current density, surpassing other reported electrocatalysts.
