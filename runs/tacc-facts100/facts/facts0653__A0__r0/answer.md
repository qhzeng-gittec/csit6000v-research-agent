**Leonard Kleinrock**
- At MIT, he published the first paper on packet switching theory in July 1961 and the first book on the subject in 1964.
- He convinced Roberts of the theoretical feasibility of communications using packets rather than circuits, which the document calls "a major step along the path towards computer networking."
- His conviction in the need for packet switching was confirmed by Roberts' 1965 experiment, which showed that the circuit-switched telephone system was totally inadequate for connecting time-shared computers.
- His team at UCLA prepared the network measurement system for the ARPANET.
- Because of his early development of packet switching theory and his focus on analysis, design, and measurement, his Network Measurement Center at UCLA was selected to be the first node on the ARPANET. In September 1969, BBN installed the first IMP at UCLA and connected the first host computer.
- One month later, when SRI was connected, the first host-to-host message was sent from Kleinrock's laboratory to SRI.

**Lawrence G. Roberts**
- An MIT researcher, he was one of those convinced by J.C.R. Licklider (along with Ivan Sutherland and Bob Taylor) of the importance of Licklider's "Galactic Network" concept.
- In 1965, working with Thomas Merrill, he connected the TX-2 computer in Massachusetts to the Q-32 in California with a low-speed dial-up telephone line, creating the first (however small) wide-area computer network ever built. The experiment showed that time-shared computers could work well together, but that the circuit-switched telephone system was inadequate for the job.
- In late 1966 he went to DARPA to develop the computer network concept and quickly put together his plan for the "ARPANET," publishing it in 1967. At the conference where he presented the paper, he learned from Roger Scantlebury about the parallel packet network work at NPL in the UK (by Donald Davies and Scantlebury) and at RAND. The word "packet" was adopted from the NPL work, and the proposed ARPANET line speed was upgraded from 2.4 kbps to 50 kbps.
- After DARPA released an RFQ in August 1968 for the Interface Message Processors (won in December 1968 by Frank Heart's group at BBN), Roberts, working with Howard Frank and his team at Network Analysis Corporation, designed and optimized the network topology and economics.
- In July 1972, Roberts expanded the utility of electronic mail by writing the first email utility program to list, selectively read, file, forward, and respond to messages.

**Together**, their work formed the core of the ARPANET's origin: Kleinrock supplied the packet switching theory and measurement foundation, while Roberts built the first wide-area network experiment, designed the ARPANET plan, and shaped its topology and economics. By the end of 1969, four host computers were connected into the initial ARPANET, and "the budding Internet was off the ground."
