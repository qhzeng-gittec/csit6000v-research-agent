The article describes hypertension as one of the greatest contributors to cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related years of life lost globally, alongside tobacco exposure, high body mass index (BMI), and high fasting plasma glucose. It states that "Hypertension affects more than 4 billion people worldwide, representing a near doubling in the absolute prevalence of hypertension since 1990." In the United States, "nearly half of adults (47%) have hypertension, but only about 1 in 4 (24%) have their condition under control."

The article also links hypertension to obesity, noting that obesity "substantially increases the risk of diseases such as hypertension, myocardial infarction, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and dementia, thereby contributing to a decline in both quality of life and life expectancy."

Regarding disparities, the article notes that "Disparities in hypertension, stroke, and stroke mortality exist, exhibiting significant sociodemographic (eg, racial) and geographic (eg, rural–urban, county, zip code) variations." It further reports that Goulding and colleagues, using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data collected from 2011 through 2018, provided estimates of the prevalence of high blood pressure among US children aged 8 to 17 years. The authors "documented that elevated blood pressure was most prevalent among children who were older, male, or non-Hispanic Black, with factors beyond inequalities in body weight likely contributing to disparities in elevated blood pressure."

The article also mentions that Salahuddin and colleagues documented zip code variations in infant mortality rates associated with a high prevalence of maternal cardiometabolic high-risk conditions, which included "chronic or gestational hypertension, smoking during pregnancy, and prepregnancy obesity" in 2 counties in Texas.

Finally, the article describes intervention programs that are evaluated in the collection, including "behavioral modifications strengthening chronic disease awareness, use of self-measured blood pressure monitoring, and sodium intake reduction." These programs are part of broader efforts to prevent, manage, and control traditional CVD risk factors through timely intervention programs, identifying social determinants of health, examining disparities in CVD risks, and implementing collective efforts through community-based approaches to achieve population-level improvements in cardiovascular health.
