**Violence and crime**
- **Markey and Ferguson 2017**: "Countries that consume more video games have lower levels of violent crime than those devoid of this media" (doc.txt).
- **Markey, Markey, and French 2015**: "Months when people play violent video games the most tend to be safer than months they play them less" (doc.txt).
- **Beerthuizen, Weijters, and van der Laan 2017**: "Even when violent video games, like Grand Theft Auto, were first released, there tends to be a decrease in violent crimes" (doc.txt).
- **Cunningham, Engelstätter, and Ward 2016; Ward 2011**: "These findings have been replicated by psychologists, economists, and sociologists at various universities considering numerous other variables" (doc.txt).
- **Messner 1986**: "violent assaults, rapes, and murders all decrease when people are watching extremely violent television shows" (doc.txt).
- **Dahl and DellaVigna 2009; Markey, French, and Markey 2015**: "years in which the most violent films were released saw decreases in violent crime, and crime consistently decreases in the days following the release of popular violent movies" (doc.txt).
- **Felson 1994**: The document cites this as the source of "routine activity theory," whose "simple notion... is this: For a violent crime to occur, a perpetrator must be in the same location as the victim, and this location tends to be free of those who would likely prevent the crime" (doc.txt).
- **Snider 2014**: "Male gamers in the United States spend a total of 468 million hours each month playing video games" (doc.txt).

**Aggression**
- **Bushman et al. 2016**: "aggressive acts tend not to cause such extreme physical harm" (doc.txt).
- **Krahé and Möller 2004; Anderson and Dill 2000; Greitemeyer 2019; Anderson et al. 2004**: Cited as examples of questionnaires that "attempt to measure aggression by using items that assess whether an individual might be" "jerky," antisocial, gossipy, or "conservative" (doc.txt).
- **Bushman and Gibson 2011**: Found that "individuals who play violent video games are more likely to expose others to loud irritating noises" (doc.txt).
- **Anderson and Dill 2000**: Participants "report feeling more hostile on a questionnaire" (doc.txt); the study also scored aggression as "the log-transformation of duration, ignoring the intensity" (doc.txt).
- **Deselms and Altman 2003**: Participants "give longer prison sentences to hypothetical criminals" (doc.txt).
- **Yang, Huesmann, and Bushman 2014**: Participants "even give hot sauce to people who do not like spicy food" (doc.txt).
- **Kühn et al. 2019**: "many other researchers cannot replicate these effects" (doc.txt).
- **Bartholow, Sestir, and Davis 2005**: Scored aggression as "the product of the intensity and duration" (doc.txt).
- **Carnagey and Anderson 2005**: Scored aggression as "the square root of the duration score multiplied by the intensity score" (doc.txt).
- **Elson et al. 2014**: "one can make it appear as if video games increase aggression, decrease aggression, or have no effect on aggression even within the same sample" (doc.txt).
- **Ferguson 2015b; Hilgard, Engelhardt, and Rouder 2017**: "On average, only 0.4 percent to 4 percent of the variance in minor forms of aggression can be explained by violent video games" (doc.txt).
