## Differences between the two routes

**Traditional publishing**
- You get support from a full team of experts — editors, designers, and salespeople — which often leads to higher sales and a more polished product.
- It is "notoriously slow" and has historically excluded writers from marginalized groups.
- Most major publishers don't accept submissions directly; you need an agent, who sells your work to publishers in return for around 15% of profits. Querying agents is considered one of the hardest parts — writers may receive hundreds of rejections over multiple books.
- Multiple rounds of agent and editor revisions are baked into the process.
- After publication, the publisher's teams handle pricing, negotiating with booksellers, and writing reviews so teachers and librarians know which books to buy.
- You are paid an advance (a lump sum the publisher expects to earn back through sales): small indie publishers might pay $1,000–$2,000; large publishers up to six figures or more, depending on genre, target age group, and career stage. After the advance is "earned out," you earn royalties on every additional copy sold.
- You pay nothing up front. The document warns that if an agent or editor charges "reading fees" or asks you to pay for design or production, you are being scammed.

**Self-publishing** (via platforms like IngramSpark, Amazon Kindle Direct, or Indie Author Project / Biblioboard)
- A quicker way to share your book with readers, with freedom from industry pressure to write something "sellable."
- Books can suffer from lack of professional editing and design; you decide how much to revise (hire a freelance editor, use friends and family, or skip revisions — which can hurt quality).
- Once published, "the tables turn": you become the whole team, doing pricing, formatting, selling, marketing, and distribution. Even for e-publishing only, you must format your manuscript (or pay someone to) and submit to the platform(s), and you must devise a marketing strategy to stand out against professional marketing teams from billion-dollar publishing companies.
- There are no advances; your income depends entirely on how many books you sell.
- You may pay up front for a freelance editor, book designer, or printing. Free e-publishing options exist (Kindle Direct, Indie Author Project / Biblioboard), and print-on-demand (IngramSpark) can save you from paying to print and warehouse unsold copies.

## How to know which route to take

The document says there's "no one right answer" — it depends on your personal goals. It suggests weighing:

1. **How you want to spend your time.** Writing is only one part of an author's career (revising, marketing, and — for traditional — finding an agent). Ask whether you'd rather spend time querying agents or becoming an expert in everything from typesetting to book distribution.
2. **Why you want a writing career.** "Usually, the answer is not money." If you know up front that writing isn't a financial or career goal, you can save yourself a lot of stress and rejection (e.g., 100 query letters or hours of typesetting).
3. **If money is a goal, know how and when you get paid** on each route (advances + royalties vs. pure sales income, as above).

It also flags that **genre, book length, and readers' age** matter a lot:
- **Romance** is a rare genre that tops traditional sales *and* has seen significant income growth for self-published authors; many romance writers are now "hybrid," doing both.
- **Memoirs** are notoriously hard to sell either way unless you're already famous.
- **Niche topics** (e.g., regional history) may have too small an audience for big publishers to profit, making self-publishing the only way to share them.
- **Children's books** tend to struggle with self-publishing, since many self-published books are e-books only, few children have e-readers, and adults (parents, grandparents) buy physical gifts and worry about screen time.
