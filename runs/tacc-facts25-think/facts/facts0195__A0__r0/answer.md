## Ellana's Background in Medicine

- **Credentials and experience:** Ellana Stinson is a board-certified emergency medicine physician who has practiced emergency medicine for 15 years.
- **Steward Health System (2013–2020):** She worked on staff as an emergency medicine physician at Carney Hospital, Good Samaritan, and the former Quincy Hospital, all part of the Steward Health System.
- **Nationwide work:** Over the past 11 years, she has worked intermittently at various facilities across the country run and operated by HCA, Envision, Tenant, Team Health, and CHS.

## Her Concerns About Changes in Emergency Medicine

**Dwindling resources and degraded care:**
- She could no longer care for patients the way she would want a loved one treated, forcing her to decide whether to stay or go.
- At Steward facilities, resources were "dwindled down and pulled from each facility"; most lost certain specialty services, and Quincy Hospital was reduced to "bare bones" before its closure.
- The absence of blood products, respiratory therapy (at times), and certain specialty services made her feel she could no longer provide safe or quality care.

**Private equity's impact:**
- Over 30% of U.S. hospitals have been subject to buyouts, mergers, and acquisitions led by private equity (PE) firms, and at some PE sites the deprivation was even worse.
- Consequences include increased wait times, critical staff shortages, dangerous boarding levels, and critically dangerous patient-to-nurse ratios in the ED—up to 14-to-1 at times.
- The pandemic exacerbated these problems, increasing the cost of care, infection rates, mortality, and even death.
- At PE-led sites, medicine is "no longer about patient safety and quality, but about making medical decisions and judgment due corporate decision making with profit motives at the expense of patients."

**Specific profit-driven practices she criticizes:**
- Forcing staff to see patients in the waiting room to make wait times appear reduced and improve door-to-doc times.
- Calling codes for sepsis and strokes as "innovative ways to make profits."
- Increasingly daunting, nearly unattainable and unsafe metrics imposed on physicians and staff.
- Removing emergency physicians from in-network, causing "surprise billing" and higher bills for patients who have no choice of where to go during chest pain or stroke-like symptoms—especially when the next closest facility can be an hour away.

**Impact on vulnerable communities:**
- Most facilities she worked in served vulnerable populations—mostly people of color, low-income, or with limited access to other facilities or primary care—forcing them to seek care only at PE-backed sites.
- Most buyouts target hospitals already struggling or with higher Medicaid and Medicare populations, worsening care access and quality in communities already harmed by historical injury.
- She warns that the "financialization of medicine will continue to exacerbate health disparities."

**Impact on physicians:**
- For emergency physicians of color (whom she typically encounters at PE-backed facilities), these practices felt unsafe, raising questions of medical-legal risk and the emotional toll on those who entered the profession to do no harm.
- Staffing at PE-backed facilities does not qualify physicians burdened with student debt for loan forgiveness.
- A profession once competitive is now among the least competitive to enter, as students witness "the destruction of the profession"; in 2023, 554 residency spots went unfilled.
- PE-backed hospitals have opened residency programs not adequately equipped to properly train emergency medicine trainees.

She concludes that while emergency physicians "train to deal with hard stuff," they are "running out of options on how to continue providing safe and adequate care at these PE run facilities."
