- **Methylphenidate (MPH)** — a stimulant
  - *Mechanism of action:* Causes an increase in dopamine (DA) signalling through multiple actions, including blockade of the DA reuptake transporter, amplification of DA response duration, disinhibition of the dopamine D2 receptor, and amplification of DA tone. MPH is also an inhibitor of noradrenaline (NE) re-uptake.
  - *Risks:* Appears to cause minor increases in blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR), with no strong data to suggest it increases the QT interval. Little is known about the long-term effects of stimulants on the functional organisation of the developing brain (one year of MPH treatment may be beneficial for enduring normalisation of neural correlates of attention, and chronic use in drug-naive boys enhanced neuropsychological functioning on "recognition memory" tasks with modest executive demands).

- **Atomoxetine (ATX)** — a non-stimulant
  - *Mechanism of action:* A selective inhibitor of synaptic re-uptake; in vivo, it specifically increases extracellular levels of DA in the prefrontal cortex but not in the striatum, probably by modulating cortical synaptic DA uptake via the NE transporter.
  - *Risks:* Limited data suggest it may increase BP and HR in the short term; in the long term it appears to only increase BP. Its effects on the QT interval remain uncertain.

- **Dextroamphetamine** — a stimulant
  - *Mechanism of action:* Increases the synaptic activity of DA and NE by increasing the release of the neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft, decreasing reuptake back into the presynaptic neuron, and inhibiting their catabolism.
  - *Risks:* The document does not list specific risks for dextroamphetamine; it is grouped with MPH as a stimulant with strong evidence of efficacy in improving ADHD symptoms.

- **Guanfacine**
  - *Mechanism of action:* A selective alpha2A adrenergic receptor agonist, which improves working memory by stimulating postsynaptic alpha2A adrenoceptors and strengthening the functional connectivity of prefrontal cortex networks.
  - *Risks:* The document does not list specific risks; it notes guanfacine has been shown to be effective in reducing ADHD symptoms.

**General notes from the document:**
- Strong evidence exists that stimulant medications (MPH and dextroamphetamine) and the non-stimulant ATX are effective in improving ADHD symptoms.
- ADHD pharmacological therapies are generally well-tolerated, but concerns surrounding the cardiovascular safety of some of these drugs have prompted examination of their effects on BP, HR, and ECG parameters; firm conclusions are difficult because the current evidence is based on research not specifically designed to investigate cardiovascular effects.
- Patients receiving pharmacological treatment for ADHD should always be closely monitored for both common and unusual potentially severe adverse effects.
- In Italy, only ATX and immediate-release MPH are currently approved for the treatment of ADHD, and pharmacological treatment can only be initiated after failure of cognitive behavioural therapy over 6 months or longer, with patients first enrolled in the ADHD medication registry.
