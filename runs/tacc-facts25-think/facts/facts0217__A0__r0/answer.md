**Point 6 — The right to information**
- Every child and young person has a right to information about their health and healthcare, in a form they can understand, including information about the choice of health care services available.
- It covers the freedom to seek, receive and impart information and ideas, not only orally but through other means of the child's choice (e.g., play and art).
- The language and format must be appropriate to the child's abilities and level of understanding, and the child must be helped to understand the information and given every opportunity to participate in conversations about their health and care.
- It includes access to information in Te Reo Māori and, for those from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, in their own language.
- Health professionals must talk directly to children and young people (as well as families/whānau), be as open as possible, and give honest information about their condition and treatment outcomes, what will happen before a procedure, and help with coping strategies.
- Timely, accurate information and advance preparation for hospitalisation or surgery help children retain a sense of control and mastery over the healthcare environment.

**Point 7 — The right to participate in decision-making**
- Every child and young person has a right to participate in decision-making and, as appropriate to their capabilities, to make decisions about their care, to the greatest extent possible in line with their capacities for understanding.
- This includes the right to be involved in decisions about the use, return or disposal of any bodily parts or substances removed, changed or added during health care.
- Children should be offered healthcare choices wherever possible and are always entitled to a second opinion; their questions and ideas should be heard, and if their views cannot be acted on, they are entitled to an explanation.
- Health professionals must be available, trained and committed to communicating with children; effective communication is critical so children can give informed consent, talk with staff, understand who the staff are, and question them.
- Participation includes verbal and nonverbal communication (play, art, other media), with attention to nonverbal cues of children who use this as their main form of communication (infants, very young children, those with disabilities).
- The right extends beyond the individual: children and young people as groups should be involved in consultation on the development, implementation and evaluation of services, policies and strategies affecting them.
- Informed consent must be sought before involvement in teaching or research; participants may withdraw at any time without reason, non-participation must not alter access to treatment, and ethical oversight by a Human Research Ethics Committee is required for research in child healthcare services.

**Key differences**
1. **Core right**: Point 6 is about *receiving understandable information* about one's health and care; Point 7 is about *participating in and making decisions* about one's care.
2. **Scope of decisions**: Point 7 uniquely covers decisions about bodily parts/substances, the right to a second opinion, and being offered healthcare choices — none of which appear in Point 6.
3. **Group participation and research**: Only Point 7 extends the right to group consultation on services, policies and strategies, and sets out rules for teaching/research (informed consent, right to withdraw, no effect on treatment, Human Research Ethics Committee oversight).
4. **Language access**: Only Point 6 explicitly mentions access to information in Te Reo Māori and in the child's own language.
5. **Nonverbal communication**: Only Point 7 explicitly addresses nonverbal communication and cues (e.g., for infants and children with disabilities), whereas Point 6 mentions play and art only as means of seeking/receiving/imparting information.
6. **Professional obligations**: Point 6 stresses that professionals talk directly and openly with children and prepare them for procedures; Point 7 stresses that professionals be available, trained and committed to communication so that informed consent is possible.
