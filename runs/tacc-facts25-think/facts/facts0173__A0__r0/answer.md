

Periorbital hyperchromia — also called periorbital hyperpigmentation, peri-palpebral hyperpigmentation, dark eyelids, dark eye circles, dark circles, or simply under-eye circles — is described in the document as a mere color difference between the palpebral (eyelid) skin and the remaining facial skin. Although it is only a color difference, it makes people look tired or older, which negatively affects their quality of life.

**Prevalence and inheritance.** The condition has a higher prevalence in individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes, and it affects age groups and genders equally. Nevertheless, there are more complaints from women, especially senior women. Few studies exist on its etiology, but dark eye circles with a vascular component are known to present a dominant autosomal family inheritance pattern.

**Etiology.** Periorbital hyperpigmentation seems to have multifactorial causes involving intrinsic factors (determined by the individual's genetics) and extrinsic factors such as sun exposure, smoking, alcoholism, and sleep deprivation. A distinctive feature of its etiopathogeny is the presence of melanic pigment and hemosiderotic pigment in the affected sites. Melanic hyperpigmentation is more frequent in brunet adults, as a consequence of excessive and cumulative sun exposure, which increases melanin production, reduces skin thickness, and increases the dilatation of blood vessels. Intense vascularization is mainly found in people belonging to certain ethnic groups — Arabs, Turks, Hindus, inhabitants of the Iberian Peninsula, and their respective descendants. In these ethnicities the condition tends to manifest earlier, often during childhood. In those individuals there is no change in the color of the skin; the eyelid appears darkened because the dilated vessels are visible due to the transparency of the skin. The problem is often aggravated when the lower eyelid's vessels are more dilated (e.g., from fatigue, insomnia, oral breathing, crying), causing dermal blood extravasation. Ferric ions are liberated locally, entailing the formation of free radicals that stimulate the melanocytes, which generates melanic pigmentation.

**Other causes.** Other causes noted as responsible for dark eye circles are post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation secondary to atopic and contact dermatitis, sleep deprivation, oral breathing, alcoholism, smoking, use of certain medications (contraceptives, chemotherapy, antipsychotics, and some types of eye drops), palpebral sagging due to aging, and disorders that develop with hydric retention and palpebral edema (thyroid disorders, nephropathies, cardiopathies, and pneumopathies) — all of which worsen the unattractive appearance of dark eye circles.

**Treatments.** Various treatments have been proposed, but there are few studies on their long-term efficacy. The main types are: topical application of depigmenting products, chemical peelings, dermabrasion, cryosurgery, fillings with hyaluronic acid, intense pulsed light, and CO2, argon, ruby, and excimer lasers.

**Anatomical background.** The eyelids are tegumentary pleats that participate in facial expression and aesthetics, but their main function is to protect the eyeballs through sensorial filtration by the palpebral cilia and the secretions of the Meibomian and lachrymal glands, keeping the cornea hydrated and preventing external trauma and drying. The upper eyelid reaches up to the eyebrow; the lower eyelid extends down to the lower border of the orbit and is delimited by the genian region. The palpebral fissure measures 9–10 mm in adults and is determined by the muscles that open and close the eyelids; the palpebral elevator muscle is assisted by Muller's and frontalis muscles. Aging decreases the fissure's vertical opening due to progressive lowering of the upper eyelid, caused by decreased aponeurosis action of the lifter muscle; the skin becomes flaccid, less elastic, and more prone to wrinkle, and the orbicular and tarsal muscles, orbital septum, and conjunctival mucous membrane also transform in the elderly. A cohort study of 320 patients (aged 10–89) found a correlation between a decrease in the palpebral fissure and increasing age.

**Skin and vascularity.** Palpebral skin is the thinnest in the human body (<1 mm); its epidermis is very thin (0.4 mm) compared with the palmoplantar region (~1.6 mm). The nasal portion has thinner hair and more sebaceous glands than the temporal portion. The dermis is loose conjunctive tissue, extremely thin, and absent in the pre-tarsal skin and medial and lateral ligaments, where skin adheres to underlying fibrous tissue. This thinness, combined with the lack of fatty tissue, gives the region its characteristic translucency, so melanin accumulation and/or vessel dilatation are easily seen as bilateral homogeneous hyperpigmentation. Arterial irrigation comes through the supratrochlear, supraorbital, lachrymal, and dorsum-of-nose arteries (from the facial artery), the angular and transverse arteries (facial artery), the transverse facial artery (superficial temporal artery), and branches of the superficial temporal artery. Venous drainage follows an external pattern through associated veins and an internal pattern penetrating the orbit via ophthalmic veins; lymphatic drainage is mainly through the parotid nodes, with some medial-angle drainage toward the submandibular nodes.

**Skin color.** The palpebral skin's color results from a combination of factors of genetic-racial origin (such as the amount of melanin pigment) and of individual, regional, or even gender origin, such as the thickness of the various components and the blood volume in their vessels.

**Individuals with darker skin, hair, and eyes are most affected by periorbital hyperchromia.**
