

Based on `doc.txt`, chewing gum does not inherently cause tooth decay; in fact, it can be protective, though sugar-containing gum is an exception.

**General protective effect:** Chewing gum stimulates saliva, which helps prevent decay. The document states that "Increasing saliva volume helps to dilute and neutralize acids produced by the bacteria in plaque on teeth," and that "There are several mechanisms by which stimulated saliva flow may protect against dental caries," including that "Increased saliva flow carries with it calcium and phosphate ions, which can contribute to remineralization of tooth enamel."

**Sugar-containing gum can contribute to decay:** The document notes that "These fermentable carbohydrates can be metabolized by oral bacteria. The bacteria (particularly S. mutans and Lactobacillus spp.) in turn produce dental biofilm and acid, which can lead to enamel demineralization and caries." It adds that "The potential cariogenicity of sugar-containing gum depends on the physical consistency, oral retention time of the gum, the frequency with which it is chewed, and the sequence of consumption."

**Sugar-free gum is noncariogenic and reduces decay:** The document reports that "Clinical trials have found decreased caries incidence in subjects who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals," and that "Unlike sugar, these sweeteners are noncariogenic, since they are metabolized slowly or not at all by cariogenic plaque bacteria." A 2021 systematic review also "reported that the use of sugar-free chewing gum resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the S. mutans load."

In short, according to the document, chewing gum generally protects against decay through increased saliva flow; only sugar-containing gum can lead to caries, while sugar-free gum is associated with reduced caries incidence.
