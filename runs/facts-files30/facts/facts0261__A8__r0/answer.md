**Before the federal income tax**
- "The tax mechanisms used during first 150 years or so of U.S. tax history bears little resemblance to the current system of taxation." The Constitution restricted "direct" taxation, so "the federal government relied on indirect taxes including taxes on imports (tariffs) and excise taxes."
- "Tariffs were the major source of U.S. government receipts from the beginning of the nation up to the early 1900's. For example, in 1800 custom duties comprised about 84% of government receipts." In 1900, "over 60% of internal revenue collections came from alcohol excise taxes with another 20% from tobacco excise taxes."
- "The Constitutional framers were wary of a government's power to tax. Taxation of the American Colonies by a distant and corrupt England was a driving force behind the American Revolution." Most revenue collection was delegated to localities, "which relied primarily on property taxes." During wars, such as the War of 1812, "federal taxes were temporarily raised to finance the war or pay down the ensuing debts," then reduced afterward.
- The Civil War "initiated an increase in both excise tax and tariff rates. Government revenue collections increased by a factor of seven between 1863 and 1866." The most significant change was "the institution of the first national income tax," with "a maximum rate of 10% along with generous exemptions" so "only about 10% of households were subject to any income tax." It "generated over 20% of federal revenues in 1865," but "in 1872 it was allowed to expire."
- The Populist movement of the late 1800s revived interest in an income tax "as a vehicle of social justice." "A federal income tax, with a large exemption of $4,000, was instituted in 1894 but the Supreme Court ruled it unconstitutional in 1895." "Eventually, in 1913 the 16th Amendment was ratified creating the legal basis for the federal income tax." Initially it was modest: "in the first few years only about 2% of households paid any income tax" at "tax rates of only 1%-7%."

**The growth of direct taxation**
- World War I transformed the system: "the top marginal rate increased dramatically from 7% in 1915 to 67% in 1917." Corporate taxes became important (over one-quarter of internal revenue collections in 1917), and "in 1916 the estate tax was created."
- Unlike after WWI, "much of the tax system laid down during World War I remained in place after the war. In the period from 1910 to 1925 tariffs fell from about half of government receipts to less than 15%. Meanwhile the new corporate and individual income taxes made up nearly half of government receipts in the mid 1920s."
- The Great Depression caused a decline in receipts; "in 1932 tax rates were increased." A key New Deal measure was old-age insurance: rather than funding Social Security through income taxes, "the funding mechanism was a separate tax, split equally between employers and employees."
- World War II produced another emergency expansion. Roosevelt sought higher taxes on corporations and high incomes, stating that "no American citizen ought to have a net income, after he has paid his taxes, of more than $25,000." The compromise made the tax more progressive and broader: "Personal exemptions were reduced by half between 1939 and 1942 – meaning the income tax reached well into the middle class for the first time," and "the top marginal tax rate reached a record high of 94% in 1944." Withholding from paychecks was introduced. As a result, "the number of individual taxpayers grew from 3.9 million in 1939 to 42.6 million in 1945, and federal income tax collections over the period leaped from $2.2 billion to $35.1 billion."

**Postwar through the 1970s**
- "As in the period after World War I, much of the new tax structure instituted during World War II remained in place after the war." "Changes to the tax system between the end of World War II and the 1980's were generally minor." The Social Security tax rose from an initial 2% to 6.13% by 1979; Medicare and Medicaid were established in the 1960s; and "across-the-board tax cuts in 1964 reduced marginal rates for both low- and high-income households (the top marginal rate fell from 91% in 1963 to 70% in 1965)." Government grew from "less than 18% of GDP in 1946 to over 22% by the mid 1970s."

**The "Reagan Revolution" to the Bush tax cuts**
- "The general stasis of the federal tax system ended in the 1980s." "The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) enacted the largest tax cut in American history" and "phased in a reduction in the top tax rate from 70% to 50%," with supply-side rationale. In practice, "ERTA resulted in the largest reduction in federal revenues of any tax bill since World War II," and "in 1982, the largest peacetime tax increase was passed." Social Security reforms in 1983 "increased Social Security tax rates and initiated taxation of some benefits."
- "The Tax Reform Act of 1986 – considered to be the most comprehensive revision of the tax code since the 1950s" – "reduced top income tax rates even further – from 50% in 1986 to 28% in 1988" and lowered the top corporate rate "from 46% to 34%."
- Despite the cuts, "total federal revenues increased about 76% from 1980-1988 while GDP increased 83%," and "the annual budget deficits of the federal government tripled during the 1980s."
- "The first President Bush reneged on his campaign promise of 'no new taxes' and agreed to a compromise tax proposal in 1990 that raised the top marginal tax bracket to 31%." Clinton "reinstated additional progressivity in 1993 by creating the 36% and 39.6% individual tax brackets," and the corporate rate rose to 35%.
- "The most recent important tax legislation was the $1.35 trillion Bush tax cut passed in 2001," which lowered individual rates across the board, scheduled repeal of the estate tax in 2010, and (for the top bracket) "the top marginal bracket fell from 39.6% in 2001 to 38.6% in 2002 but eventually fell to 35.0% in 2006." It "reduced the overall progressiveness of the federal income tax as high-income taxpayers received a disproportionate share of the total cuts." A smaller cut followed in 2003, and "the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 instituted or expanded various tax credits such as a payroll tax credit of $400 per worker and an expanded tax credit for college tuition."

**Overall trend**
- "Until quite recently, tax collections have tended to increase over time; paralleling the increase in the size of the federal government," with "a large increase in federal tax collections... during World War II, with relatively consistent growth after about 1960" and occasional declines "due either to recessions or to major tax code changes." The document notes this trend "has recently stabilized or reversed," and that the history section is "primarily derived from Brownlee (1996)."
