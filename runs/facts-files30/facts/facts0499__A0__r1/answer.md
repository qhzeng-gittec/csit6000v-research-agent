Based on the document in `doc.txt`, here's what you need to know about VBAC (vaginal birth after cesarean):

## Benefits of VBAC compared to a C-section
- **Faster recovery and shorter hospital stay** (recovering from a C-section typically takes longer)
- **No abdominal surgery**
- **Avoiding or lowering C-section risks** like heavy bleeding and infection
- **Better lung preparation for your baby** — vaginal birth helps clear amniotic fluid from your baby's lungs as it passes through the birth canal, better preparing the baby to breathe oxygen after birth
- **Immune system boost for your baby** as it travels through the birth canal
- **Less risk of complications from repeat surgeries** like scarring or injuries to nearby organs
- **Personal preference** — some people simply want the experience of a vaginal birth

## Risks involved
- **Uterine rupture** — because a C-section leaves a scar on the uterus, the pressure of labor could cause the uterus to rupture along the scar. Although rare, this is a serious complication that can have life-threatening results. The risk is about **0.9% (slightly less than 1 in 100)** if you had a C-section with a transverse (sideways) cut in the lower part of the uterus.
- **Blood loss**
- **Infection**
- **Emergency C-section**, which is riskier than a planned C-section

Note that the type of incision matters: a low transverse incision has the lowest rupture risk, a low vertical incision has a higher risk, and a high vertical ("classical") incision in the upper uterus has the highest risk. The document notes that recent studies show it's possible to have a safe VBAC after a C-section in most cases.

## Success rate
- Up to **75%** of people who had **one** C-section went on to have a successful vaginal birth in their next pregnancy.
- The chance drops to about **50%** if you've had **two or more** prior C-section deliveries.

## Criteria that favor a successful VBAC
Your chances may be **higher** if you:
- Have had previous C-section deliveries (one or two) with **low transverse incisions**
- Have had a **vaginal birth before**
- **Haven't had other uterine surgeries** (such as fibroid removal/myomectomy)
- Have **no history of uterine rupture**
- **Don't have a medical condition** (like fibroids or problems with placenta location) that makes vaginal delivery risky
- **Go into labor on your own** before your due date
- Have already had a successful VBAC without complications (which makes future vaginal deliveries more likely)

Your chances may be **lower** if you:
- Had a prior C-section because labor didn't progress (didn't dilate or efface)
- Are older than 35
- Have obesity or are overweight
- Are giving birth to a large fetus or expecting multiples
- Are past your due date
- Had a baby within the last 18 months (short time between pregnancies)
- Have preeclampsia (providers may prefer a C-section)

## Recommended next steps
The document suggests discussing with your provider: your past pregnancies and deliveries, the reason(s) for your C-section, the safety of VBAC vs. repeat C-section for you and your baby, reasons for/against each option, a plan for inducing labor (or not), whether the facility can manage emergency deliveries, and your plans for more children. It also recommends making sure your provider reviews your medical records (including the location/direction of your uterine incision and reasons for prior C-sections) and reviewing your delivery plan at prenatal visits as your pregnancy progresses.
