

According to `doc.txt`, the answer depends on the type of gum:

**Sugar-containing gum can contribute to decay.** The document states that "Monosaccharides and disaccharides may be used in sugar-containing chewing gum. These fermentable carbohydrates can be metabolized by oral bacteria. The bacteria (particularly S. mutans and Lactobacillus spp.) in turn produce dental biofilm and acid, which can lead to enamel demineralization and caries." It adds that "The potential cariogenicity of sugar-containing gum depends on the physical consistency, oral retention time of the gum, the frequency with which it is chewed, and the sequence of consumption."

**Sugar-free gum is not cariogenic and may reduce decay.** The document says "Unlike sugar, these sweeteners are noncariogenic, since they are metabolized slowly or not at all by cariogenic plaque bacteria," and that "Clinical trials have found decreased caries incidence in subjects who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals." A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis "reported that the use of sugar-free chewing gum resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the S. mutans load."

**Chewing in general stimulates saliva, which protects teeth.** The document explains that "Increasing saliva volume helps to dilute and neutralize acids produced by the bacteria in plaque on teeth. Over time, these acids can damage tooth enamel, potentially resulting in decay," and lists several mechanisms "by which stimulated saliva flow may protect against dental caries," including remineralization of enamel, buffering of acids, and clearing acid by swallowing excess saliva.

So the document does not say chewing gum causes tooth decay outright: sugar-containing gum can be cariogenic, while sugar-free gum is described as noncariogenic and associated with decreased caries incidence.
