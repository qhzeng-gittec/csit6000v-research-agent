<answer>
When a sequence of faces is quickly presented in peripheral vision (4–5 faces per second), after a few seconds of staring at a central fixation cross, the faces start to appear deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque, even though they are perfectly normal and unaltered [E1, E2, E3].

The role of peripheral vision:
- Bowden et al. (2019) tested whether the intrinsic blur of peripheral vision — caused by retinal rod cells with poor spatial acuity — makes the faces appear distorted. Although they found some evidence of face distortion in central vision using blurred faces, the effect was much larger when using un-blurred peripheral faces [E4].
- Balas and Pearson (2019) found that peripheral presentation makes a big difference, as does presentation speed; face orientation did not matter, with upside-down faces producing a similar distortion [E5].
- Afraz and Cavanagh (2009) found that peripheral distortion effects are idiosyncratic: the same face can appear differently depending on where it is placed in peripheral vision, stable within an observer but varying substantially between observers; these particularities of peripheral distortion may be responsible for the particular ways flashed faces appear distorted [E6].
