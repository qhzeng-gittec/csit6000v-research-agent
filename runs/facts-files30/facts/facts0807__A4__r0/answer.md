<answer>
When a sequence of faces is quickly flashed in peripheral vision (4–5 faces per second), after a few seconds of staring at a central fixation cross, the faces start to appear deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque, although they are actually perfectly normal and unaltered [E1, E2].

Peripheral vision plays a central role in this illusion. Everything in peripheral vision looks blurry compared to central vision because peripheral stimuli are processed by retinal rod cells with poor spatial acuity, so the intrinsic blur of peripheral vision may make the faces appear distorted [E3]. However, Bowden et al. (2019) found that although blurred centrally presented faces showed some distortion, the effect was much larger with un-blurred peripheral faces [E4]. Balas and Pearson (2019) likewise found that peripheral presentation makes a big difference, as does presentation speed [E5]. Additionally, Afraz and Cavanagh (2009) showed that peripheral distortion effects are idiosyncratic: the same face can appear male in one corner of the periphery and female in another, and these differences are stable within an observer but vary substantially between observers [E6]. Therefore, the particularities of the visual distortions we experience in peripheral vision may be responsible for the particular ways flashed faces appear distorted [E7].
