**Sugar-containing gum can cause tooth decay.** The document explains that monosaccharides and disaccharides in sugar-containing gum are fermentable carbohydrates that oral bacteria (particularly *S. mutans* and *Lactobacillus* spp.) can metabolize, producing dental biofilm and acid that can lead to enamel demineralization and caries. The gum's potential to cause decay depends on factors such as its physical consistency, how long it stays in the mouth, how often it is chewed, and the sequence of consumption (e.g., chewing it before eating foods that reduce acid production is less cariogenic than the reverse).

**Sugar-free gum does not cause decay and may help prevent it.** The sweeteners used in sugar-free gum (such as xylitol, sorbitol, aspartame, sucralose, etc.) are noncariogenic because they are metabolized slowly or not at all by cariogenic plaque bacteria. Clinical trials found decreased caries incidence in people who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals, and a 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis reported that xylitol-sweetened sugar-free gum significantly reduced the *S. mutans* load (effect size −0.42, 95% CI: −0.60 to −0.25).

**How chewing gum generally affects decay:** Chewing gum stimulates salivary flow (10–12 times the unstimulated rate for plain gum base, even more for sweetened/flavored gum). Increased saliva helps dilute and neutralize acids produced by plaque bacteria, buffers acids, carries calcium and phosphate ions that contribute to enamel remineralization, and (if fluoride is present) helps form more caries-resistant fluorapatite. So the act of chewing itself is protective; it is the sugar content that makes gum cariogenic.

In short: according to the document, chewing gum does not inherently cause tooth decay—sugar-containing gum can, while sugar-free gum is noncariogenic and may actually reduce caries risk.
