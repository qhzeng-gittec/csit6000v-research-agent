## What the Glass-Steagall Act is

- It was passed under FDR as a response to the 1929 stock market crash, and it "effected a wall between commercial banking and investment banking."
- Its origins trace to the Pecora Commission, which investigated the crash and identified problems such as risky securities investments that endangered bank deposits, unsound loans to companies in which banks were invested, conflicts of interest, a blurring of insured and uninsured practices, and abusive joint-purchase requirements. Congress addressed these with the Banking Act of 1933 and other legislation.
- Importantly, the Act was **not** intended to limit the size or volume of banking activities — only the scope of activities a single organization could combine. The document's example: J.P. Morgan & Co. could no longer conduct all the same activities within one organization, but the same activities (type and volume) were still permitted if carried out separately through JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley.
- It was **partially** repealed in November 1999, when President Clinton signed the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA), which repealed Sections 20 and 32 (the provisions prohibiting the interlocking of commercial and investment activities). This allowed "universal banking" — combining commercial and investment banking under one roof. Sections 16 and 21 were left intact and still have practical effects today (e.g., limiting investment management firms like Bridgewater Associates from offering checking accounts, and prohibiting commercial banks like Wells Fargo from dealing in risky securities such as cattle futures).

## Pros of the repeal (arguments in its favor)

- Many experts view GLBA as "ratifying, rather than revolutionizing" — by the late 1990s the Act had essentially become ineffective, so the repeal simply formalized a change already underway.
- It enabled universal banking, combining commercial and investment services under one roof.
- Ironically, the repeal allowed the rescue of large institutions after the 2008 crisis: JPMorgan Chase rescued Bear Stearns and Bank of America rescued Merrill Lynch — both would have been impermissible before the 1999 repeal.
- On balance, the document concludes the evidence does not support the view that Glass-Steagall's absence was a cause of the financial crisis.

## Cons of the repeal (arguments against it)

- The 1999 repeal "set in motion the wave of mega-mergers that created huge financial conglomerates, many of which fall firmly within the 'too big to fail' (TBTF) camp" — institutions so large, interconnected, and important that their failure would be disastrous to the economy, with costs absorbed by government and taxpayers.
- After the repeal, universal banks may have lowered underwriting standards to gain initial market share in the securities market, and may have self-dealt, favoring their own interests over customers' — incentives that could have led to or exacerbated the decline in underwriting standards that fueled the housing boom and collapse.
- Had Glass-Steagall been in place, these universal banking institutions would not have been created, so its absence "could have aggravated" the situation.
- The document notes that "the underlying culture of excessive risk-taking and short-term profit was real."

## Important caveats the document raises

The document also stresses limits on blaming the repeal: Glass-Steagall was never intended to regulate loan qualification standards; it did not prevent non-depositories from extending mortgages that competed with commercial banks, nor investment banks from securitizing mortgages and selling them to institutional investors, nor did it address the incentives of the institutions that originated or sold mortgage-related securities. So while its absence may have worsened underwriting standards, it is "unlikely" the Act could have prevented the decline in mortgage underwriting standards that led to the 2000s housing boom. The general consensus, per the document, is that Glass-Steagall's absence was **not** a principal cause of the 2008 crisis.
