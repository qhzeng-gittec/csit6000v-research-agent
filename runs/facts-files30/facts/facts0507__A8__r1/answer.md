

Based on `doc.txt`, the answer is nuanced: chewing gum itself is not described as causing decay; rather, the type of gum matters.

**Sugar-containing gum can contribute to decay.** The document states that "Monosaccharides and disaccharides may be used in sugar-containing chewing gum. These fermentable carbohydrates can be metabolized by oral bacteria. The bacteria (particularly S. mutans and Lactobacillus spp.) in turn produce dental biofilm and acid, which can lead to enamel demineralization and caries." It adds that "The potential cariogenicity of sugar-containing gum depends on the physical consistency, oral retention time of the gum, the frequency with which it is chewed, and the sequence of consumption."

**Chewing gum in general tends to be protective.** The document explains that "Chewing gum after a meal can increase salivary flow by stimulating both mechanical and taste receptors in the mouth," and that "Increasing saliva volume helps to dilute and neutralize acids produced by the bacteria in plaque on teeth. Over time, these acids can damage tooth enamel, potentially resulting in decay." It lists "several mechanisms by which stimulated saliva flow may protect against dental caries," including remineralization of enamel, buffering acids, and clearing acid by swallowing excess saliva.

**Sugar-free gum reduces decay.** The document notes that "Clinical trials have found decreased caries incidence in subjects who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals," and that "Unlike sugar, these sweeteners are noncariogenic, since they are metabolized slowly or not at all by cariogenic plaque bacteria." It also cites a 2021 systematic review finding that sugar-free xylitol gum "resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the S. mutans load."

So according to the document, sugar-containing gum can lead to caries, while chewing gum (especially sugar-free gum after meals) is associated with protection against decay rather than causing it.
