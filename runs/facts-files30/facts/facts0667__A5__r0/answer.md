<answer>
**What monkeypox is:** Monkeypox, now called mpox, is a disease caused by infection with the Monkeypox virus, which is in the same virus family as the smallpox virus; it is not related to chickenpox [E1]. It is a zoonotic disease, meaning it can spread between animals and people, and it is endemic (found regularly) in parts of Central and West Africa, where the virus has been found in small rodents, monkeys, and other mammals [E2].

**Is it currently spreading:** The document says that in 2022 mpox spread around the world, and that clade I of the virus "is responsible for the current rise of cases in Central and Eastern Africa" [E3, E4]. So according to the document, there is a current rise in cases in Central and Eastern Africa, following the 2022 global spread [E3, E4].

**Direct effects:** People with mpox often get a rash, along with other symptoms; the rash goes through several stages, including scabs, before healing [E1]. The virus can spread through close contact (including intimate contact) with a person with mpox, direct contact with contaminated materials, and direct contact with infected animals [E6].

**How dangerous it is:** Although cases of mpox are not life-threatening, some people may be more likely to get severely ill, including people with severely weakened immune systems, children younger than 1, people with a history of eczema, and people who are pregnant [E7]. Historically, clade I caused higher numbers of severe illnesses than clade II, with up to 10% of people dying from it, while recent outbreaks have seen much lower death rates of about 1–3.3% [E4]. Clade II infections are generally less severe, with more than 99.9% of people surviving [E5].

**Ways to prevent it:** The document lists several ways to protect yourself and others: getting vaccinated (check with your healthcare provider whether the mpox vaccine is recommended for you); avoiding close, skin-to-skin contact with people who have a rash that looks like mpox; avoiding contact with materials a person with mpox has used, including sharing eating utensils and cups, and handling their bedding or clothing; if you do get mpox, isolating at home and covering lesions and wearing a mask if you must be around others; and, if you are in an area of Western or Central Africa where mpox occurs regularly, avoiding contact with live or dead wild animals [E8].
