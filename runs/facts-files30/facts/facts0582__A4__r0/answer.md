**Is there anything that can permanently help?**
Based only on doc.txt, no treatment is guaranteed to be permanent. Surgery "can be an effective treatment for many people with challenging pain," but "surgeons cannot guarantee that symptoms will disappear after surgery" [E14]. Some people may continue to experience pain after the recovery period, and in some cases the pain may worsen over time [E15]. In around 5% of people, the problematic disc may rupture again, causing symptoms to recur [E13]. That said, the document notes that people who undergo surgery earlier rather than later may have a faster recovery and improved long-term health [E16], and that in many cases nonsurgical treatment (pain medication [E21], NSAIDs and physical therapy [E22], or selective nerve root blocks [E23]) offers effective pain relief, with surgery recommended if there is no improvement [E20].

**Surgical options and their pros/cons (as described in the document):**

1. **Open discectomy** – The surgeon performs open surgery to remove the herniated section of the disc [E2]. The document gives no specific pros/cons beyond the general purpose of easing pressure on the nerve [E1] and the general discectomy risks below.

2. **Endoscopic spine surgery** – Uses a long thin tube (endoscope) to remove the herniated section; minimally invasive with a tiny incision, only a small scar, and a quicker recovery [E3].

3. **Surgery on the core of the spinal disc** – Instruments access the disc core and a vacuum removes it, making the disc smaller and reducing pressure on the nerve [E4]. Limitation: it is only possible if the outer layer of the disc is not damaged [E4].

4. **Laminotomy or laminectomy** – Removal of part (laminotomy) or all (laminectomy) of the lamina, the part of the vertebrae covering the spinal canal, followed by a discectomy [E5]. Lumbar procedures help relieve leg or sciatic pain from a lower-back disc; cervical procedures help relieve neck and upper-limb pain [E6].

5. **Spinal fusion (SF)** – May be necessary after a laminotomy/laminectomy to stabilize the spine; it joins two bones together with screws [E7]. Con: people who have had an SF may experience pain and feel the treatment restricts certain movements [E8]. Typically, lumbar laminotomies require an SF [E9], and cervical laminotomies require one if operated from the front of the neck but rarely if from the back.

6. **Artificial disc surgery (ADS)** – An alternative to spinal fusion in which the damaged disc is replaced with an artificial one [E10]. Pro: it is usually associated with less pain and less restricted movement compared with spinal fusion [E11].

**Shared risks and recovery:** Discectomies hardly ever result in complications, but rare ones include bleeding, infections, tears in the spine's protective lining, and nerve injury [E12]. Most people can go home within 24 hours of the operation, should avoid driving, long sitting, heavy lifting, and bending for about 4 weeks [E17], and may benefit from a rehabilitation program for a shorter recovery and better mobility [E18]. The type of surgery chosen depends on the disc's location, the severity of pain, and the disability it causes [E19].
