**What the Glass-Steagall Act is**

The Glass-Steagall Act was passed under FDR as a response to the stock market crash of 1929, and it "effected a wall between commercial banking and investment banking" [E1]. It arose from the Pecora Commission's investigation of the crash, which identified problems such as "risky securities investments that endangered bank deposits, unsound loans made to companies in which banks were invested, and conflicts of interest," plus "a blurring of the distinction between uninsured and insured practices, or an abusive practice of requiring joint purchases of multiple products"; Congress addressed these with the Banking Act of 1933 and other legislation [E2].

Importantly, the Act limited the *scope* of activities but "was not intended to limit the size or volume of such activities" — for example, it stopped J.P. Morgan & Co. from doing all the same activities in one organization, but not from doing them separately through JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley [E3].

**How it was repealed**

By the late 1990s the Act "had essentially become ineffective," and in November 1999 President Clinton signed the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA), which "repealed Sections 20 and 32 of the Glass-Steagall Act, which had prohibited the interlocking of commercial and investment activities," allowing "universal banking, which combines commercial and investment banking services under one roof" [E4]. Many experts saw GLBA as "ratifying, rather than revolutionizing," since it formalized a change already underway [E5]. The repeal was only partial: Sections 16 and 21 remain in force today, still limiting, for example, investment management firms like Bridgewater Associates from offering checking accounts and commercial banks like Wells Fargo from dealing in risky securities such as cattle futures [E5]. The 2010 Dodd-Frank Act, like Glass-Steagall, "attempted to promote financial stability and protect the consumer, but Dodd-Frank did not reinstate the repealed provisions of Glass-Steagall" [E14].

**Pros and cons of the repeal (the debate in the document)**

*Arguments that the repeal was not harmful (pros/defenses of repeal):*
- Those who believe the absence of Glass-Steagall did not cause the 2008 crisis note that "offering mortgages has always been a core business for commercial banks," and that "Glass-Steagall was never intended to address or regulate loan qualification standards" [E6].
- The Act "did not prevent non-depositories from extending mortgages... or from selling these mortgages to investment banks," did not stop securitization, and "did not address the incentives of the institutions that originated mortgages or sold mortgage-related securities," so "it's unlikely the Glass-Steagall Act could have prevented the decline in mortgage underwriting standards that led to the housing boom of the 2000s" [E7].
- Ironically, the repeal "actually allowed for the rescue of many large institutions after the crisis": JPMorgan Chase rescued Bear Stearns and Bank of America rescued Merrill Lynch, which "would have been impermissible prior to the 1999 repeal" [E12].

*Arguments that the repeal was harmful (cons):*
- Others argue the decline in underwriting standards "was in fact partially, or indirectly, caused by the Act's absence," because Glass-Steagall's provisions had addressed "conflicts of interest and other potential abuses of universal banks"; after repeal, universal banks may have lowered underwriting standards to gain securities-market share, and may have "self-deal and favor their own interests over those of their customers" [E8].
- The repeal "set in motion the wave of mega-mergers that created huge financial conglomerates, many of which fall firmly within the TBTF camp" — "too big to fail" institutions so large, interconnected, and important that their failure would be disastrous to the economy, with costs "absorbed by government and taxpayers" [E10, E11].

*The document's overall assessment:*
- The evidence on underwriting standards is "not entirely conclusive," but "it does suggest that Glass-Steagall's absence could have worsened underwriting standards" — the Act "could have aggravated the situation" even if it couldn't have prevented the decline [E9].
- "On balance, therefore, the evidence does not seem to support the view that Glass-Steagall's absence was a cause of the financial crisis" [E12].
- Overall, "the general consensus is that Glass-Steagall's absence was not a principal cause of the crisis, the underlying culture of excessive risk-taking and short-term profit was real" [E13].
