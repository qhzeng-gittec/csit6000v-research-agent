

Based on `doc.txt`, the answer depends on the type of chewing gum:

**Sugar-containing gum can cause decay.** The document explains that monosaccharides and disaccharides in sugar-containing gum are fermentable carbohydrates that oral bacteria (particularly *S. mutans* and *Lactobacillus* spp.) metabolize, producing dental biofilm and acid that "can lead to enamel demineralization and caries." The degree of cariogenicity depends on the gum's physical consistency, how long it stays in the mouth, how often it is chewed, and the sequence of consumption.

**Sugar-free gum appears to reduce decay risk.** Clinical trials cited in the document found "decreased caries incidence in subjects who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals." The sweeteners used (e.g., aspartame, acesulfame-K, saccharin, sucralose, stevia, and sugar alcohols) are noncariogenic because they are metabolized slowly or not at all by cariogenic plaque bacteria. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of xylitol-sweetened sugar-free gum also reported a statistically significant reduction in *S. mutans* load.

**Mechanism behind the benefit:** Chewing gum stimulates salivary flow (10–12 times the unstimulated rate for unsweetened gum base, even more for sweetened/flavored gum). Increased saliva dilutes and neutralizes the acids that plaque bacteria produce on teeth—acids that, over time, damage enamel and cause decay. Stimulated saliva also carries calcium and phosphate ions that help remineralize enamel, buffers acids, and contributes proteins that form a protective enamel pellicle.

So, in short: chewing gum does not inherently cause tooth decay—sugar-containing gum can contribute to it, while sugar-free gum is associated with reduced caries incidence.
