## What the Apgar score is

The article defines it as follows: "The Apgar score is based on five components (skin color, heart rate, reflex irritability, muscle tone, and respiration). Each item is scored from 0 to 2 with a total score of 7–10 as normal and the highest score of 10 representing the optimal condition" (doc.txt). It is "used worldwide as a vitality index for almost every newborn immediately after birth" (doc.txt).

## The study behind the article

The authors "conducted a population-based cohort study using data from Danish national registers" (doc.txt). "2,272,473 live singletons were identified during 1978–2015 from the Danish Medical Birth Registry" (doc.txt), and "The final cohort comprised 2,213,822 births" (doc.txt).

## Significance for health outcomes

**Even "normal" scores carry some risk.** The main finding is that "individuals with even clinically “normal” Apgar score range of 7–9 still had higher risks of overall mental disorders and some specific diagnoses: organic disorders and a series of neurodevelopmental disorders (intellectual disability, pervasive developmental disorders, childhood autism, and ADHD)" (doc.txt). The authors note a "dose-response increasing the overall risk of mental disorders with decreasing Apgar score of 9 toward 7" (doc.txt).

**Lower scores carry greater risk.** "Compromised Apgar scores were at elevated risks of developing organic disorders and neurotic disorders, which were reported for the first time" (doc.txt). The strongest association was for intellectual disability: the authors "observed 3~5-fold risks of intellectual disability in childhood (until 18 years of age)" (doc.txt), and their findings "further support that compromised 5-min Apgar scores were associated with childhood autism" (doc.txt).

**Early adulthood.** "During early adulthood, compromised 5-min Apgar scores were not found to be associated with mental disorders" (doc.txt), though the authors caution this "may be attributed to incomplete records of Apgar scores during the initial establishment of the Danish Medical Birth Register" (doc.txt), and that "the maximum attained age in our study was only up to 39 years" (doc.txt), so late-onset disorders (e.g., dementia) could not be detected.

**Context on current guidelines.** "Current guidelines recommend Apgar scores of 7 or higher to be reassuring" (doc.txt), but "recent studies showed that even reassuring Apgar scores of 7–9 are associated with higher risks of neonatal mortality, neonatal morbidity, and adverse long-term neurological outcomes, compared with an Apgar score of 10" (doc.txt). The authors conclude that "5-min Apgar scores routinely available in contemporary neonatal settings, even within the normal range 7–9, are not totally reassuring" (doc.txt).

## Possible explanations offered

The article suggests that "less-than-optimal Apgar scores at birth may be a potential sign of the cumulative effect of those adverse prenatal events" (doc.txt), such as "hypoxia-ischemia, white matter injury, reduced blood flow, malnutrition" (doc.txt), which "exert a long-lasting impact on brain function in later life" (doc.txt). It also notes that "SES factors at least partially mediate the observed association between compromised Apgar scores and neurotic disorder" (doc.txt), since individuals with compromised Apgar scores "tended to be born in families with worse SES" (doc.txt).

One caveat the authors raise: "a distressed infant will receive resuscitation well before the 5-min Apgar score is assigned, so the score 7–9 could not well reflect severe conditions prior to the assessment" (doc.txt).

In short, per this document, the Apgar score is a standard newborn vitality measure, and the study's significance is that even scores in the "normal" 7–9 range are associated with elevated childhood risks of mental and neurodevelopmental disorders, with risk increasing as the score decreases.
