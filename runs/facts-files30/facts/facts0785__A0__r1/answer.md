**The medication: tirzepatide**

The article describes tirzepatide as a diabetes and weight loss drug, marketed as Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes and Zepbound for obesity. Mechanistically, it is a long-acting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. The connection to sleep apnea comes from two new studies, the SURMOUNT-OSA trials, which found that tirzepatide was so effective at reducing sleep disruptions in patients with obesity and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) that 40% to 50% of them no longer needed to use a continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) device.

**Why the connection matters**

The article explains that OSA affects 1 billion people worldwide and 30 million American adults, many of them undiagnosed, and that obesity is a common risk factor: 40% of those with obesity have OSA, and 70% of those with OSA have obesity. CPAP is the most-used and effective intervention for OSA, but many patients refuse to use the device, stop using it, or cannot use it, and as many as half of patients abandon CPAP therapy within 3 years. Because of this, an effective medication to treat obesity is "an obvious avenue to pursue." If tirzepatide eventually gains FDA approval for OSA, it would be the first drug approved for the condition, offering a more accessible alternative for individuals who cannot tolerate or adhere to existing therapies.

**Study design**

The two double-blind randomized controlled trials were conducted at 60 sites in nine countries in patients with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA, defined as more than 15 events per hour on the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) and a BMI of 30 kg/m² or greater. Study 1 enrolled patients not using CPAP; study 2 enrolled those using CPAP. Participants received either the maximum tolerated dose of tirzepatide (10 or 15 mg by once-weekly injection) or placebo for one year, along with lifestyle counseling to reduce food intake by 500 kcal/day and engage in at least 150 minutes per week of physical activity. At baseline, 65% to 70% of participants had severe OSA, with a mean of 51.5 events per hour.

**Benefits observed**

The benefits reported in the article include:

- **Fewer apnea events:** By one year, patients taking tirzepatide had 27 to 30 fewer events per hour compared with only 4 to 6 fewer events per hour for placebo.
- **Reduced need for CPAP:** Up to half of those receiving tirzepatide in both trials had fewer than 5 events per hour, or 5 to 14 AHI events per hour, and an Epworth Sleepiness Scale score of 10 or less — thresholds the authors say represent a level at which CPAP therapy may not be recommended.
- **Weight loss:** Patients lost 18% to 20% of their body weight.
- **Blood pressure:** Systolic blood pressure decreased by 9.7 mm Hg in study 1 and 7.6 mm Hg in study 2 at Week 48. Editorialist Sanjay R. Patel noted this improvement was substantially larger than effects seen with CPAP therapy alone, suggesting tirzepatide may be attractive for patients seeking to reduce cardiovascular risk.
- **Inflammation:** The drug also lowered C-reactive protein levels.
- **Sleep quality:** Patients reported fewer daytime and nighttime disturbances, measured by the PROMIS Short Form scale for Sleep-Related Impairment and Sleep Disturbance.

**Caveats and side effects**

The article also notes important limitations and risks. The most common adverse events were diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, occurring in approximately a quarter of patients, and there were two adjudicated-confirmed cases of acute pancreatitis in study 2. Patel raised concerns about whether patients outside a trial would stick with therapy, given high discontinuation rates of GLP-1 receptor agonists, and about racial disparities in access to these drugs. Finally, Louis Aronne noted it is still unclear whether tirzepatide had an independent effect on OSA or whether the positive results were primarily due to weight loss; he believes the sleep apnea effect is related to weight. He also suggested that treating obesity with tirzepatide plus CPAP is the optimal treatment for OSA and obesity-related cardiometabolic risks.

In short, the article presents tirzepatide as a promising new therapeutic option that addresses both the respiratory and metabolic complications of OSA, largely through substantial weight loss, though it is not yet approved for sleep apnea and carries side effects and access concerns worth discussing with your doctor.
