

When a sequence of faces is "quickly presented in our peripheral vision" (doc.txt), they "start to appear deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque" (doc.txt), even though "upon closer inspection, one can tell the faces are perfectly normal and have not been altered" (doc.txt).

Peripheral vision plays several roles in this effect:

- **Intrinsic blur:** Peripheral vision is blurry because "peripheral stimuli are processed by retinal rod cells that have poor spatial acuity" (doc.txt). Bowden et al. (2019) tested whether this blur explains the distortion: "Although they found some evidence of face distortion in central vision using blurred faces, the effect was much larger when using un-blurred peripheral faces" (doc.txt).

- **Presentation location and speed:** Balas and Pearson (2019) found that "peripheral presentation makes a big difference, as does the presentation speed" (doc.txt), while face orientation did not matter.

- **Idiosyncratic peripheral distortions:** Afraz and Cavanagh (2009) found that "peripheral distortion effects are quite idiosyncratic" (doc.txt): "the very same stimulus can appear differently depending on where it is placed within the observer's peripheral vision" (doc.txt), and these differences are "stable over time for a particular observer but vary substantially between observers" (doc.txt).

Therefore, "the particularities of the visual distortions we experience in peripheral vision may be responsible for the particular ways flashed faces appear distorted" (doc.txt).
