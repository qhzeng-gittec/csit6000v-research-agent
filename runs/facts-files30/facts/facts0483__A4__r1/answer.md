Based only on doc.txt, here is what the document says — and where it does not support the specific claims in your question:

**How the spying works (per the document):** The mechanism is not "tapping into the wifi" in a generic sense, but the abuse of standard local-network discovery protocols. IoT devices expose sensitive data on the local network using standard protocols such as UPnP or mDNS, which "essentially allows nearly any company to learn what devices are in a home, when the user is home, and where the home is" [E1]. These protocols act as side-channels that reach data "supposedly protected by several mobile app permissions such as household locations" [E3]. As one researcher put it, spyware apps and ad companies "abuse local network protocols to silently access such sensitive information without any user awareness. All they have to do is kindly ask for it to other IoT devices deployed in the local network using standard protocols like UPnP" [E4]. In other words, an app that has joined the home network can query the other IoT devices on that network directly, bypassing the consent/permission system of the phone's operating system, because the protocols are "not sufficiently protected" [E6].

**What the document does NOT cover:** The document does not mention browsing history, financial transactions, or health issues at all. The specific sensitive data it names are unique device names, UUIDs, unique hardware addresses, household geolocation, and information about the homeowners' use of their devices [E2, E6]. So the document does not explain how smart devices would spy on those three categories you listed.

**On a connected device reaching all other devices without consent:** The document does support the core of your concern, though it frames it as data access rather than "control." One researcher states: "If a new phone connects to a network, then all the apps on it can have direct access to everything else on that network," and notes that spyware found in apps with tens of millions of installs "was in fact scanning networks and talking to routers" [E5]. The document presents this as a genuine, previously undisclosed security problem: the local protocols are under-protected, the collection happens "in an opaque way," and it makes it easier "to create profiles of our habits or socioeconomic level" [E6, E2]. The document itself is a news report of research rather than an opinion piece, so it reports the threat rather than editorializing, but the quoted researchers clearly treat this as a serious privacy failure — data exposure that occurs "without user awareness" [E2, E4].
