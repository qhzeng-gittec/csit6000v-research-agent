<answer>
When a sequence of faces is quickly presented in peripheral vision (4–5 faces per second), after a few seconds of staring at the central fixation cross, the faces start to appear deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque, but are actually normal and unaltered [E1, E2, E3].

Everything in peripheral vision looks blurry compared to central vision, because peripheral stimuli are processed by retinal rod cells that have poor spatial acuity, so this intrinsic blur could make faces appear distorted [E4]. However, Bowden et al. (2019) found some distortion with blurred central faces, but the effect was much larger with un-blurred peripheral faces [E5]. Balas and Pearson (2019) found that peripheral presentation makes a big difference, as does presentation speed [E6]. Orientation did not matter: upside-down faces produced a similar distortion effect as upright faces [E7]. Afraz and Cavanagh (2009) found peripheral distortion effects are quite idiosyncratic: the same face might appear male in the upper right corner but female in the lower right corner, and these differences are stable over time for a particular observer but vary substantially between observers [E8, E9, E10]. Therefore, the particularities of the visual distortions we experience in peripheral vision may be responsible for the particular ways flashed faces appear distorted [E11].
