According to `doc.txt` (a GDAE teaching module by Brian Roach), the history of U.S. taxation can be summarized as follows:

**Before the federal income tax (first ~150 years).** The early system bore little resemblance to the modern one. The Constitution restricted "direct" federal taxation, so the federal government relied on indirect taxes—tariffs on imports and excise taxes. Tariffs were the major source of government receipts from the founding up to the early 1900s (in 1800, custom duties were about 84% of receipts), and in 1900 over 60% of internal revenue came from alcohol excise taxes and another 20% from tobacco [E1]. The framers, wary of taxing power after the Revolution, decentralized taxation, delegating most revenue collection to localities that relied primarily on property taxes; in peacetime the federal government got by with modest excise taxes and tariffs, and wartime emergencies (e.g., the War of 1812) brought temporary tax increases that were later rolled back [E2].

**Civil War and first income tax.** The Civil War raised excise and tariff rates, and government revenue collections increased sevenfold between 1863 and 1866. The most significant policy was the first national income tax, with a maximum rate of 10% and generous exemptions, so only about 10% of households paid it; it generated over 20% of federal revenues in 1865 but was allowed to expire in 1872 [E3].

**Populism, the 1894 tax, and the 16th Amendment.** The Populist movement of the late 1800s, reacting to a regressive excise-tax system and concentration of wealth, revived the income tax as a vehicle of social justice. A federal income tax with a $4,000 exemption was enacted in 1894 but ruled unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 1895; after failed amendment attempts, the 16th Amendment was ratified in 1913, creating the legal basis for the federal income tax [E4]. The initial tax was progressive but mild—only about 2% of households paid it, at rates of 1%–7% [E5].

**World War I and the growth of direct taxation.** To finance WWI, the Wilson administration transformed the income tax: the top marginal rate rose from 7% in 1915 to 67% in 1917; corporate taxes became a major revenue source (over a quarter of internal revenue in 1917); and the estate tax was created in 1916 [E6]. Unlike after earlier wars, much of this system survived: tariffs fell from about half of government receipts (1910) to under 15% (1925), while corporate and individual income taxes made up nearly half of receipts in the mid-1920s, and excise collections dropped, especially during Prohibition [E7].

**Depression, New Deal, and Social Security.** The Great Depression cut federal receipts; tax rates were raised in 1932, and Roosevelt presented progressive taxation as a key New Deal element. The most significant measure was old-age insurance (Social Security), funded not by income taxes but by a separate tax split equally between employers and employees, a design intended to insulate the program from political attack [E8, E9].

**World War II.** WWII again drove major tax changes: the income tax became more progressive and reached the middle class for the first time (personal exemptions were halved between 1939 and 1942), the income subject to the top rate fell from $5 million (1941) to $200,000 (1942), the top marginal rate hit a record 94% in 1944, and paycheck withholding was introduced [E10]. The number of individual taxpayers grew from 3.9 million in 1939 to 42.6 million in 1945, and federal income tax collections leaped from $2.2 billion to $35.1 billion; by 1950 the federal income tax produced over 51% of all tax collections at all government levels, making the federal government the dominant part of the nation's revenue system [E11].

**Postwar to the 1980s.** Changes were generally minor: the Social Security tax rose from an initial 2% to 6.13% by 1979; Medicare and Medicaid were established in the 1960s; and the 1964 across-the-board tax cuts lowered the top marginal rate from 91% (1963) to 70% (1965) [E12].

**Reagan era through the 2000s.** The 1980s ended the stasis: the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) enacted the largest tax cut in American history (in constant dollars), phasing the top rate down from 70% to 50%, cutting corporate taxes, and indexing many parameters to inflation [E13]. In practice ERTA produced the largest revenue reduction of any post-WWII tax bill, and in 1982 the largest peacetime tax increase was passed; 1983 Social Security reforms raised those tax rates and taxed some benefits; and the Tax Reform Act of 1986 cut the top individual rate from 50% to 28% (1988) and the top corporate rate from 46% to 34% [E14]. With deficits tripling in the 1980s, President Bush (Sr.) broke his "no new taxes" pledge in 1990, raising the top bracket to 31%; Clinton added 36% and 39.6% brackets in 1993 and raised the corporate rate to 35%, increasing progressivity [E15]. The $1.35 trillion Bush tax cut of 2001 lowered individual rates across the board (the top bracket fell from 39.6% to 35.0% by 2006) and scheduled repeal of the estate tax in 2010 [E16]; a smaller 2003 cut accelerated rate decreases and lowered the top capital-gains/dividend rate, and the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act instituted or expanded credits such as a $400-per-worker payroll tax credit [E17].

**Overall trend.** Until quite recently, tax collections have tended to increase over time, paralleling the growth of the federal government; federal revenues grew considerably through the 20th century (in inflation-adjusted terms), with a large jump during WWII and relatively consistent growth after about 1960, punctuated by occasional declines from recessions or major tax-code changes [E18].
