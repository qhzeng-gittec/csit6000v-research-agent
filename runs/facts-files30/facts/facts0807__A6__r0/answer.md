When a sequence of faces is quickly presented in peripheral vision (4–5 faces per second), after a few seconds of staring at the central fixation cross, the faces start to appear "deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque," although they are perfectly normal.

Peripheral vision plays a key role in this illusion. Because peripheral stimuli are processed by retinal rod cells with poor spatial acuity, everything in peripheral vision looks blurry compared to central vision. Bowden et al. (2019) tested whether this intrinsic blur causes the distortion: they found some evidence of face distortion in central vision using blurred faces, but "the effect was much larger when using un-blurred peripheral faces." Balas and Pearson (2019) found that "peripheral presentation makes a big difference, as does the presentation speed," while face orientation did not matter.

Afraz and Cavanagh (2009) showed that peripheral distortion effects are "quite idiosyncratic": the same face can appear male in one corner of peripheral vision but female in another, and these differences are stable for a particular observer but vary substantially between observers. The document suggests that "the particularities of the visual distortions we experience in peripheral vision may be responsible for the particular ways flashed faces appear distorted."
