Mechanistically, the document describes it as "a long-acting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist."

**The connection to sleep apnea.** The link the document draws between tirzepatide and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) runs through obesity. The article states that "Obesity is a common risk factor" for OSA, and that "40% of those with obesity have OSA and 70% of those with OSA have obesity." The evidence comes from the SURMOUNT-OSA studies, described as "two double-blind randomized controlled trials in patients with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA" conducted "at 60 sites in nine countries," with results "presented here at the American Diabetes Association (ADA) 84th Scientific Sessions and simultaneously published online in the New England Journal of Medicine." The headline result: tirzepatide "was so effective at reducing sleep disruptions in patients with obesity and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) that 40% to 50% no longer needed to use a continuous pressure airway positive (CPAP) device."

**Study design.** Participants had moderate-to-severe OSA, "defined as more than 15 events per hour (using the apnea–hypopnea index [AHI]) and a body mass index of 30 kg/m2 or greater." Those not using a CPAP device were enrolled in study 1, and those using one in study 2. Participants "received either the maximum tolerated dose of tirzepatide (10 or 15 mg by once-weekly injection) or placebo for 1 year," along with lifestyle counseling to "reduce food intake by 500 kcal/day and to engage in at least 150 min/week of physical activity." At baseline, "65% to 70% of participants had severe OSA, with more than 30 events/hour on the AHI scale and a mean of 51.5 events/hour."

**Results.** By one year, "patients taking tirzepatide had 27 to 30 fewer events/hour compared with 4 to 6 fewer events/hour for those taking placebo." Moreover, "Up to half of those who received tirzepatide in both trials had less than 5 events/hour or 5 to 14 AHI events/hour and an Epworth Sleepiness Scale score of 10 or less," thresholds that "represent a level at which CPAP therapy may not be recommended."

**Benefits reported in the document:**
- Weight loss: "patients taking the medication lost 18% to 20% of their body weight."
- Inflammation and blood pressure: tirzepatide "lowered C-reactive protein levels and systolic blood pressure," with "a decrease in systolic blood pressure from baseline of 9.7 mm Hg in study 1 and 7.6 mm Hg in study 2 at Week 48."
- Sleep-related quality of life: patients "reported fewer daytime and nighttime disturbances, as measured using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Short Form scale for Sleep-Related Impairment and Sleep Disturbance."
- Cardiovascular risk: Patel wrote that the blood pressure improvement "was substantially larger than effects seen with CPAP therapy alone and indicate that tirzepatide may be an attractive option for those patients who seek to reduce their cardiovascular risk."
- First drug for OSA: "Should tirzepatide eventually gain US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for OSA, it would be the first drug approved for the condition."

**Caveats the document raises.** The "most common adverse events were diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, which occurred in approximately a quarter of patients taking tirzepatide," and "There were two adjudicated-confirmed cases of acute pancreatitis in those taking tirzepatide in study 2." Patel noted "high rates of discontinuation of GLP-1 receptor agonists" and raised concerns about "Racial disparities in the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists." Aronne said it is "still not clear whether tirzepatide had an independent effect in the OSA trial" and "or whether the positive results were primarily due to weight loss," adding, "I believe that over time we'll see that this particular effect in sleep apnea is related to weight." He also said he believes "the treatment of obesity with tirzepatide plus CPAP is really the optimal treatment for obstructive sleep apnea and obesity-related cardiometabolic risks."
