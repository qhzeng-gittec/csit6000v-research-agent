<answer>
According to doc.txt, chewing gum does not inherently cause tooth decay; in fact, the act of chewing is described as protective. Chewing gum increases salivary flow, and the increased saliva helps dilute and neutralize the acids that can otherwise damage enamel and lead to decay [E1]. Stimulated saliva also protects against dental caries through remineralization of enamel (calcium and phosphate ions) and formation of stronger, caries-resistant fluorapatite crystals when fluoride is present [E2].

The one exception is sugar-containing chewing gum: the monosaccharides and disaccharides in it are fermentable carbohydrates that oral bacteria (particularly S. mutans and Lactobacillus spp.) metabolize into acid, which can lead to enamel demineralization and caries [E3]. However, the gum's cariogenic potential is not absolute — it depends on the gum's physical consistency, how long it stays in the mouth, how often it is chewed, and the sequence of consumption [E4].

By contrast, sugar-free chewing gum is described as noncariogenic: its sweeteners are metabolized slowly or not at all by cariogenic plaque bacteria, and clinical trials found decreased caries incidence in subjects who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals [E5]. Sugar-free gum sweetened with xylitol also produced a statistically significant reduction in the S. mutans bacterial load [E6].

So, per the document: the chewing itself does not cause decay and is caries-protective; only sugar-containing gum can contribute to decay, while sugar-free gum is associated with reduced caries.
