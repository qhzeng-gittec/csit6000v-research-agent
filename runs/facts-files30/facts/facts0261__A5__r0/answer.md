According to Section III ("A Brief History of Taxation in the U.S.") of doc.txt, the history of U.S. taxation unfolds as follows:

**Before the federal income tax (founding to early 1900s).** For the first ~150 years, the U.S. tax system bore little resemblance to today's. The Constitution restricted federal "direct" taxes on individuals, so the federal government relied on indirect taxes — tariffs on imports and excise taxes. Tariffs were the major source of government receipts from the nation's founding into the early 1900s (in 1800, customs duties were about 84% of receipts), and internal revenue came mainly from alcohol and tobacco excise taxes (in 1900, over 60% of internal revenue was from alcohol excises, another 20% from tobacco) [E1]. The framers, having been driven to revolution by British taxation, favored decentralized taxation, delegating most revenue collection to localities that relied on property taxes; in peacetime the federal government lived on modest excise taxes and tariffs, and wartime emergencies (e.g., the War of 1812) brought temporary tax increases that were later rolled back [E2].

**Civil War and first income tax.** The Civil War raised excise and tariff rates, and revenue collections grew sevenfold between 1863 and 1866. The most significant policy was the first national income tax: with a 10% maximum rate and generous exemptions, only about 10% of households paid it, yet it generated over 20% of federal revenues in 1865. After the war, few politicians favored keeping it, and it expired in 1872 [E3].

**Populism, the 1894 tax, and the 16th Amendment.** The modern income tax was driven by the late-1800s Populist movement, which saw a progressive, ability-to-pay income tax as a vehicle of social justice against monopoly profits and concentrated wealth, not as a major revenue source. A federal income tax with a $4,000 exemption was enacted in 1894 but ruled unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 1895; after failed amendment attempts, the 16th Amendment was ratified in 1913, creating the legal basis for the federal income tax [E4]. The initial tax was progressive but mild: only about 2% of households paid it, at rates of just 1%–7% [E5].

**World War I and the growth of direct taxation.** To finance WWI, the Wilson administration transformed the income tax: the top marginal rate jumped from 7% in 1915 to 67% in 1917; corporate taxes became a major revenue source (over a quarter of internal collections in 1917); and the estate tax was created in 1916 [E6]. Unlike after earlier wars, much of this system survived: tariffs fell from about half of government receipts in 1910 to under 15% by 1925, while corporate and individual income taxes made up nearly half of receipts in the mid-1920s, and excise collections fell sharply, especially during Prohibition [E7].

**Depression, New Deal, and Social Security.** The Great Depression cut federal receipts; rates were raised in 1932. Roosevelt presented progressive taxation as a key New Deal element, but the most significant measure was old-age insurance (Social Security), funded by a separate tax split equally between employers and employees, with all covered employees contributing and receiving benefits regardless of income [E8, E9].

**World War II.** WWII again required emergency revenues, with Roosevelt seeking higher taxes on corporations and high incomes. The compromise produced a more progressive tax that also reached the middle class for the first time: personal exemptions were halved between 1939 and 1942, the income subject to the top rate fell from $5 million (1941) to $200,000 (1942), and the top marginal rate hit a record 94% in 1944. Payroll withholding was introduced. Individual taxpayers grew from 3.9 million in 1939 to 42.6 million in 1945, and federal income tax collections leaped from $2.2 billion to $35.1 billion; by 1950 the federal income tax produced over 51% of all tax collections at all government levels — the most dramatic shift in U.S. tax policy since 1916 [E10, E11, E12].

**Postwar era (1945s–1970s).** Much of the WWII structure remained; both parties supported a broad progressive income tax, relatively flat corporate rates, and regressive social insurance taxes [E13]. Changes were generally minor: the Social Security tax rate rose from an initial 2% to 6.13% by 1979; Medicare and Medicaid were established in the 1960s; and the 1964 across-the-board tax cuts lowered the top marginal rate from 91% (1963) to 70% (1965) [E14].

**The "Reagan Revolution" (1980s).** The stasis ended with major 1980s reforms. Elected in 1980 on a platform of smaller government and lower taxes, Reagan's Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) enacted the largest tax cut in American history (in constant dollars), phasing the top rate down from 70% to 50%, cutting corporate taxes, and indexing many parameters to inflation [E15, E16]. In practice ERTA produced the largest federal revenue reduction of any post-WWII tax bill, and the deficit kept growing; in 1982 the largest peacetime tax increase was passed, repealing some ERTA provisions and closing corporate loopholes, and 1983 Social Security reforms raised rates and taxed some benefits [E17]. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 — the most comprehensive revision since the 1950s — cut the top individual rate from 50% to 28% (by 1988) and the top corporate rate from 46% to 34% [E18]. Despite the cuts, total federal revenues grew at nearly the pace of the economy (up ~76% from 1980–1988 vs. GDP up 83%); the bigger change was in the revenue mix, with individual and corporate shares falling and social insurance taxes up 38%, reducing overall progressivity [E19].

**1990s–2000s.** Federal deficits tripled in the 1980s; in 1990, the first President Bush broke his "no new taxes" pledge and raised the top bracket to 31%. Clinton's 1993 reforms added 36% and 39.6% brackets and raised the corporate rate to 35%, increasing progressivity [E20]. The $1.35 trillion Bush tax cut of 2001 lowered individual rates across the board, scheduled the estate tax's repeal for 2010, and expanded retirement contributions; it reduced the income tax's progressiveness because high-income taxpayers got a disproportionate share of the cuts [E21, E22]. A smaller 2003 cut accelerated rate decreases and lowered the top capital-gains/dividends rate, and the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act instituted or expanded credits such as a $400-per-worker payroll tax credit and an expanded college tuition credit [E22].

**Overall trend.** Until quite recently, tax collections have tended to increase over time, paralleling the growth of the federal government; federal revenues grew considerably through the 20th century (even in inflation-adjusted terms), with a large jump during WWII and steady growth after about 1960, punctuated by occasional declines from recessions or major tax-code changes [E23].
