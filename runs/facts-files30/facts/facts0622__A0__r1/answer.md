## What the Apgar score is

The Apgar score is a "vitality index" used worldwide for almost every newborn immediately after birth. It is based on five components:

1. Skin color
2. Heart rate
3. Reflex irritability
4. Muscle tone
5. Respiration

Each item is scored from 0 to 2, giving a total score. A total of 7–10 is considered normal, and the highest score of 10 represents the optimal condition. Current guidelines treat scores of 7 or higher as "reassuring."

## What the study found

The article describes a large population-based cohort study using Danish national registers, covering 2,272,473 live singleton births from 1978–2015 (final cohort of 2,213,822 after exclusions). Its main findings:

- **Even "normal" scores of 7–9 carry elevated risk.** During childhood, individuals with Apgar scores in the clinically normal range of 7–9 still had higher risks of overall mental disorders and of specific diagnoses, including organic disorders and a series of neurodevelopmental disorders: intellectual disability, pervasive developmental disorders, childhood autism, and ADHD. The study found a dose-response relationship: the overall risk of mental disorders increased as the Apgar score decreased from 9 toward 7.
- **Compromised (lower) scores carry greater risk.** Compromised 5-minute Apgar scores were associated with elevated risks of organic disorders and neurotic disorders in childhood — a finding reported for the first time in this study. The study observed 3–5-fold risks of intellectual disability in childhood (followed until age 18), and its findings supported that compromised 5-minute Apgar scores were associated with childhood autism.
- **No association in early adulthood.** Compromised 5-minute Apgar scores were not found to be associated with mental disorders during early adulthood (the authors note this may reflect incomplete Apgar records in the early years of the Danish register, and that follow-up only extended to age 39, which is too short to detect late-onset disorders such as dementia).
- **Not fully reassuring.** The authors conclude that 5-minute Apgar scores routinely available in contemporary neonatal settings, even within the normal range of 7–9, are "not totally reassuring," consistent with prior studies linking scores of 7–9 to higher risks of neonatal mortality, neonatal morbidity, and adverse long-term neurological outcomes compared with a score of 10.

## How the authors interpret these findings

- The causes of mental disorders are multifactorial. Adverse prenatal events (e.g., gestational diabetes mellitus, preterm birth, restricted fetal growth) are important risk factors that can have a "programming effect" on fetal brain development.
- A less-than-optimal Apgar score at birth may be a potential sign of the cumulative effect of those adverse prenatal events. In particular, the clinically reassuring but suboptimal range of 7–9 may indicate subtle but still detrimental intrauterine insults that act negatively on fetal brain development.
- The increased risk of organic disorder with low Apgar scores implies that adverse prenatal insults (e.g., hypoxia-ischemia, white matter injury, reduced blood flow, malnutrition) can exert a long-lasting impact on brain function in later life.
- For neurotic disorders, the authors note that individuals with compromised Apgar scores tended to be born into families with worse socioeconomic status (e.g., mothers living alone with low education levels), so socioeconomic factors may at least partially mediate that association.
- One practical caveat: in clinical settings, a distressed infant receives resuscitation well before the 5-minute Apgar score is assigned, so a score of 7–9 may not well reflect severe conditions that existed prior to the assessment.

In short, according to this document, the Apgar score is a standard newborn vitality assessment, and the study's significance is that even scores within the "normal" 7–9 range — and especially lower scores — are associated with increased risks of mental and neurodevelopmental disorders in childhood, suggesting that a less-than-optimal Apgar score may signal subtle prenatal insults affecting brain development.
