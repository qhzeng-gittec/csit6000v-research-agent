

When faces are quickly flashed (4–5 per second) in peripheral vision, they appear deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque, though they are actually normal. Peripheral vision plays a central role in this illusion:

- **Blur hypothesis (Bowden et al., 2019):** Peripheral vision is intrinsically blurry because peripheral stimuli are processed by retinal rod cells with poor spatial acuity. However, when blurred central faces were compared to un-blurred peripheral faces, the distortion effect was much larger for the peripheral faces, so blur alone does not explain the effect.
- **Presentation factors (Balas & Pearson, 2019):** Both peripheral presentation and fast presentation speed make a big difference. Notably, face orientation did not matter—upside-down faces produced similar distortions as upright ones.
- **Idiosyncratic distortions (Afraz & Cavanagh, 2009):** Peripheral distortion effects vary by location within the periphery and between observers. The same neutral face might appear male in one corner of the periphery and female in another. These stable, individual-specific distortions may account for the particular ways flashed faces appear distorted.

Further research is needed to identify the underlying neural mechanisms.
