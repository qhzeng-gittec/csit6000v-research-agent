<answer>
The article in doc.txt describes tirzepatide, a diabetes and weight loss drug marketed as Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes and Zepbound for obesity [E1]. Mechanistically, it is a long-acting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist [E2].

The connection between tirzepatide and sleep apnea is rooted in the strong link between obesity and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The article notes that OSA affects 1 billion people worldwide and 30 million American adults, many of whom are undiagnosed, and that obesity is a common risk factor: 40% of those with obesity have OSA and 70% of those with OSA have obesity [E4]. Because tirzepatide is an effective weight loss medication — patients in the trials lost 18% to 20% of their body weight [E2] — it targets one of the main drivers of OSA.

The evidence comes from two double-blind randomized controlled trials, the SURMOUNT-OSA studies, conducted at 60 sites in nine countries, in patients with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA, defined as more than 15 events per hour on the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) and a body mass index of 30 kg/m2 or greater [E6]. Participants received either the maximum tolerated dose of tirzepatide (10 or 15 mg by once-weekly injection) or placebo for 1 year, along with lifestyle counseling [E7].

The results were striking. By 1 year, patients taking tirzepatide had 27 to 30 fewer events per hour compared with 4 to 6 fewer events per hour for those taking placebo [E8]. The drug was "so effective at reducing sleep disruptions" that 40% to 50% of patients no longer needed to use a CPAP device [E1]. Up to half of those who received tirzepatide in both trials had less than 5 events per hour, or 5 to 14 AHI events per hour, and an Epworth Sleepiness Scale score of 10 or less — thresholds that "represent a level at which CPAP therapy may not be recommended" [E9].

The benefits it may include are:
- Weight loss of 18% to 20% of body weight [E2].
- Reduced sleep disruption: patients reported fewer daytime and nighttime disturbances, as measured by the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Short Form scale for Sleep-Related Impairment and Sleep Disturbance [E12].
- Lowered C-reactive protein levels and systolic blood pressure, with decreases of 9.7 mm Hg in study 1 and 7.6 mm Hg in study 2 at Week 48 [E2, E10].
- Cardiovascular risk reduction: the improvement in systolic blood pressure "was substantially larger than effects seen with CPAP therapy alone and indicate that tirzepatide may be an attractive option for those patients who seek to reduce their cardiovascular risk" [E14].
- An accessible alternative for people who refuse, stop, or cannot use CPAP — as many as half of patients abandon CPAP therapy within 3 years [E5, E13]. If tirzepatide eventually gains FDA approval for OSA, it would be the first drug approved for the condition [E5].

Important caveats: the most common adverse events were diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, occurring in approximately a quarter of patients, and there were two adjudicated-confirmed cases of acute pancreatitis in study 2 [E11]. It is still not clear whether tirzepatide had an independent effect on OSA or whether the positive results were primarily due to weight loss; one expert said, "I believe that over time we'll see that this particular effect in sleep apnea is related to weight" [E15]. An accompanying editorial also raised concerns about long-term adherence and about racial disparities in access to GLP-1 receptor agonists [E13, E17]. Finally, one expert believes the trials demonstrate that "the treatment of obesity with tirzepatide plus CPAP is really the optimal treatment for obstructive sleep apnea and obesity-related cardiometabolic risks" [E16].
</answer>
