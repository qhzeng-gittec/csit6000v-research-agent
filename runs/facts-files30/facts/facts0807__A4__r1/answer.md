<answer>
When a sequence of faces is quickly flashed in peripheral vision (4–5 faces per second), the faces begin to look deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque, even though they are actually perfectly normal and unaltered — the "flashed face distortion effect" [E1, E2].

Peripheral vision plays a central role in this illusion. Because peripheral stimuli are processed by retinal rod cells with poor spatial acuity, everything in peripheral vision looks blurry compared to central vision, so one hypothesis is that this intrinsic blur causes the distortion [E3]. However, Bowden et al. (2019) found that although blurred centrally presented faces produced some distortion, the effect was much larger with un-blurred peripherally presented faces, indicating that blur alone cannot explain the effect [E4]. Balas and Pearson (2019) likewise found that peripheral presentation makes a big difference, as does presentation speed [E5].

Finally, peripheral distortions are idiosyncratic: the same face can appear male in one corner of peripheral vision and female in another, and these differences are stable within an observer but vary between observers [E6]. These individual-specific peripheral distortions may be responsible for the particular ways flashed faces appear distorted [E7].
</answer>
