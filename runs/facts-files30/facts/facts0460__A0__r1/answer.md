

Based on `doc.txt`, changing marijuana from Schedule I to Schedule III would have the following effects:

**1. Prescription/medical use becomes possible in principle**
- Aside from Schedule I substances, controlled substances have accepted medical uses and may be lawfully dispensed by prescription under the Controlled Substances Act (CSA), subject to various restrictions. Rescheduling marijuana to Schedule III would therefore "open the door" for the medical marijuana industry to market their products consistent with federal law.

**2. It would NOT automatically legalize medical marijuana**
- Any such products must still comply with other federal legal requirements, such as the FD&C Act, before they could be legally marketed in the United States.
- Under the FD&C Act, new drugs — including small-molecule drugs synthesized in a laboratory and biologics derived from living organisms — must be approved by the FDA before they can be marketed and sold. A sponsor must submit a new drug application (NDA) with "substantial evidence" that the drug is safe and effective for its proposed use, or a biologics license application (BLA) demonstrating the product is safe, potent, and pure.
- Some chemicals related to or derived from marijuana may be considered small-molecule drugs, while other components of the cannabis plant — including marijuana itself — may be considered a biologic.

**3. Medical research controls would generally loosen, but with a caveat**
- Generally, rescheduling a drug from Schedule I to Schedule III loosens the controls on medical research involving that drug: researchers and manufacturers of Schedule III substances are subject to less-onerous registration requirements than those for Schedule I substances.
- However, medical researchers and drug sponsors of marijuana- or CBD-containing drugs would **not** benefit from these looser restrictions without congressional action. The 2022 Medical Marijuana and Cannabidiol Research Expansion Act (P.L. 117-215) created separate requirements for marijuana researchers and manufacturers to expedite registration with the DEA, and those separate requirements would not be affected by rescheduling without additional congressional action.

**4. Related but distinct substances are not covered**
- Other tetrahydrocannabinols remain Schedule I substances and, because they are scheduled separately from marijuana, they are not included in the DOJ's proposed rule rescheduling marijuana. (The document also notes that FDA-approved cannabis-derived drugs like Epidiolex, Marinol, Syndros, and Cesamet could not be marketed until the DOJ rescheduled them individually — e.g., Epidiolex to Schedule V, dronabinol products to Schedule II or III, and nabilone products to Schedule II.)

In short: rescheduling to Schedule III would permit prescription-based medical use in principle and generally loosen research controls, but it would not by itself legalize medical marijuana (FDA approval under the FD&C Act would still be required), and the special expedited research registration rules for marijuana/CBD would remain in place absent further congressional action.
