Based only on doc.txt, here is what the document says about VBAC (vaginal birth after cesarean):

**Benefits of VBAC compared to a repeat C-section** [E2]:
- Faster recovery and shorter hospital stay.
- No abdominal surgery.
- Avoiding or lowering C-section risks like heavy bleeding and infection.
- Vaginal birth helps clear amniotic fluid from your baby's lungs as it passes through the birth canal, better preparing the baby to breathe oxygen after birth.
- Your baby gets an immune system boost as it travels through the birth canal.
- Less risk of complications from repeat surgeries like scarring or injuries to nearby organs.
- Personal preference — some people want the experience of a vaginal birth.

**Risks** [E3, E4, E5]:
- The main risk is uterine rupture: because a C-section leaves a scar on the uterus, the pressure of labor could cause the uterus to open (rupture) along the C-section scar. Although rare, a uterine rupture is a serious complication that can have life-threatening results, and if you're at high risk for it, your provider will typically not attempt a VBAC.
- The risk of uterine rupture after a C-section with a transverse cut (sideways across the lower part of the uterus) is about 0.9%, or slightly less than 1 in 100.
- Other risks of attempting a VBAC are blood loss, infection, and an emergency C-section, which is riskier than a planned C-section.
- The document also notes that recent studies show it's possible to have a safe VBAC after a C-section in most cases.

**Success rate** [E1]:
- Up to 75% of people who had one C-section went on to have a successful vaginal birth in their next pregnancy.
- The chance drops to about 50% if you've had two or more prior C-section deliveries.

**Criteria that may make VBAC more likely to succeed** [E6]:
- Having had previous C-section deliveries (one or two) with low transverse incisions.
- Having had a vaginal birth before.
- Not having had any other uterine surgeries (such as fibroid removal/myomectomy).
- No history of uterine rupture.
- No medical condition (like fibroids or problems with the location of your placenta) that makes a vaginal delivery risky.
- Going into labor on your own before your due date.
- If you've already had a successful VBAC without complications such as ruptured uterus, you're more likely to have successful future vaginal deliveries.

The document also lists factors that may lower your chances [E7]: a prior C-section due to labor not progressing (not dilating or effacing), being older than 35, obesity or overweight, a large fetus or multiples, being past your due date, having had a baby within the last 18 months, or having preeclampsia (in which case providers may prefer a C-section).

The document also suggests discussing with your provider the reasons you had a C-section, the safety of VBAC versus a repeat C-section, a plan for inducing labor (or not), and whether the healthcare facility can manage emergency deliveries.
