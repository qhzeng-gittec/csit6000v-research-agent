## What the Glass-Steagall Act is

- It "was passed under FDR as a response to the stock market crash of 1929" and "effected a wall between commercial banking and investment banking, only to be partially repealed in 1999" (doc.txt).
- It arose from the Pecora Commission's investigation of the crash's causes, which "identified issues including risky securities investments that endangered bank deposits, unsound loans made to companies in which banks were invested, and conflicts of interest," plus "a blurring of the distinction between uninsured and insured practices, or an abusive practice of requiring joint purchases of multiple products" (doc.txt). Congress addressed these with the Banking Act of 1933 and other legislation.
- Importantly, the Act "was not intended to limit the size or volume of such activities" — it only limited the scope of activities, so the same activities could still be carried out separately through different organizations (doc.txt).
- The repeal: "In November 1999, then-President Bill Clinton signed the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) into effect. GLBA repealed Sections 20 and 32 of the Glass-Steagall Act, which had prohibited the interlocking of commercial and investment activities. The partial repeal allowed for universal banking, which combines commercial and investment banking services under one roof" (doc.txt). Sections 16 and 21 were left intact and "continue to have practical effects on the industry today," e.g., limiting investment management firms from offering checking accounts and prohibiting commercial banks from dealing in risky securities such as cattle futures.

## Pros of the repeal (as presented in the document)

- Many experts view GLBA as "ratifying, rather than revolutionizing" in that "it simply formalized a change that was already ongoing" (doc.txt).
- "Ironically, Glass-Steagall's repeal actually allowed for the rescue of many large institutions after the crisis: After all, JPMorgan Chase rescued Bear Stearns and Bank of America rescued Merrill Lynch, which would have been impermissible prior to the 1999 repeal" (doc.txt).
- On balance, "the evidence does not seem to support the view that Glass-Steagall's absence was a cause of the financial crisis" (doc.txt), and "the general consensus is that Glass-Steagall's absence was not a principal cause of the crisis" (doc.txt).

## Cons of the repeal (as presented in the document)

- The repeal "set in motion the wave of mega-mergers that created huge financial conglomerates, many of which fall firmly within the TBTF camp" (doc.txt). "Too big to fail" (TBTF) institutions "are so large, interconnected, and important that their failure would be disastrous to the greater economic system," and "should they fail, the associated costs are absorbed by government and taxpayers" (doc.txt).
- After the repeal, "it is feasible that universal banks aimed to establish an initial market share in the securities market by lowering underwriting standards. Separately, universal banks might also self-deal and favor their own interests over those of their customers. Both of these incentives could have led to or exacerbated the decline in underwriting standards" (doc.txt).
- The document concludes that "Glass-Steagall's absence could have aggravated the situation" regarding the decline in mortgage underwriting standards, and that "the underlying culture of excessive risk-taking and short-term profit was real" (doc.txt).
