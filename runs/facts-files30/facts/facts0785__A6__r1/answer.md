The article in `doc.txt` is about tirzepatide, described as "The diabetes and weight loss drug tirzepatide (Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes; Zepbound for obesity)," and its headline is "Tirzepatide Significantly Reduces Sleep Disruptions."

**The connection between tirzepatide and sleep apnea**

The link between the medication and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) rests on the strong association between obesity and OSA. The article states that "OSA affects 1 billion people worldwide and 30 million American adults, many of whom are undiagnosed. Obesity is a common risk factor. According to the ADA, 40% of those with obesity have OSA and 70% of those with OSA have obesity." Because tirzepatide produces major weight loss, it targets one of OSA's key risk factors. The drug is "a long-acting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist," and in the studies "patients taking the medication lost 18% to 20% of their body weight." The article notes the mechanism is not fully settled: "It is still not clear whether tirzepatide had an independent effect in the OSA trial — as has been seen in other studies where the drug clearly reduced cardiovascular risk — or whether the positive results were primarily due to weight loss." Louis Aronne, MD, commented, "I believe that over time we'll see that this particular effect in sleep apnea is related to weight."

**The evidence**

The evidence comes from the SURMOUNT-OSA studies, "two double-blind randomized controlled trials in patients with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA" conducted "at 60 sites in nine countries," presented at the American Diabetes Association 84th Scientific Sessions and "simultaneously published online in the New England Journal of Medicine." Patients were enrolled "who had moderate-to-severe OSA, defined as more than 15 events per hour (using the apnea–hypopnea index [AHI]) and a body mass index of 30 kg/m2 or greater." Study 1 enrolled those not using a CPAP device; study 2 enrolled those using one. Participants received "the maximum tolerated dose of tirzepatide (10 or 15 mg by once-weekly injection) or placebo for 1 year," plus lifestyle counseling.

**Benefits reported**

1. Fewer apnea events: "By 1 year, patients taking tirzepatide had 27 to 30 fewer events/hour compared with 4 to 6 fewer events/hour for those taking placebo."
2. Reduced severity: "Up to half of those who received tirzepatide in both trials had less than 5 events/hour or 5 to 14 AHI events/hour and an Epworth Sleepiness Scale score of 10 or less. Those thresholds 'represent a level at which CPAP therapy may not be recommended.'"
3. CPAP independence: the drug "was so effective at reducing sleep disruptions in patients with obesity and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) that 40% to 50% no longer needed to use a continuous pressure airway positive (CPAP) device, according to two new studies."
4. Blood pressure: patients had "a decrease in systolic blood pressure from baseline of 9.7 mm Hg in study 1 and 7.6 mm Hg in study 2 at Week 48." Sanjay R. Patel, MD, wrote this improvement "was substantially larger than effects seen with CPAP therapy alone and indicate that tirzepatide may be an attractive option for those patients who seek to reduce their cardiovascular risk."
5. Inflammation: the drug "also lowered C-reactive protein levels and systolic blood pressure."
6. Sleep quality: "Patients who received tirzepatide also reported fewer daytime and nighttime disturbances, as measured using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Short Form scale for Sleep-Related Impairment and Sleep Disturbance."
7. An alternative for those who cannot use CPAP: "CPAP is an effective and the most-used intervention for OSA, but many patients refuse to use the device, stop using it, or cannot use it." Atul Malhotra, MD, said, "This new drug treatment offers a more accessible alternative for individuals who cannot tolerate or adhere to existing therapies," and "Should tirzepatide eventually gain US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for OSA, it would be the first drug approved for the condition."

**Caveats**

The most common adverse events were "diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, which occurred in approximately a quarter of patients taking tirzepatide," and "There were two adjudicated-confirmed cases of acute pancreatitis in those taking tirzepatide in study 2." Patel raised concerns about adherence, "noting studies have shown high rates of discontinuation of GLP-1 receptor agonists," and about racial disparities in access to GLP-1 receptor agonists. He also observed that "the integration of obesity management into the approaches to care for obstructive sleep apnea has lagged" and that "As many as half of patients abandon CPAP therapy within 3 years." Aronne concluded that "the treatment of obesity with tirzepatide plus CPAP is really the optimal treatment for obstructive sleep apnea and obesity-related cardiometabolic risks."
