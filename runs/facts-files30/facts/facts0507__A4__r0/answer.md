<answer>
According to doc.txt, chewing gum does not inherently cause tooth decay; in fact, chewing gum stimulates salivary flow, and the increased saliva helps dilute and neutralize the acids that can damage enamel and lead to decay [E1], with several mechanisms by which stimulated saliva protects against dental caries [E2].

However, the effect depends on the type of gum:

- Sugar-containing chewing gum can contribute to decay: its fermentable carbohydrates (monosaccharides and disaccharides) can be metabolized by oral bacteria, which produce dental biofilm and acid that can lead to enamel demineralization and caries [E3]. The degree of this cariogenic potential depends on the gum's physical consistency, how long it is retained in the mouth, how often it is chewed, and the sequence of consumption [E4].

- Sugar-free chewing gum does not cause decay: its sweeteners are noncariogenic because they are metabolized slowly or not at all by cariogenic plaque bacteria [E6], and clinical trials found decreased caries incidence in subjects who chewed sugar-free gum for 20 minutes after meals [E5].

So, per the document, only sugar-containing gum has the potential to cause tooth decay, while sugar-free gum is associated with reduced caries.
