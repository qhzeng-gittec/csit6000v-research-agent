Based on `doc.txt` (a GDAE teaching module by Brian Roach, "Taxes in the United States: History, Fairness, and Current Political Issues"), here is the history of U.S. taxation as presented in the document:

**Before the federal income tax**
- The U.S. Constitution restricted "direct" federal taxation (taxes directly on individuals), so the federal government relied on indirect taxes: tariffs on imports and excise taxes. Tariffs were the major source of government receipts from the founding of the nation into the early 1900s — in 1800, customs duties were about 84% of government receipts. As late as 1900, over 60% of internal revenue collections came from alcohol excise taxes and another 20% from tobacco.
- The framers, wary of the power to tax (taxation of the colonies by England had driven the Revolution), favored decentralized taxation, delegating most revenue collection to localities that relied primarily on property taxes. In peacetime, modest excise taxes and tariffs sufficed; in wartime (e.g., the War of 1812), taxes were temporarily raised and then cut after public opposition.
- Government was small relative to the economy: in the late 1800s government expenditures were only about 2% of national output, versus about 20% in 2010.
- The Civil War raised excise and tariff rates (revenue collections rose sevenfold between 1863 and 1866) and produced the first national income tax. With a maximum rate of 10% and generous exemptions, only about 10% of households paid it, yet it generated over 20% of federal revenues in 1865. Few politicians favored keeping it, and it expired in 1872.
- The Populist movement of the late 1800s revived the income tax as a progressive, ability-to-pay vehicle of social justice (a response to monopoly profits and concentrated wealth), not mainly as a revenue source. A federal income tax with a $4,000 exemption was instituted in 1894, but the Supreme Court ruled it unconstitutional in 1895. After failed amendment attempts, the 16th Amendment was ratified in 1913, creating the legal basis for the federal income tax. The initial tax was progressive but mild — only about 2% of households paid it, at rates of just 1%–7%.

**The growth of direct taxation (WWI–WWII)**
- World War I transformed the income tax: the top marginal rate jumped from 7% in 1915 to 67% in 1917; corporate taxes became a major revenue source (over a quarter of internal revenue in 1917); and the estate tax was created in 1916 as an instrument of progressive taxation.
- Unlike after earlier wars, much of the WWI tax system remained. Tariffs fell from about half of government receipts in 1910 to under 15% by 1925, while corporate and individual income taxes made up nearly half of receipts in the mid-1920s. Excise collections dropped sharply, especially during Prohibition.
- The Great Depression cut federal receipts; rates were raised in 1932. FDR made progressive taxation a key element of the New Deal, but the most significant measure was old-age insurance (Social Security), funded by a separate tax split equally between employers and employees (initially 2%, 1% each) — a design that protected it from political attack.
- World War II produced the most dramatic shift. FDR sought to tax corporations and high incomes heavily (he proposed capping net income after taxes at $25,000, but lacked Congressional support). The compromise halved personal exemptions between 1939 and 1942, so the income tax reached the middle class for the first time; the top rate applied to income as low as $200,000 by 1942 (down from $5 million in 1941), and the top marginal rate hit a record 94% in 1944. Payroll withholding was introduced. Individual taxpayers grew from 3.9 million in 1939 to 42.6 million in 1945, and federal income tax collections leaped from $2.2 billion to $35.1 billion. By the end of the war, nearly 90% of the labor force filed returns and about 60% paid income taxes; federal income tax's share of all taxes collected rose from 16% in 1940 to over 51% by 1950.
- After WWII, much of this structure remained, with both parties supporting a progressive but broad income tax, relatively flat corporate rates, and regressive social insurance taxes.

**From the "Reagan Revolution" to the Bush tax cuts**
- Changes between the end of WWII and the 1980s were generally minor: the Social Security tax rose from 2% to 6.13% by 1979; Medicare and Medicaid were established in the 1960s; and the 1964 across-the-board tax cut lowered the top marginal rate from 91% (1963) to 70% (1965). Government spending grew from under 18% of GDP in 1946 to over 22% by the mid-1970s.
- The 1980s ended this stasis. Reagan's Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) enacted the largest tax cut in American history (in constant dollars), on supply-side reasoning; it phased the top rate down from 70% to 50%, cut corporate taxes, and indexed many parameters to inflation. In practice, ERTA produced the largest revenue reduction of any tax bill since WWII, and the 1982 act imposed the largest peacetime tax increase. Social Security reforms in 1983 raised its tax rates and began taxing some benefits. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 — the most comprehensive revision since the 1950s — cut the top individual rate from 50% to 28% (by 1988) and the top corporate rate from 46% to 34%. Despite the cuts, federal revenues grew nearly in line with GDP (revenues +76% vs. GDP +83% from 1980–1988); the mix shifted toward regressive social insurance taxes (up 38% of revenues), reducing overall progressivity.
- Deficits tripled during the 1980s. The first President Bush broke his "no new taxes" pledge in 1990, raising the top bracket to 31%. Clinton restored progressivity in 1993 with 36% and 39.6% brackets and a slight corporate rate increase to 35%.
- The $1.35 trillion Bush tax cut of 2001 lowered individual rates across the board (top bracket from 39.6% in 2001 to 35% by 2006), scheduled repeal of the estate tax in 2010, and expanded retirement contributions; it reduced progressivity. A 2003 cut accelerated rate decreases and lowered the top capital gains/dividends rate. The 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act instituted or expanded tax credits, including a $400 payroll tax credit per worker and an expanded college tuition credit.

**Summary data trends (Section IV of the document)**
- Federal tax revenues grew considerably through the 20th century (even in inflation-adjusted terms), with the largest leap during WWII and relatively consistent growth after about 1960, punctuated by occasional declines from recessions or major tax-code changes. State and local collections grew more steadily, since they rely mainly on property and sales taxes.
- Total tax collections generally rose as a share of GDP over the 20th century; federal revenues now substantially exceed state and local revenues (a trend that began before WWII but was solidified by it). Since 2000, federal taxes as a share of GDP have declined due to recessions and rate cuts — in 2009 they were lower than in any year since the 1940s.
- The composition of federal taxation changed markedly: excise taxes dominated at the start of the century (with a Depression-era revival), corporate taxes were the most significant source from 1918–1932, and personal income taxation has since become central.
