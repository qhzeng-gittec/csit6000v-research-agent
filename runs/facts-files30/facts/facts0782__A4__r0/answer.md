<answer>
What the act is: The Glass-Steagall Act was passed under FDR as a response to the 1929 stock market crash, and it "effected a wall between commercial banking and investment banking" [E1]. It was partially repealed in November 1999, when President Clinton signed the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA), which repealed Sections 20 and 32 of Glass-Steagall — the provisions that had prohibited the interlocking of commercial and investment activities — thereby allowing "universal banking," which combines commercial and investment banking services under one roof [E2]. Notably, the Act was only meant to limit the scope of activities for commercial and investment banks, not the size or volume of those activities [E3].

Pros of the repeal (as presented in the document):
- The repeal allowed for the rescue of large institutions after the 2008 crisis: JPMorgan Chase rescued Bear Stearns and Bank of America rescued Merrill Lynch, which "would have been impermissible prior to the 1999 repeal" [E7].
- The repeal simply formalized a change that was already underway — many experts view GLBA as "ratifying, rather than revolutionizing" [E4].
- On balance, the document concludes that "the evidence does not seem to support the view that Glass-Steagall’s absence was a cause of the financial crisis" [E8], and the general consensus is that its absence was not a principal cause of the crisis [E10].

Cons of the repeal (as presented in the document):
- The repeal "set in motion the wave of mega-mergers that created huge financial conglomerates, many of which fall firmly within the TBTF camp" — i.e., "too big to fail" institutions whose failure would be disastrous to the greater economic system, with costs absorbed by government and taxpayers [E6].
- After the repeal, it is feasible that universal banks lowered underwriting standards to establish market share in the securities market, and might also self-deal and favor their own interests over customers' — incentives that "could have led to or exacerbated the decline in underwriting standards" [E5].
- While the Act likely could not have prevented the decline in mortgage underwriting standards, its absence "could have aggravated the situation" [E9].
- The document also notes that "the underlying culture of excessive risk-taking and short-term profit was real" [E10].
