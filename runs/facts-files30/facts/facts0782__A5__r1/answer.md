What the act is: According to doc.txt, the Glass-Steagall Act was passed under FDR as a response to the 1929 stock market crash, and it "effected a wall between commercial banking and investment banking" [E1]. It arose from the Pecora Commission's investigation of the crash, which identified risky securities investments that endangered bank deposits, unsound loans to companies in which banks were invested, conflicts of interest, a blurring of the distinction between uninsured and insured practices, and the abusive practice of requiring joint purchases of multiple products; Congress addressed these with the Banking Act of 1933 and other legislation [E2]. The act was not intended to limit the size or volume of banking activities, only the scope of activities that could be combined in one organization [E1, E6].

How it was repealed: By the late 1990s the act had essentially become ineffective; in November 1999 President Clinton signed the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA), which repealed Sections 20 and 32 of Glass-Steagall (the provisions prohibiting the interlocking of commercial and investment activities) and thereby allowed "universal banking," combining commercial and investment banking under one roof [E3]. Many experts view GLBA as "ratifying, rather than revolutionizing," since it formalized a change already underway [E4]. The repeal was partial: Sections 16 and 21 remain in place today and still have practical effects, such as limiting investment management firms like Bridgewater Associates from offering checking accounts and prohibiting commercial banks like Wells Fargo from dealing in risky securities such as cattle futures [E4].

Arguments against the repeal (cons of repeal / in favor of the act): Those who argue the absence of Glass-Steagall did cause the 2008 crisis believe the decline in underwriting standards was partially or indirectly caused by the repeal, since Glass-Steagall's provisions had addressed conflicts of interest and other potential abuses of universal banks; after repeal, universal banks may have lowered underwriting standards to gain initial market share in securities, and may have self-dealt, favoring their own interests over customers' [E7]. The repeal also "set in motion the wave of mega-mergers that created huge financial conglomerates, many of which fall firmly within the TBTF [too big to fail] camp," and TBTF institutions' failure would be disastrous to the greater economic system, with costs absorbed by government and taxpayers [E8].

Arguments in favor of the repeal (pros of repeal / against the act): Those who believe the absence of Glass-Steagall did not cause the crisis note that offering mortgages has always been a core business of commercial banks, so the banking system was always exposed to high residential mortgage default rates, and Glass-Steagall was never intended to regulate loan qualification standards [E5]. The act did not prevent non-depositories from extending mortgages, did not prevent investment banks from securitizing mortgages, and did not address the incentives of institutions that originated or sold mortgage-related securities, so it was unlikely to have prevented the decline in underwriting standards behind the 2000s housing boom [E6].
