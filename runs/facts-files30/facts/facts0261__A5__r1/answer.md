<answer>
According to `doc.txt`, the history of U.S. taxation can be summarized in the following stages:

**Before the federal income tax (first ~150 years).** The early tax system bore "little resemblance to the current system of taxation" [E1]. The Constitution restricted "direct" federal taxation of individuals, so the federal government relied on indirect taxes — tariffs on imports and excise taxes [E1]. Tariffs were the major source of government receipts from the founding until the early 1900s; in 1800 custom duties were about 84% of receipts, and in 1900 over 60% of internal revenue came from alcohol excise taxes and another 20% from tobacco excise taxes [E1]. The framers, wary of taxing power after the Revolution, delegated most revenue collection to localities, which relied primarily on property taxes; in peacetime the federal government met expenses with modest excise taxes and tariffs, while in wartime (e.g., the War of 1812) taxes were temporarily raised and then cut after public opposition [E2].

**Civil War and the first income tax.** The Civil War raised excise and tariff rates, and revenue collections increased sevenfold between 1863 and 1866 [E3]. The first national income tax was created during the war, with a 10% maximum rate and generous exemptions so that only about 10% of households paid it; it generated over 20% of federal revenues in 1865, but few politicians wanted to keep it, and it expired in 1872 [E3].

**Populism, the 1894 tax, and the 16th Amendment.** The Populist movement of the late 1800s revived the income tax as a progressive, ability-to-pay vehicle of social justice, aimed at monopoly profits and concentrated wealth [E4]. A federal income tax with a $4,000 exemption was enacted in 1894 but ruled unconstitutional by the Supreme Court in 1895; after defeated amendment proposals, the 16th Amendment was ratified in 1913, creating the legal basis for the federal income tax [E4]. The initial tax was progressive but mild — only about 2% of households paid it, at rates of 1%–7% [E5].

**World War I and the growth of direct taxation.** To finance WWI, the Wilson administration transformed the income tax: the top marginal rate rose from 7% in 1915 to 67% in 1917; corporate taxes became a major revenue source (over a quarter of internal collections in 1917), and the estate tax was created in 1916 [E6]. Unlike after earlier wars, much of this system survived: tariffs fell from about half of receipts in 1910 to under 15% by 1925, while corporate and individual income taxes made up nearly half of receipts in the mid-1920s, and excise collections dropped, especially during Prohibition [E7].

**Depression and New Deal.** The Great Depression sharply cut federal receipts; tax rates were raised in 1932 [E8]. Roosevelt made progressive taxation a key element of the New Deal, but the most significant measure was old-age insurance (Social Security), funded by a separate tax split equally between employers and employees, a design meant to protect the program from political attack [E8, E9].

**World War II — the most dramatic shift.** Roosevelt again sought revenue from higher taxes on corporations and high incomes [E10]. The compromise made the income tax more progressive and broader: personal exemptions were halved between 1939 and 1942, the income tax reached the middle class for the first time, the top-rate threshold fell from $5 million (1941) to $200,000 (1942), and the top marginal rate hit a record 94% in 1944; taxes began to be withheld from paychecks [E11]. Individual taxpayers grew from 3.9 million in 1939 to 42.6 million in 1945, and income tax collections leaped from $2.2 billion to $35.1 billion; by 1950 the federal income tax produced over 51% of all government tax collections, making the federal government the dominant tax collector — "the most dramatic shift in the nation's tax policies since 1916" [E12]. Much of this structure remained after the war, with both parties supporting a broad progressive income tax, relatively flat corporate rates, and regressive social insurance taxes [E13].

**Postwar to the 1980s.** Changes were generally minor: the Social Security tax rose from 2% to 6.13% by 1979; Medicare and Medicaid were established in the 1960s; and the 1964 tax cuts lowered marginal rates (top rate from 91% in 1963 to 70% in 1965) [E14].

**The "Reagan Revolution" and later reforms.** The 1980s ended the stasis: Reagan was elected in 1980 on a platform of smaller government and lower taxes, and the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) enacted the largest tax cut in American history (in constant dollars), on supply-side reasoning [E15]. ERTA phased the top rate down from 70% to 50%, cut corporate taxes, and indexed many tax parameters to inflation [E16]. In practice, ERTA caused the largest federal revenue reduction of any tax bill since WWII, and in 1982 the largest peacetime tax increase was passed, repealing some ERTA provisions and closing corporate loopholes; 1983 Social Security reforms raised rates and began taxing some benefits [E17]. The Tax Reform Act of 1986, the most comprehensive code revision since the 1950s, cut the top individual rate from 50% to 28% (by 1988) and the top corporate rate from 46% to 34% [E18]. Despite the cuts, federal revenues grew at nearly the pace of GDP (revenues up ~76% from 1980–1988 vs. GDP up 83%), with the revenue mix shifting from individual/corporate taxes toward social insurance taxes [E19]. Federal deficits tripled in the 1980s; in 1990 President Bush broke his "no new taxes" promise and raised the top bracket to 31%, and in 1993 Clinton added 36% and 39.6% brackets and raised the corporate rate to 35%, restoring progressivity [E20]. The $1.35 trillion Bush tax cut of 2001 lowered individual rates across the board, scheduled estate-tax repeal for 2010, and expanded retirement contributions, with "back-loaded" phase-ins (top rate fell from 39.6% in 2001 to 35.0% by 2006) [E21]. It reduced the progressiveness of the income tax, a smaller 2003 cut accelerated rate decreases and lowered capital-gains/dividend rates, and the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act instituted or expanded credits such as a $400 payroll tax credit per worker and an expanded college-tuition credit [E22].

**Overall trends.** Federal tax collections grew considerably over the 20th century (in inflation-adjusted terms), with the largest jump during WWII and steady growth after about 1960, punctuated by declines from recessions or major code changes [E23]. Total tax collections generally grew as a share of GDP, the federal government became the dominant tax collector (a trend that began before WWII), and federal taxes as a share of GDP have declined since 2000, reaching in 2009 the lowest level since the 1940s [E24]. In composition, federal taxation began the century dominated by excise taxes; corporate taxes were the largest source 1918–1932; personal income taxes became the largest source in 1944 and have supplied 40–50% of federal revenues since WWII; and social insurance taxes have grown from about 10% of federal revenues around 1950 to nearly 40% [E25]. State and local taxes, dominated by property and sales taxes, saw property taxes lead until the 1970s, sales taxes lead until 2008, and property taxes regain the lead in 2008 [E26].
