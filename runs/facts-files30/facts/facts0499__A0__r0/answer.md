## Benefits of VBAC compared to a C-section
- **Faster recovery and shorter hospital stay** (recovering from a C-section typically takes longer)
- **No abdominal surgery**
- **Avoiding or lowering C-section risks** like heavy bleeding and infection
- **Better lung preparation for your baby** — vaginal birth helps clear amniotic fluid from your baby's lungs as it passes through the birth canal, better preparing the baby to breathe oxygen after birth
- **Immune system boost for your baby** as it travels through the birth canal
- **Less risk of complications from repeat surgeries**, such as scarring or injuries to nearby organs
- **Personal preference** — some people simply want the experience of a vaginal birth

## Risks involved
- **Uterine rupture** — because a C-section leaves a scar on the uterus, the pressure of labor could cause the uterus to open (rupture) along the scar. Although rare, this is a serious complication that can have life-threatening results. If you're at high risk for rupture, a provider will typically not attempt a VBAC.
- **Blood loss**
- **Infection**
- **Emergency C-section**, which is riskier than a planned C-section

The document notes the risk of uterine rupture with a transverse (sideways) uterine cut is about **0.9% (slightly less than 1 in 100)**. The type of incision matters: a **low transverse incision** has the lowest rupture risk, a **low vertical incision** has a higher risk, and a **high vertical ("classical") incision** in the upper uterus has the highest risk. Note that the belly scar's location/direction doesn't necessarily match the uterine scar's — your medical records should have this information, and your provider should review them.

## Success rate
- **Up to 75%** of people who had **one** C-section went on to have a successful vaginal birth in their next pregnancy.
- The chance drops to **about 50%** if you've had **two or more** prior C-section deliveries.

## Factors that may make VBAC more likely to succeed
- Previous C-section deliveries (one or two) with **low transverse incisions**
- A **prior vaginal birth**
- **No other uterine surgeries** (such as fibroid removal/myomectomy)
- **No history of uterine rupture**
- **No medical condition** (like fibroids or problems with placenta location) that makes vaginal delivery risky
- **Going into labor on your own before your due date**
- A **prior successful VBAC** without complications (like ruptured uterus) makes future successful vaginal deliveries more likely

## Factors that may lower your chances
- Prior C-section due to **labor not progressing** (not dilating or effacing)
- **Older than 35**
- **Obesity or overweight**
- **Large fetus or multiples**
- **Past your due date**
- **Short time between pregnancies** (a baby within the last 18 months)
- **Preeclampsia** (providers may prefer a C-section)

## What to work toward / discuss with your provider
The document recommends discussing early in your pregnancy and reviewing your delivery plan at prenatal visits, including:
- Your past pregnancies and deliveries, and the reason(s) you had a C-section
- The safety of VBAC vs. a repeat C-section for you and your baby, and reasons to consider or avoid either
- A plan for **inducing labor (or not)**
- Whether the **healthcare facility can manage emergency deliveries**
- Your plan for more children

Since your doctor has said it's an option, the document suggests making sure they review your medical records (especially the location/direction of your uterine incision and the reasons for your prior C-section) as part of that decision.
