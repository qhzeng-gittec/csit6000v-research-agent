

Based on `doc.txt` (a GDAE teaching module by Brian Roach, "Taxes in the United States: History, Fairness, and Current Political Issues"), the history of U.S. taxation is presented in Section III, "A Brief History of Taxation in the U.S." (noted as "primarily derived from Brownlee (1996)"), plus Section IV, "Summary Data of U.S. Tax History."

**Before the federal income tax (first ~150 years)**
- "The tax mechanisms used during first 150 years or so of U.S. tax history bears little resemblance to the current system of taxation." The Constitution "restricted 'direct' taxation by the federal government – meaning taxes directly on individuals," so "the federal government relied on indirect taxes including taxes on imports (tariffs) and excise taxes."
- "Tariffs were the major source of U.S. government receipts from the beginning of the nation up to the early 1900's. For example, in 1800 custom duties comprised about 84% of government receipts." Internal revenue was mostly excise taxes: "In 1900 over 60% of internal revenue collections came from alcohol excise taxes with another 20% from tobacco excise taxes."
- Government was small: "In the late 1800s government expenditures were responsible for only about 2% of national output," versus about 20% of output in 2010.
- "The Constitutional framers were wary of a government's power to tax. Taxation of the American Colonies by a distant and corrupt England was a driving force behind the American Revolution." They "delegated most public revenue collection to localities, which relied primarily on property taxes." In wartime, "such as the War of 1812, federal taxes were temporarily raised to finance the war or pay down the ensuing debts," then reduced after the crisis.
- "Like previous wars, the Civil War initiated an increase in both excise tax and tariff rates. Government revenue collections increased by a factor of seven between 1863 and 1866." The key innovation: "the institution of the first national income tax," with "a maximum rate of 10% along with generous exemptions meant that only about 10% of households were subject to any income tax." It "generated over 20% of federal revenues in 1865," but "in 1872 it was allowed to expire."
- "The impetus for the modern federal income tax rests not with a wartime emergency but with the Populist movement of the late 1800s," which saw an income tax "as a vehicle of social justice." "A federal income tax, with a large exemption of $4,000, was instituted in 1894 but the Supreme Court ruled it unconstitutional in 1895." After failed amendment attempts, "in 1913 the 16th Amendment was ratified creating the legal basis for the federal income tax." Initially, "only about 2% of households paid any income tax – tax rates of only 1%-7%."

**The growth of direct taxation (WWI–WWII)**
- World War I: "the top marginal rate increased dramatically from 7% in 1915 to 67% in 1917." "Corporate taxes also became an important revenue source, accounting for over one-quarter of internal revenue collections in 1917," and "In 1916 the estate tax was created."
- Unlike after earlier wars, "much of the tax system laid down during World War I remained in place after the war. In the period from 1910 to 1925 tariffs fell from about half of government receipts to less than 15%," while "the new corporate and individual income taxes made up nearly half of government receipts in the mid 1920s." Excise collections dropped, "especially during the years of Prohibition when alcohol excise taxes virtually disappeared."
- The Great Depression "caused a significant decline in federal receipts. In 1932 tax rates were increased in an attempt to boost federal revenue." "Franklin Roosevelt, in the years leading up to World War II, presented progressive taxation as a key element of the New Deal," but "the most significant measure enacted during this period was the creation of old-age insurance" (Social Security), funded by "a separate tax, split equally between employers and employees."
- World War II: Roosevelt sought revenue "primarily from higher taxes on corporations and high-income households." "Personal exemptions were reduced by half between 1939 and 1942 – meaning the income tax reached well into the middle class for the first time," and "the top marginal tax rate reached a record high of 94% in 1944." Paycheck withholding was introduced. As a result, "the number of individual taxpayers grew from 3.9 million in 1939 to 42.6 million in 1945, and federal income tax collections over the period leaped from $2.2 billion to $35.1 billion." "In 1940, federal income tax had accounted for only 16 percent of the taxes collected by all levels of government; by 1950 the federal income tax produced more than 51 percent of all collections."
- After WWII, "much of the new tax structure instituted during World War II remained in place after the war," with both parties supporting "a progressive but broad income tax, relatively flat tax rates on corporate profits, and social insurance taxes that were basically regressive."

**From the "Reagan Revolution" to the Bush tax cuts**
- "Changes to the tax system between the end of World War II and the 1980's were generally minor." The Social Security tax "had increased to 6.13% by 1979" (from an initial 2%); "The Medicare and Medicaid programs were established in the 1960s"; and "Across-the-board tax cuts in 1964 reduced marginal rates for both low- and high-income households (the top marginal rate fell from 91% in 1963 to 70% in 1965)." Government spending grew "from less than 18% of GDP in 1946 to over 22% by the mid 1970s."
- The 1980s: "Ronald Reagan was elected president in 1980 on a platform of smaller government and lower taxes. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) enacted the largest tax cut in American history" (measured in constant dollars). "ERTA phased in a reduction in the top tax rate from 70% to 50%, enacted several corporate tax cuts, and indexed many tax parameters to inflation." In practice, "ERTA resulted in the largest reduction in federal revenues of any tax bill since World War II," and "in 1982, the largest peacetime tax increase was passed." "Social Security reforms were enacted in 1983 that increased Social Security tax rates and initiated taxation of some benefits." The "Tax Reform Act of 1986 – considered to be the most comprehensive revision of the tax code since the 1950s" "reduced top income tax rates even further – from 50% in 1986 to 28% in 1988" and "lowered the top corporate tax rate from 46% to 34%."
- Despite the cuts, "total federal revenues increased about 76% from 1980-1988 while GDP increased 83%," but the mix shifted: "The share of revenues from both individual and corporate taxation fell (by 9% and 16% respectively) while the portion from social insurance taxes increased by 38%," decreasing overall progressivity. "The annual budget deficits of the federal government tripled during the 1980s."
- 1990s: "the first President Bush reneged on his campaign promise of 'no new taxes' and agreed to a compromise tax proposal in 1990 that raised the top marginal tax bracket to 31%." "President Clinton reinstated additional progressivity in 1993 by creating the 36% and 39.6% individual tax brackets. In 1993, the corporate tax rate was increased slightly to 35%."
- 2000s: "The most recent important tax legislation was the $1.35 trillion Bush tax cut passed in 2001," including "lowering individual income tax rates across-the-board, scheduling repeal of the estate tax in 2010, and increasing the amount employees can contribute under various programs for retirement purposes." "The top marginal bracket fell from 39.6% in 2001 to 38.6% in 2002 but eventually fell to 35.0% in 2006," and the cut "reduced the overall progressiveness of the federal income tax as high-income taxpayers received a disproportionate share of the total cuts." "A somewhat smaller tax cut was passed in 2003 that, among other changes, accelerated scheduled tax rate decreases and lowered the maximum tax rate on capital gains and dividends." "Most recently, the 2009 American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 instituted or expanded various tax credits such as a payroll tax credit of $400 per worker and an expanded tax credit for college tuition."

**Summary data (Section IV)**
- "Federal tax revenues have grown considerably during the 20th century, even after adjusting for inflation. A large increase in federal tax collections occurred during World War II, with relatively consistent growth after about 1960," with "occasional declines... due either to recessions or to major tax code changes."
- "Total tax collections have generally grown as a percentage of GDP over the 20th century," with the largest leap during WWII; "federal tax revenues now substantially exceed state and local tax revenues"; and "federal taxes as a percentage of GDP were lower in 2009 than in any year since the 1940s."
- Composition: "at the beginning of the century federal taxation was dominated by excise taxes"; "Corporate taxes became the most significant source of federal revenues for the period 1918-1932"; "Personal income taxes became the largest source of federal revenues in 1944 and have remained so"; "Since World War II, income taxes have consistently supplied between 40-50% of federal revenues"; and "Since about 1950, social insurance taxes have increased their share of federal revenues from about 10% up to nearly 40%."
- State and local: "While property taxes were the primary source of state and local revenues until the 1970s, sales taxes became the major source of revenues until 2008, when property taxes again became the major revenue source."
