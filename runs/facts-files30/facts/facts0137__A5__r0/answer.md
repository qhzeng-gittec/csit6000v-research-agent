**The EI System**

EI (Employment Insurance) is an insurance-based program that was implemented in 1940 and marked its 75th year in 2015, with its last major reform occurring in 1996 [E2]. The program has two parts: Part I provides temporary income support, while Part II provides employment assistance to eligible insured participants through various programs and services [E9, E10].

The system is administered by several bodies. Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC) is the government department responsible for developing, managing and delivering programs and services [E3]. Service Canada, a federal institution within ESDC, oversees the legislative provisions of the act, determines benefit eligibility and entitlement, and issues EI benefits (Part I) directly to claimants [E4]. Provinces and territories deliver the majority of Part II Employment Benefits and National Employment Services through Labour Market Development Agreements (LMDA) funded by the Government of Canada [E5]. The Canada Employment Insurance Commission (CEIC), a departmental corporation of ESDC, administers the program, makes regulations (with Governor-in-Council approval), reviews and approves policies, sets EI premium rates, and produces the annual EI Monitoring and Assessment Report [E6].

Funding and eligibility: all workers in insurable employment must pay EI premiums, and it is the employment—not the individual—that is insured [E7]. The fundamental requirement for insurable employment is an employer-employee relationship (i.e., the person is not self-employed) [E8].

The types of benefits include:
- Regular benefits, for individuals who lose their jobs for reasons outside their control and are available for and actively seeking employment [E11].
- Special benefits, for insured workers and self-employed individuals who are too sick to work, are pregnant or have recently given birth, are caring for a newborn or newly adopted child, or must temporarily care for a family member with a serious medical condition [E12]. These comprise:
  - Maternity benefits (up to 15 weeks) for biological mothers who are pregnant or have given birth [E13].
  - Parental benefits (up to 40 weeks standard, or 69 weeks extended when shared between parents) for any legal parent caring for a newborn or newly adopted child [E14].
  - Sickness benefits (up to 26 weeks) for people who cannot work due to injury, illness, or quarantine [E15].
  - Compassionate Care benefits (up to 26 weeks, shareable among family members) for those caring for a gravely ill family member with a significant risk of death within 26 weeks [E16].
  - Family Caregiver benefits (up to 35 weeks for a child; up to 15 weeks for an adult, shareable) for those caring for a critically ill or injured family member [E17].
  - In certain circumstances, special benefits may be combined up to a maximum of 102 weeks within a maximum 104-week benefit period [E18].
- Fishing benefits, for eligible self-employed fishers who do not meet the eligibility requirements for regular benefits in their region [E19].
- Job creation partnerships, work sharing, and training benefits, for clients participating in developmental programs [E20].

**The Benefits (What EI Provides to Individuals)**

The core benefit of EI is protection for individuals who have lost their job for reasons outside their control, while they look for new employment or upgrade their skills [E1]. The program may also help unemployed individuals across the country find employment [E1].

Beyond job loss, EI provides temporary financial assistance to workers who are sick, pregnant, caring for a newborn or newly adopted child, or caring for a family member with a serious medical condition [E1, E9].

The 1996 reform was intended to better reflect the program's primary objective of promoting employment, and to emphasize that access to the program is linked to significant work attachment [E2].
