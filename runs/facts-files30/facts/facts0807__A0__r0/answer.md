

When a sequence of faces is quickly flashed (4–5 per second) in peripheral vision, they begin to look deformed, cartoonish, and even grotesque, though they are actually normal. Peripheral vision plays a central role in this "flashed face distortion effect."

Bowden et al. (2019) tested whether the effect stems from the intrinsic blur of peripheral vision, which arises because peripheral stimuli are processed by retinal rod cells with poor spatial acuity. They compared blurred central faces with unblurred peripheral faces and found that while blur produced some distortion in central vision, the effect was much larger with unblurred peripheral faces—so blur alone cannot explain the illusion.

Balas and Pearson (2019) confirmed that peripheral presentation makes a big difference, as does presentation speed. Notably, face orientation did not matter: upside-down faces produced similar distortions as upright ones.

Afraz and Cavanagh (2009) showed that peripheral distortion effects are idiosyncratic—the same face can appear male in one corner of peripheral vision and female in another. These differences are stable within an observer but vary between observers. Thus, the particularities of each person's peripheral visual distortions may be responsible for the specific ways flashed faces appear distorted.
