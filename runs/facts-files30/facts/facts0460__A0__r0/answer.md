

Based on `doc.txt`, changing marijuana from Schedule I to Schedule III would have the following effects:

**1. It would open the door for the medical marijuana industry to market products consistent with federal law.**
- Aside from Schedule I substances, controlled substances have accepted medical uses and may be lawfully dispensed by prescription under the Controlled Substances Act (CSA), subject to various restrictions. Rescheduling marijuana to Schedule III would therefore allow the medical marijuana industry to market its products consistent with federal law.

**2. It would NOT automatically legalize medical marijuana in the United States.**
- Any such products must still comply with other federal legal requirements, such as the FD&C Act, before they could be legally marketed.
- The FD&C Act requires new drugs — including small-molecule drugs synthesized in a laboratory and biologics derived from living organisms — to be approved by the FDA before they can be marketed and sold. A sponsor must submit a new drug application (NDA) with "substantial evidence" that the drug is safe and effective for its proposed use, or a biologics license application (BLA) demonstrating the product is safe, potent, and pure.
- Certain chemicals related to or derived from marijuana may be considered small-molecule drugs, while other components of the cannabis plant — including marijuana itself — may be considered a biologic.
- Marijuana itself is not an approved drug. (FDA has approved four cannabis-derived/related drugs — Epidiolex, Marinol, Syndros, and Cesamet — but these could not be marketed until the DOJ rescheduled their active ingredients away from Schedule I.)

**3. It would generally loosen the controls on medical research involving marijuana — but with an important caveat.**
- Researchers and manufacturers of Schedule III substances are subject to less-onerous registration requirements than those for Schedule I substances, so rescheduling would generally loosen the controls on medical research involving marijuana.
- However, medical researchers and drug sponsors of marijuana- or CBD-containing drugs would **not** benefit from these looser restrictions without congressional action. The 2022 Medical Marijuana and Cannabidiol Research Expansion Act (P.L. 117-215) created separate requirements for marijuana researchers and manufacturers to expedite registration with the DEA, and these separate requirements would not be affected by rescheduling without additional congressional action.

**4. What would NOT change:**
- Other tetrahydrocannabinols remain Schedule I substances; because they are scheduled separately from marijuana, they are not included in the DOJ's proposed rule rescheduling marijuana.
- Products marketed without FDA approval still cannot legally be marketed in interstate commerce under the FD&C Act. FDA categorizes products based on how they are marketed, so products marketed as foods, dietary supplements, tobacco products, or cosmetics could still be deemed illegal drugs if their labeling makes claims that they are intended to cure, mitigate, or treat a disease or health-related condition (as FDA has done in warning letters to companies marketing cannabis products such as CBD tea and hemp-infused body butter). Such a manufacturer or seller could remedy the issue by submitting an NDA and obtaining FDA approval, or by removing the claims.

In short: rescheduling to Schedule III would permit prescription dispensing and open the door for the medical marijuana industry to market products, and would generally loosen research controls — but it would not by itself legalize medical marijuana (FDA approval under the FD&C Act would still be required), and the looser research registration requirements would not apply to marijuana/CBD researchers without additional congressional action.
