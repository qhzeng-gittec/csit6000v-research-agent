**Point 6 — Right to information**
- Every child and young person has a right to information about their health and healthcare in a form they can understand, including information about the choice of health care services available.
- Emphasis is on *receiving* information: special attention and creativity are needed so children can seek, receive and impart information and ideas, orally or through means of their choice (e.g., play and art).
- The language and format must suit the child's abilities and level of understanding, and it is essential to ensure the child has understood the information and had every opportunity to participate in conversations about their health and care.
- Includes access to information in Te Reo Māori and, for those from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, in their own language.
- Health professionals must talk directly to children (even if they seem unable to comprehend), be as open as possible, tell them what will happen before a procedure, give honest information about their condition and treatment outcomes, and help them select and practice coping strategies.
- Timely, accurate information and advance preparation for hospitalisation, procedures or surgery help children retain a sense of control and mastery, especially in hospital.

**Point 7 — Right to participate in decision-making**
- Every child and young person has a right to participate in decision-making and, as appropriate to their capabilities, to *make* decisions about their care, to the greatest extent possible in line with their capacities for understanding.
- This includes decisions about the use, return or disposal of bodily parts or substances removed, changed or added during health care.
- Children should be offered healthcare choices wherever possible and are always entitled to a second opinion; their questions and ideas should be heard, and if their views cannot be acted on, they are entitled to an explanation.
- Health professionals must be available, trained and committed to communicating with children; effective communication is critical so children and families can give informed consent.
- Participation includes verbal and nonverbal communication (play, art, other media), with attention to nonverbal cues from infants, very young children and those with disabilities.
- The right extends beyond the individual: children and young people as groups should be involved in consultation on the development, implementation and evaluation of services, policies and strategies affecting them.
- It also covers teaching and research: informed consent must be sought beforehand, participants may withdraw at any time without giving a reason, non-participation must not alter access to treatment, and research requires ethical oversight by a Human Research Ethics Committee.

**Key differences**
- **Scope of the right:** Point 6 is about the child's right to *receive* understandable, honest information about their health and care; Point 7 is about the child's right to *participate in and make* decisions about their care.
- **Focus of Point 6:** how information is delivered — understandable form, appropriate language/format, direct and open communication, honesty about procedures and outcomes, coping strategies, and advance preparation to give a sense of control.
- **Focus of Point 7:** how decisions are made — involvement in decisions (including about bodily parts/substances), being offered choices, second opinions, having views heard and explained, requirements on health professionals, informed consent, group consultation on services and policies, and protections around teaching/research (consent, withdrawal, no impact on treatment, ethics oversight).
- **Overlap:** both points stress communication through play, art and other media of the child's choice, and both require communication appropriate to the child's abilities — but Point 6 frames this as a means of conveying information, while Point 7 frames it as a means of enabling participation in decision-making.
