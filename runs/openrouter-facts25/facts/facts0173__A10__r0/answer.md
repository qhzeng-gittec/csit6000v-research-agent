

Periorbital hyperpigmentation, also called peri-palpebral hyperpigmentation, dark eyelids, dark eye circles, dark circles, or simply under-eye circles, is described as "a mere color difference between the palpebral skin and the remaining facial skin," yet it "makes people look tired or older, which negatively affects their quality of life." The condition has a higher prevalence in individuals with darker skin, hair and eyes, and affects age groups and genders equally. Nevertheless, there are a higher number of complaints from women, especially senior women. Dark eye circles with a vascular component are known to present a dominant autosomal family inheritance pattern.

The etiology of periorbital hyperpigmentation is multifactorial, involving intrinsic factors determined by the individual's genetics and extrinsic factors such as sun exposure, smoking, alcoholism, and sleep deprivation. The presence of melanic pigment and hemosiderotic pigment in the affected sites is a distinctive feature in its etiopathogeny. Melanic hyperpigmentation is more frequent in brunet adults, as a consequence of excessive and cumulative exposure to the sun, which increases the production of melanin, reduces the skin's thickness, and increases the dilatation of blood vessels.

Intense vascularization is mainly found in people belonging to certain ethnic groups such as Arabs, Turks, Hindus, inhabitants of the Iberian Peninsula and their respective descendants. In these ethnicities, the manifestation tends to take place earlier, often during childhood. In those individuals there is no change in the color of the skin; the eyelid appears darkened because the dilated vessels are visible due to the transparency of the skin. The problem is often aggravated when the lower eyelid's vessels are more dilated (for example from fatigue, insomnia, oral breathing, crying), causing dermal blood extravasation. The liberation of ferric ions takes place locally, entailing the formation of free radicals that stimulate the melanocytes, which generates melanic pigmentation.

Other causes noted as being responsible for the appearance of dark eye circles include post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation secondary to atopic and contact dermatitis, sleep deprivation, oral breathing, alcoholism, smoking, use of certain medications (contraceptives, chemotherapy, antipsychotic and some types of eye drops), the presence of palpebral sagging due to aging, and disorders that develop with hydric retention and palpebral edema (thyroid disorders, nephropathies, cardiopathies and pneumopathies).

Regarding anatomy, the palpebral skin is the thinnest in the human body, measuring less than 1 mm. Its epidermis is very thin (0.4 mm) compared to that of the palmoplantar region (approximately 1.6 mm). The palpebral dermis is composed of loose conjunctive tissue and is extremely thin in that region. It is absent in the pre-tarsal skin, in the medial and lateral ligaments of the eyelid, where the skin adheres to the underlying fibrous tissue. The thinness of the skin, combined with the lack of fatty tissue, gives that region its characteristic translucency. As a result, the accumulation of melanin and/or vessel dilatation in that region can be easily seen, through transparency, as bilateral homogeneous hyperpigmentation.

The palpebral skin's color results from the combination of several factors, some of genetic-racial origin (such as the amount of melanin pigment), others of individual or regional and even gender origins, such as the thickness of the several components and the blood volume in their vessels.

Various treatments have been proposed for periorbital hyperchromia, however there are few studies on their long-term efficacy. The main types of treatment are: topical application of depigmenting products, chemical peelings, dermabrasion, cryosurgery, fillings with hyaluronic acid, intense pulsed light, CO2, argon, ruby and excimer lasers.

**Individuals with darker skin, hair and eyes are most affected by periorbital hyperchromia.**
